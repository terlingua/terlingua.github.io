## Learning objectives

- Define a group and explain why people join groups
- Define social norms and social roles, and explain the dangers of strong roles (Stanford prison study)
- Define group cohesiveness and explain its benefits and risks
- Explain the effects of group diversity

## Key ideas

A **group** is two or more people who interact and are interdependent (p. 234). People join groups for belonging, information, identity and survival (p. 235); groups are typically 3–6 people.

**Social norms** are a group's rules for acceptable behavior, values and beliefs; **social roles** are expectations about how particular members behave (p. 236). Roles can overwhelm personal identity, as in Zimbardo's Stanford prison study, whose methods and interpretation have since been heavily criticized, and in real abuses such as Abu Ghraib.

**Group cohesiveness** binds members (p. 237): it improves performance when the task requires close cooperation, but can hurt decisions when maintaining harmony overrides accuracy.

**Diversity** (p. 237): diverse groups can initially have more friction but often make better decisions, consider more information, and are more innovative, especially over time.
