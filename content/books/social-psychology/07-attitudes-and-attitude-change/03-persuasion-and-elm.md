## Learning objectives

- Explain how changing behavior changes attitudes (dissonance revisited)
- Describe the Yale Attitude Change Approach: source, message and audience
- Explain the elaboration likelihood model and the heuristic–systematic model
- Explain what determines motivation and ability to attend to arguments
- Explain why centrally formed attitudes last longer

## Key ideas

Changing behavior can change attitudes via **cognitive dissonance** (p. 177; chapter 4).

**Persuasive communication**: a message advocating one side (p. 177). The **Yale Attitude Change Approach** studied *who* says *what* to *whom*: credible, attractive sources; two-sided versus one-sided messages; audience distraction, intelligence and age.

**Elaboration likelihood model (ELM)** (Petty & Cacioppo; p. 179): the **central route** when people have motivation and ability to think about the arguments; the **peripheral route** when they don't, so surface cues (source attractiveness, length, number of arguments) persuade. The **heuristic–systematic model** is a similar two-route model.

**Motivation** (p. 179): personal relevance (Petty, Cacioppo & Goldman, 1981: comprehensive-exam proposal for next year versus 10 years later), need for cognition. **Ability** (p. 181): distraction, time, knowledge. **Long-lasting change** (p. 182): central-route attitudes are more stable, resistant and predictive of behavior.
