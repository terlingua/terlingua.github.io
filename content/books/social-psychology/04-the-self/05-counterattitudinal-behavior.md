## Learning objectives

- Distinguish external and internal justification
- Explain the Festinger and Carlsmith $1/$20 study and the "saying is believing" paradigm
- Explain insufficient punishment and the forbidden-toy study
- Explain the Ben Franklin effect and hypocrisy induction
- Describe cultural differences in dissonance

## Key ideas

**Counterattitudinal behavior**: acting against your private beliefs (p. 102). With **external justification** (a big reward, a threat), little dissonance arises. With insufficient external justification, people use **internal justification**, changing their attitude to fit the behavior.

- Festinger & Carlsmith (1959): people paid $1 to tell the next participant a boring task was fun later rated it more enjoyable than people paid $20.
- **Insufficient punishment** (Aronson & Carlsmith, 1963): children mildly threatened not to play with a toy later devalued it more than severely threatened children; the effect lasted weeks (Freedman, 1965).
- **Ben Franklin effect**: doing someone a favor makes you like them more (Jecker & Landy, 1969); harming someone makes you derogate them.
- **Hypocrisy induction**: making people aware that they preach what they do not practice motivates behavior change (Aronson; Stone et al., 1994).

**Culture** (p. 104): dissonance appears across cultures but its triggers differ; in interdependent cultures, dissonance may be stronger when decisions affect others or others are watching.
