## Learning objectives

- Define the self-concept and distinguish it from self-esteem
- Explain self-awareness theory and why focusing on the self can be uncomfortable
- Explain how culture shapes the self-concept (independent versus interdependent views)
- Define impression management and self-handicapping, and distinguish behavioral from reported self-handicapping

## Key ideas

The **self-concept** is the overall set of beliefs people have about their personal attributes (p. 82); **self-esteem** is the evaluation of their own worth (p. 83). Children recognize themselves in mirrors by about 18–24 months, and self-descriptions shift with age from concrete attributes to inner states and relationships.

**Self-awareness theory**: focusing attention on yourself leads you to compare your behavior with internal standards and values (Duval & Wicklund, 1972). When we fall short, self-awareness is aversive, so people sometimes escape it; when we meet standards, it can help us act on our values.

**Seeking to feel good about ourselves** (p. 83): people favor self-enhancing views; cultures vary in how much they emphasize self-enhancement versus self-improvement.

**Impression management** (p. 85) is the attempt to get others to see us as we want to be seen, including ingratiation (flattery) and **self-handicapping**: creating obstacles or excuses so that failure can be blamed on them rather than on ability (Jones & Berglas, 1978).
