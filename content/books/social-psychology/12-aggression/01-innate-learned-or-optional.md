## Learning objectives

- Define aggression and distinguish hostile from instrumental aggression
- Explain the evolutionary view, the challenge hypothesis and the dual-hormone hypothesis
- Describe what animal research suggests
- Explain cultural variation, including changes across time and cultures of honor
- Describe gender differences in physical and relational aggression
- Explain social cognitive learning theory and Bandura's Bobo doll studies
- Explain the effects of alcohol, pain and heat

## Key ideas

**Aggression** is intentional behavior aimed at causing physical harm or psychological pain (p. 336). **Hostile aggression** stems from anger; **instrumental aggression** is a means to another goal.

**Evolutionary view** (p. 338): aggression may have helped males compete. The **challenge hypothesis** says testosterone relates to aggression only when there are opportunities for reproduction; the **dual-hormone hypothesis** says testosterone relates to dominance seeking only when cortisol is not elevated. **Animals** (p. 339): chimpanzees are aggressive, bonobos much less so, and cats raised with rats can learn not to kill them, so aggression is shaped by experience.

**Culture** (p. 341): violence has declined over centuries and varies dramatically across societies. **Cultures of honor** (p. 342): Southern U.S. men who were insulted showed more anger, higher cortisol and testosterone, and more aggression (Cohen et al., 1996).

**Gender** (pp. 343–344): men are more physically aggressive, especially when unprovoked; women engage as much or more in **relational aggression**, harming relationships through gossip and exclusion.

**Learning** (p. 345): **social cognitive learning theory**; Bandura's Bobo doll studies. **Alcohol** lowers inhibitions and narrows attention; **pain and heat** increase aggression (p. 347).
