## Learning objectives

- Give the book's social psychological definition of culture and its three functions
- Explain the rice-versus-wheat theory and what it shows about culture as coordination
- Explain how beliefs in group superiority, social dominance orientation and institutional signals fuel cultural division
- Describe between-group comparisons, experiments and factor analysis as methods for studying culture, and Hofstede's IBM data

## Key ideas

**Culture** is the set of meanings a group of people in a time and place adopt or develop; these meanings facilitate social coordination, clarify group boundaries and provide space for innovation (p. 51). It is a set of processes, not just a label for a group.

**Coordination**: Talhelm et al. (2014) found that regions of China with a history of rice farming, which needed villages to build and share irrigation, are more **interdependent** and holistic; wheat regions, which rely on rainfall, are more **independent** (pp. 52–53). Rice regions also adopted COVID-19 masking faster (English et al., 2022; p. 55).

**Division**: belief that some cultures are superior justified the Nazi movement and, through the 1493 papal bull *Inter Caetera* and the Doctrine of Discovery, colonization (pp. 55–56). **Social dominance orientation** is an individual's preference for inequality among groups (p. 56). Institutional endorsement eases the **cognitive dissonance** of harming others, and normative signals from powerful institutions shape what people think is acceptable (p. 57).

**Methods**: culture feels like reality, so you have to step outside it to see it (p. 57). Researchers use **between-group comparisons**, experiments and **factor analysis** of large datasets; Hofstede's breakthrough came from IBM's surveys of over 100,000 employees in more than 50 countries (pp. 58–59).
