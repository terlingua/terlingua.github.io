## Learning objectives

- Explain what cross-cultural leadership research (GLOBE) found
- Define warmth and competence and explain how race and gender shape judgments of leaders
- Explain the tension between perceived expertise and journalistic objectivity
- Explain how migration and demographic change affect cultures, including acculturation, status threat and assimilation costs

## Key ideas

Leaders can suppress or support cultural change (p. 75). The GLOBE study (House et al., 2004) surveyed 17,000 managers in 62 countries: preferences differ (Europe values autonomous leadership more than Latin America), but charismatic and team-oriented leaders are valued everywhere (p. 75).

Influence depends on being seen as **warm** (trustworthy, sociable) and **competent** (capable, assertive) (Fiske; p. 76). Race and gender shape these judgments: Dupree (2024) found women leaders used more dominant language to signal competence but faced backlash for it, especially Black and Latina women (p. 76). White Americans saw Black journalists as more expert on racial issues but less objective (Torrez et al., 2024; p. 77).

**Multiculturalism**: migrants bring their cultures; **acculturation** takes time, sometimes generations (p. 77). Looser cultures integrate and gain creativity; diversity drives innovation (Page, 2008, 2019), and second-generation immigrants are among the strongest U.S. economic contributors (National Academies, 2017; p. 78). Demographic change can trigger **status threat** (Craig & Richeson, 2014; Knowles et al., 2022). Minorities more often try to **assimilate**, at a cost: Chinese students who Anglicized their names had lower self-esteem and health (Zhao & Biernat, 2018); Black women face natural hair bias (Koval & Rosette, 2021) (p. 79).
