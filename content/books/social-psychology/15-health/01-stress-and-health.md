## Learning objectives

- Define stress and stressors; distinguish acute and chronic stress
- Explain resilience
- Explain early life-event approaches and their limits
- Explain physiological toughening and stress mindsets
- Define perceived stress and describe the common-cold study

## Key ideas

**Resilience** (p. 426): mild, transient reactions to stress followed by a quick return to normal functioning. Fewer than half of bereaved spouses show significant long-term distress (Bonanno et al., 2005; p. 427).

**Negative life events** (p. 427): Holmes and Rahe (1967) counted how much readjustment events required; the College Life Stress Inventory (Try It!, p. 429) is a modern version. Correlational designs cannot rule out third variables (p. 428).

**Acute versus chronic stress** (p. 428): acute is immediate and temporary; chronic is prolonged. **Physiological toughening** (pp. 430–431): exposure to intermittent stressors with full recovery between them builds a fast, strong, quickly recovering stress response. **Stress mindset** (p. 431): beliefs that stress is enhancing or debilitating change how stress affects people.

**Perceived stress** (p. 431): the negative feelings that arise when people feel they lack resources to meet demands. Cohen et al. (1991): the more stress, the more likely people were to catch a cold from rhinovirus drops, about 27% at lowest stress to nearly 50% at highest (p. 432).
