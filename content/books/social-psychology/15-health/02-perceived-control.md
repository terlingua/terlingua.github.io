- id: langer-rodin
  prompt: In Langer and Rodin's nursing-home study, residents in one group heard a speech emphasizing their responsibility for their lives, chose which night to see a movie, and cared for a plant. What happened?

The induced-control group “became happier and more active,” and “Eighteen months after the director’s speech, 15% of the residents in the induced-control group had died, compared to 30% in the comparison condition” (p. 434). A small, enduring increase in control had dramatic effects.

- id: schulz
  prompt: In Schulz's study, nursing-home residents who controlled when student volunteers visited initially did better. What happened after the program ended?
  explanation: The difference from Langer and Rodin's study is that Langer and Rodin's residents kept their control, while Schulz's residents lost theirs when the visits ended. “After the program ended, the people in the induced-control group did worse” (Schulz & Hanusa, 1978; p. 435). The book warns this “has sobering implications for the many college-based volunteer programs” (p. 435). Organizations that grant autonomy (for example, team-chosen tooling or schedules) and then revoke it may leave people worse off than if they had never had it.

  explanation: Langer and Rodin's enduring control improved well-being and survival; Schulz's temporary control backfired when removed. The lesson is to grant control that lasts. Promising control without delivering it creates the expectation and then the loss, the worst of both.

For engineers facing an outage they cannot fix immediately, focusing on controllable actions (communication, mitigation) reduces helplessness.

## Learning objectives

- Define perceived control and internal–external locus of control
- Describe Twenge's findings on changes in college students' locus of control
- Explain the Langer and Rodin and Schulz nursing-home studies and why their long-term results differed
- Explain the relationship between control, disease and well-being

## Key ideas

**Perceived control** (p. 433): believing we can influence our environment. **Locus of control**: believing outcomes are under our control (internal) or not (external). College students became more external from 1960 to 2002 (Twenge et al., 2004; p. 433).

**Nursing homes** (pp. 434–435): Langer and Rodin (1976): residents told they were responsible for their lives, given choices and a plant to care for, became happier and more active; 18 months later 15% had died versus 30% of the comparison group (Rodin & Langer, 1977). Schulz (1976): residents who controlled the timing of student visits did better, but when the visits ended, they declined more than the comparison group (Schulz & Hanusa, 1978): control given and then taken away is harmful.

**Disease** (p. 436): even when people cannot control a disease, a sense of control over aspects of their lives improves psychological well-being.
