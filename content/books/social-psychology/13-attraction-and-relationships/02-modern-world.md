## Learning objectives

- Explain research on the effects of mobile devices on face-to-face connection
- Explain how propinquity, similarity and familiarity play out online
- Evaluate the promise and pitfalls of online dating, including algorithms and deceptive profiles
- Evaluate claims about "hook-up culture"

## Key ideas

**Phones** (p. 384): in cafés, pairs with a device present reported less connectedness and empathy (Misra et al., 2016, correlational). Przybylski and Weinstein (2013) showed experimentally that the mere presence of a phone reduced trust, closeness and empathy, especially in meaningful conversations.

**Attraction 2.0** (pp. 384–385): propinquity matters less with fewer degrees of separation; similarity persists: people contact others of similar popularity (Taylor et al., 2011); familiarity can backfire when meeting reveals mismatches.

**Online dating** (pp. 385–387): sites offer access, communication and matching (Finkel et al., 2012), but matching algorithms lack empirical support. 81% of online daters misreported height, weight or age (Toma et al., 2008); 32% of photos were judged misleading (Hancock & Toma, 2009). Deceptive profiles use fewer first-person pronouns, more negations and fewer words (Toma & Hancock, 2012).

**"Hook-up culture"** (pp. 387–388) is more myth than reality: the share of young adults reporting two or fewer partners since 18 was essentially unchanged (49% in 1988–1996, 51% in 2004–2012).
