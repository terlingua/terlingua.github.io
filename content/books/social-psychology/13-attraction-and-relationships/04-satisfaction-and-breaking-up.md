Investments include time, shared friends and, in jobs, unvested equity, domain knowledge and seniority. Rusbult and Martz (1995) found women in abusive relationships were more committed when they had poorer economic alternatives or more invested (p. 399), a sobering reminder that staying is not the same as being satisfied.

- id: equity
  prompt: According to equity theory, which partner in an unequal relationship is uncomfortable?
  explanation: Equity theory is “The idea that people are happiest with relationships in which the rewards and costs experienced by both parties are roughly equal” (p. 399, glossary p. 500). Underbenefited partners feel resentful; overbenefited partners eventually feel guilty, because “equity is a powerful social norm,” but “being overbenefited isn’t as bad as being underbenefited” (p. 399). On teams, a member who always takes the boring on-call shifts will eventually feel underbenefited, even if they never say so.

## Learning objectives

- Explain social exchange theory: rewards, costs, comparison level and comparison level for alternatives
- Explain Rusbult's investment model
- Explain equity theory and the difference between exchange and communal relationships
- Describe the process of breaking up, Rusbult's four responses, and Gottman's findings

## Key ideas

**Social exchange theory** (p. 396): satisfaction depends on rewards, costs, the **comparison level** (what you expect) and the **comparison level for alternatives**. **Investment model** (Rusbult; pp. 397–399): commitment depends on satisfaction, alternatives and investments; it explains why people stay even in bad relationships.

**Equity theory** (p. 399): people are happiest when rewards and costs are roughly equal for both; underbenefited partners are more distressed than overbenefited ones. **Exchange relationships** track equity closely; **communal relationships** focus on responsiveness to needs, with equity balancing over the long run (p. 401). The "love languages" idea did not hold up to scrutiny (Impett et al., 2024; p. 401).

**Breaking up** (pp. 401–403): Duck's stages; Rusbult's four responses: two destructive (actively harming, passively neglecting) and two constructive (actively improving, passively staying loyal). Destructive behaviors hurt more than constructive ones help. Gottman: contempt, sarcasm and criticism predict breakups; calming down before discussing and listening without defensiveness help.
