## Learning objectives

- Explain the propinquity effect and the mere exposure effect, including the Westgate West study
- Explain why similarity, not complementarity, predicts attraction
- Explain reciprocal liking and misattribution of arousal
- Explain the role of physical attractiveness, cultural standards of beauty and the halo effect
- Evaluate evolutionary explanations of mate selection and alternative perspectives

## Key ideas

**Propinquity effect** (p. 371): the more we see and interact with people, the more likely they become friends. At MIT's Westgate West, 41% of next-door neighbors were close friends versus 22% two doors away (Festinger et al., 1950; p. 372). It works through the **mere exposure effect** (p. 373), unless the person is obnoxious.

**Similarity** (p. 373): of opinions, personality, interests, experiences and appearance; "it is overwhelmingly similarity and not complementarity that draws people together." **Reciprocal liking** (p. 375): we like people who like us.

**Physiological arousal** (p. 375): Dutton and Aron's (1974) shaky suspension bridge: men approached by a woman on the frightening bridge were more likely to call her, misattributing arousal to attraction.

**Physical attractiveness** (p. 377): in Walster et al.'s (1966) random blind-date dance with 752 students, attractiveness was the overriding determinant of liking, for all genders. Faces judged attractive are fairly consistent across cultures (p. 378); familiarity contributes (p. 379). The **"what is beautiful is good"** stereotype is a **halo effect** (p. 379) and can become a self-fulfilling prophecy (Snyder et al., 1977; p. 381).

**Evolution and mate selection** (pp. 381–383): evolutionary psychologists argue sexes differ in mate preferences; critics point to social roles, cultural factors and behavior-versus-attitude gaps.
