## Learning objectives

- Explain controlled thinking and research on the experience of free will
- Explain the planning fallacy and affective forecasting
- Define counterfactual thinking and explain why silver medalists can be less happy than bronze medalists
- Describe what training improves reasoning

## Key ideas

**Controlled thinking** (p. 129) is effortful and conscious. Research on free will (Wegner) suggests the feeling of consciously causing an action can arise even when we did not; still, believing in free will matters, because weakening it can increase cheating.

**Planning fallacy**: overoptimism about when projects will be done, even after past overruns. **Affective forecasting**: predicting the intensity and duration of future emotions, which people often overestimate (Try It!, p. 131).

**Counterfactual thinking** (p. 131): mentally changing the past. Easily imagined alternatives intensify emotion; Olympic bronze medalists can be happier than silver medalists (Medvec, Madey & Gilovich, 1995), because silver invites "I almost won gold." Rumination on counterfactuals can be harmful.

**Improving thinking** (p. 132): people overrate their own reasoning (overconfidence). Training in statistical and methodological reasoning, such as graduate training in psychology or medicine, improves everyday reasoning about base rates and the law of large numbers (Lehman, Lempert & Nisbett, 1988).
