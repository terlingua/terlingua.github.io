## Learning objectives

- Define obedience and describe Milgram's procedure and results
- Explain the roles of normative and informational influence in obedience
- Explain adhering to the wrong norm, self-justification and loss of responsibility
- Describe how variations changed obedience and what the Burger (2009) replication found
- Explain ethical issues raised by the studies

## Key ideas

**Obedience** is a change in behavior due to the direct influence of an authority figure (p. 292).

**Milgram** (1974; p. 293): participants, as "teachers," were told to shock a "learner" (a confederate) for errors, up to 450 volts. About 65% went to the maximum, though most protested and showed great stress. Psychiatrists had predicted only about 1 in 1,000 would.

**Why** (pp. 296–300): **normative** pressure (hard to defy the experimenter); **informational** influence in an ambiguous, novel situation where the experimenter seemed expert; **conforming to the wrong norm** as the situation escalated; **self-justification** from the gradual 15-volt increments (each step justified the last); **loss of personal responsibility** to the authority.

**Variations**: obedience dropped when the experimenter was absent or by phone, when two experimenters disagreed, when peers rebelled, and when the teacher had to touch the learner. **Then and now** (p. 301): Burger (2009) replicated to 150 volts and found similar rates; the studies remain ethically controversial.
