## Learning objectives

- Define normative social influence and describe the Asch line-judgment studies
- Explain how importance changes normative conformity
- Describe the consequences of resisting normative influence
- Explain social impact theory and the role of allies and culture
- Explain minority influence and idiosyncrasy credits

## Key ideas

**Normative social influence** (p. 273): going along with others to be liked and accepted, producing public compliance, not necessarily private acceptance.

**Asch** (1956; p. 275): on easy line-judgment tasks, 76% of participants conformed to a unanimous wrong majority at least once; overall about a third of answers conformed. Brain imaging (Berns et al., 2005) suggests conformity can change perception itself, while resisting activates areas linked to emotion.

**Importance revisited** (p. 278): when the task is easy and important, people conform less, but many still conform.

**Resisting** (p. 280): deviants are rejected (Schachter, 1951). **Social impact theory** (Latané; p. 282): conformity increases with the group's **strength** (importance), **immediacy** and **number** (up to about 4–5 people). Conformity drops sharply with one **ally** (p. 283) and is higher in **collectivist cultures** (p. 284; Bond & Smith, 1996).

**Minority influence** (p. 285): consistent minorities change majority views over time, often through private acceptance (Moscovici). **Idiosyncrasy credits** let long-time conformers deviate.
