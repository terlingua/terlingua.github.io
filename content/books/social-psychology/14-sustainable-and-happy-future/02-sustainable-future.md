## Learning objectives

- Explain how injunctive and descriptive norms reduce littering and increase towel reuse
- Explain how keeping track of consumption changes behavior
- Explain how friendly competition and hypocrisy induction promote conservation
- Explain why removing small barriers and forming implementation intentions work

## Key ideas

**Norms** (pp. 410–412): reminding people of injunctive and descriptive norms reduces littering; hotel signs saying 75% of guests reuse towels outperform generic environmental appeals (Goldstein et al., 2008). Norms shift as more people adopt a behavior, as with masks during the pandemic.

**Keeping track of consumption** (p. 413): during a 1995 drought in England, households with water meters conserved more when they felt the shortage was severe. Easy-to-read feedback helps.

**Friendly competition** (p. 414): at a Dutch factory, a unit that saw only its own feedback cut lights-left-on by 27%; a unit that also saw the other unit's performance cut it by 61% (Siero et al., 1996).

**Inducing hypocrisy** (pp. 414–415): students who endorsed water saving and then recalled wasting water took showers about a minute shorter (Dickerson et al., 1992).

**Removing small barriers** (pp. 415–417): convenience, such as curbside recycling and nearby bins, has big effects. **Implementation intentions**, specific plans about where, when and how, increase follow-through.
