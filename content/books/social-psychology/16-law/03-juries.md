## Learning objectives

- Explain the story model of jury decision making and the order-of-evidence study
- Explain why confessions are not always what they seem and how camera perspective affects judgments
- Explain how majority pressure shapes deliberations

## Key ideas

Juries are subject to the biases and pressures from earlier chapters (p. 454); judges disagreed with jury verdicts 25% of the time in one study (Kalven & Zeisel, 1966).

**Story model** (p. 455): jurors fit evidence into a coherent story and choose the verdict that best fits it. Pennington and Hastie (1988): when the prosecution used story order and the defense witness order, 78% voted to convict; reversed, 31% (p. 456).

**Confessions** (pp. 456–457): long, stressful interrogations with leading questions and false promises can produce false confessions, as in the Central Park jogger case (the Exonerated Five). Video recording helps, but focusing the camera only on the suspect makes confessions seem more voluntary (Lassiter, 2010).

**Deliberations** (pp. 457–458): the initial majority usually prevails; in one study of more than 200 juries, the final verdict matched the initial majority 97% of the time. Minority jurors are pressured to conform.
