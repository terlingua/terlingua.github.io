## Learning objectives

- Define prejudice and its three components
- Define stereotypes and explain why they are resistant to change and why positive stereotypes are still harmful
- Explain the affective component and why emotional prejudice resists argument
- Define discrimination, institutional discrimination and everyday discrimination, and explain how prejudice turns into discrimination

## Key ideas

**Prejudice** is a hostile or negative attitude toward people based solely or partly on group membership, with cognitive, affective and behavioral components (p. 197).

- **Cognitive: stereotypes** (p. 199), generalizations assigning traits to virtually all members of a group. Stereotyping is a form of schema-based efficiency (chapter 5), but stereotypes resist disconfirming evidence and can shape split-second behavior (shooter-bias studies, Correll et al., 2002). **Positive stereotypes** (p. 201), such as benevolent sexism or "model minority," still constrain and harm.
- **Affective: emotions** (p. 202). Emotional prejudice resists logical argument.
- **Behavioral: discrimination** (p. 204): unjustified negative action toward group members. **Institutional discrimination** is built into practices and laws; **everyday discrimination** includes microaggressions and unequal treatment in daily life (p. 205). Under stress, anger, or when we can justify it, prejudice more readily becomes discrimination (p. 205).
