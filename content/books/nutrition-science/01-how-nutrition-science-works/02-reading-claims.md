## Learning objectives

- Convert relative risks into absolute risks
- Use the four evidence grades applied throughout this book
- Spot the common rhetorical tricks in diet marketing

## The evidence grades used in this book

| Grade | Meaning | Typical basis |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| **Established** | We are as sure as science gets; reversal would be astonishing | Consistent RCTs, or overwhelming agreement across trials, cohorts, genetics and mechanism |
| **Strong** | Very likely true; details may change | Consistent cohort evidence with supporting trials or mechanisms |
| **Uncertain** | Mixed or weak evidence; reasonable experts disagree | Inconsistent studies, small trials, or observational data only |
| **Not supported** | Evidence contradicts the claim, or there is none despite looking | Null or harmful trials, implausible mechanisms |

These grades describe the **evidence**, not the popularity, tradition, religious status or political
alignment of a claim.

## Relative versus absolute risk

A relative risk (RR) of 1.18 means 18% more events than the comparison group. Whether that matters
depends on how common the event is.

| Baseline risk | RR 1.18 | Absolute increase |
|---------------|---------|-------------------|
| 1 in 1,000 | 1.18 in 1,000 | 0.018 percentage points |
| 5 in 100 | 5.9 in 100 | 0.9 percentage points |
| 30 in 100 | 35.4 in 100 | 5.4 percentage points |

**Number needed to treat (NNT)** is 1 ÷ absolute risk reduction. It answers "how many people must make
this change for one to benefit?"

## Hazard is not risk

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classifies **hazards** by the strength of the
evidence that something *can* cause cancer, not by *how much* cancer it causes. Group 1 contains tobacco,
alcohol, processed meat, solar radiation and plutonium. Their risks differ by orders of magnitude.

## Rhetorical tricks to recognise

| Trick | Example | Why it fails |
|-------|---------|--------------|
| Appeal to nature | "Raw is how nature intended" | Many natural things are lethal; many artificial ones save lives |
| Appeal to tradition or sanctity | "Ancient texts say this animal's urine heals" | Age and religious status are not evidence |
| Appeal to authority | "A doctor recommends it" | One person's opinion is not a body of evidence |
| Cherry-picking | Citing one positive trial out of twelve | The weight of evidence matters |
| Moving the goalposts | "The trial used the wrong dose" after every null result | An unfalsifiable claim is not scientific |
| Conspiracy | "Big Pharma hides the cure" | Real effective cheap treatments (aspirin, ORS, folic acid) are promoted, not hidden |
| Chemophobia | "You can't pronounce it, so don't eat it" | Pronounceability is not toxicity; a banana contains dozens of hard-to-pronounce compounds |

## Conflicts of interest cut every way

Kearns and colleagues (2016) documented that the Sugar Research Foundation funded a 1967 review in the
*New England Journal of Medicine* that downplayed sugar's role in heart disease. Industry funding is a
real source of bias. So is selling supplements, books, courses, raw milk or "detox" teas. Treat
everyone's financial and ideological interests the same way.
