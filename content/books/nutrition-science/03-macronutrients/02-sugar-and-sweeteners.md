## Learning objectives

- Define free and added sugars
- Separate genuine harms of sugar from exaggerations
- Assess sweeteners with appropriate uncertainty

## Definitions

- **Free sugars (WHO):** sugars added by manufacturers, cooks or consumers, plus sugars naturally present in
  honey, syrups, fruit juices and fruit-juice concentrates
- **Added sugars (US):** similar, but excludes 100% fruit juice

## Targets

| Body | Recommendation |
|------|----------------|
| WHO (2015) | Free sugars below 10% of energy; below 5% for further benefit (conditional) |
| US Dietary Guidelines 2020–2025 | Added sugars below 10% of energy |
| US Dietary Guidelines 2025–2030 | No more than 10 g added sugar per meal for adults; none for young children |
| American Heart Association | Below 25 g/day for most women, 36 g/day for most men |

A single 330 ml can of cola contains about 35 g of sugar.

## The evidence

| Claim | Grade |
|-------|-------|
| Free sugars cause dental caries | **Established** |
| Sugar-sweetened drinks promote weight gain | **Strong** |
| Sugar-sweetened drinks increase type 2 diabetes risk independent of weight | **Strong** (observational, consistent) |
| Sugar causes hyperactivity in children | **Not supported** |
| Sugar "feeds cancer", so cancer patients must avoid all sugar | **Not supported** — all cells use glucose; the body regulates blood glucose regardless of diet |
| Whole fruit is harmful because of its sugar | **Not supported** |
| Honey or "unrefined" sugars are healthier than table sugar | **Not supported** in any meaningful way |
| Approved sweeteners at normal intakes cause cancer | **Not supported**; long-term effects remain **Uncertain** |

## Sugar and cancer

Cancer cells take up glucose avidly (which is how PET scans work), but so do brain and muscle. Dietary sugar
does not selectively feed tumours; blood glucose is held in a narrow range. Excess sugar can contribute to
obesity, which is a cause of at least 13 cancers — that indirect link is real.

## Sweeteners

Approved sweeteners (aspartame, sucralose, acesulfame-K, saccharin, steviol glycosides, erythritol and
others) are assessed with an acceptable daily intake (ADI) set 100 times below the dose that caused no
effect in animals. Open questions include effects on the gut microbiome, and an observational link between
erythritol blood levels and cardiovascular events (Witkowski 2023) that needs confirmation.
