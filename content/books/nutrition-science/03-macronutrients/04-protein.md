## Learning objectives

- Know protein requirements across life stages
- Understand protein quality
- Evaluate high-protein claims

## Requirements

| Group | Suggested intake | Grade |
|-------|------------------|-------|
| Healthy adults (RDA) | 0.8 g/kg/day | **Established** as the minimum |
| Older adults | 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day (PROT-AGE, ESPEN) | **Strong** |
| Resistance-trained adults seeking muscle gain | about 1.6 g/kg/day, up to 2.2 | **Strong** |
| Endurance athletes | 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day | **Strong** |
| Pregnancy | about 1.1 g/kg/day or +25 g/day | **Established** |
| Chronic kidney disease (not on dialysis) | often restricted; individualised | Clinical guidance |

The US Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range is **10–35%** of energy.

## Protein quality

Protein quality depends on essential amino acid content and digestibility, scored by PDCAAS or the newer
DIAAS. Animal proteins and soy score highest. Plant-based diets easily meet needs when they include legumes
and enough energy.

## Claims

| Claim | Grade |
|-------|-------|
| Higher protein aids satiety and preserves lean mass during weight loss | **Strong** |
| Protein beyond about 1.6 g/kg/day adds muscle for most people | **Not supported** |
| High protein damages healthy kidneys | **Not supported** |
| High protein causes osteoporosis by leaching calcium | **Not supported** — higher protein is neutral or slightly positive for bones |
| Plant protein is "incomplete" and inadequate | **Not supported** for varied diets |
| Protein source affects disease risk | **Strong** (observational, consistent) |
| You can only absorb 20–30 g of protein per meal | **Not supported** — absorption is near-complete; a 2023 study found a 100 g dose produced a larger, longer anabolic response |
