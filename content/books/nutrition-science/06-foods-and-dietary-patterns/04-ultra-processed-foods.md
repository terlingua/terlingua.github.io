## Learning objectives

- Define ultra-processed food
- Assess the evidence and its limits
- Separate processing from nutrition

## The NOVA groups

| Group | Description | Examples |
|-------|-------------|----------|
| 1 | Unprocessed or minimally processed | Fresh fruit, eggs, milk, frozen vegetables, plain nuts |
| 2 | Processed culinary ingredients | Oils, butter, sugar, salt |
| 3 | Processed foods | Canned vegetables, cheese, freshly made bread, cured meats |
| 4 | Ultra-processed foods | Soft drinks, packaged snacks, sweetened cereals, reconstituted meat products, instant noodles |

In the US and UK, ultra-processed foods provide over half of energy intake.

## What is known

| Claim | Grade |
|-------|-------|
| High UPF intake is associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and mortality | **Strong** (association) |
| Ultra-processed diets can cause overeating | **Strong** but rests mainly on one small ward trial |
| The harms are fully explained by sugar, fat, salt and low fibre | **Uncertain** |
| Specific additives (some emulsifiers, for example) harm gut health in humans | **Uncertain** — animal and early human data |
| All processing is harmful | **Not supported** |

## Practical meaning

Diets built around minimally processed foods tend to be higher in fibre and lower in energy density, sugar and
salt. That remains good advice whatever the final explanation turns out to be.
