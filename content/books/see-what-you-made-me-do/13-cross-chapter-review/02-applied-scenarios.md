- id: sc-friend
  prompt: A colleague’s partner checks her phone, dislikes her friends and makes jokes at her expense; she says he has never hit her. What does the book suggest?
  options:
  - text: It cannot be abuse without hitting
  - text: She should be told to leave immediately or lose your friendship
  - text: These can be elements of coercive control even without physical violence; the useful questions are whether he uses such behaviour to gain power and whether she is afraid or her world is shrinking — and the best help is non-judgemental, continued support
    correct: true
  - text: Confront him publicly
  explanation: 'Notes on method: some of the worst abuse involves little violence (Khan). The test in Chapter 1: conflict becomes abuse when one partner uses violent, threatening or coercive behaviour to gain power; fear may build slowly. Chapter 2: “judgement-free, unqualified support” and connection are the best protection; ultimatums complete his isolation of her.'
- id: sc-strangle
  prompt: Police attend a couple; she has scratched him, he has no visible injuries, she seems confused and aggressive. Which concepts should make them cautious about calling her the aggressor?
  options:
  - text: Confusion indicates intoxication
  - text: Mandatory arrest requires arresting her
  - text: Strangulation often leaves no marks while victims scratch and bite to breathe; concussion can mimic intoxication and aggression; and they are seeing one scene, not the whole movie
    correct: true
  - text: Scratches always identify the aggressor
  explanation: Chapter 1 on strangulation; Chapter 7 on WLS biting data, concussion mistaken for drunkenness, mandatory arrest’s effect on women, and O’Malley’s “one scene in a movie”. Strangulation is a major homicide red flag (sevenfold risk).
- id: sc-typology
  prompt: A report says a father with one partner’s complaint is a “family-only” type, so contact is safe. What should a reader of Chapter 3 ask?
  options:
  - text: Only his income
  - text: Only the mother’s mental health
  - text: Nothing — the label settles it
  - text: Whether he has abused other partners and whether there is coercive control, because types are dimensions rather than clear categories and one relationship can mislead (Glen)
    correct: true
  explanation: 'Holtzworth-Munroe: “They’re more dimensions than clear types” and judges shouldn’t classify from the bench; Wangmann warns misclassification can endanger children; Glen looked “family-only” with Michelle but was dangerous with earlier partners.'
- id: sc-child-refuses
  prompt: A ten-year-old refuses to see her father, criticises him unprompted and shows no guilt. A report-writer suggests alienation. What does the book warn?
  options:
  - text: The signs prove the mother coached her
  - text: She should be moved to her father to “de-programme” her
  - text: Children never reject parents without manipulation
  - text: Those signs on alienation checklists also fit a child rejecting an abusive parent; abuse must be properly assessed first, ideally by trained forensic interviewers, not in a joint interview with the accused
    correct: true
  explanation: 'Chapter 9: Cafcass checklist items fit abused children; Gardner’s “cure” is discredited yet persists; Canadian data show children rarely fabricate (2%); Quadrio on forensic interviewing; Meier’s 73%.'
- id: sc-male-caller
  prompt: A man calls a helpline saying his wife is abusive and he wants her punished. What distinctions from Chapter 7 help assess this?
  options:
  - text: Ask only whether she ever hit him
  - text: All male callers are victims
  - text: Whether there is fear and changed behaviour for safety, whether he takes any responsibility, and whether he wants the violence to stop or her punished — while remembering real male victims exist and need help
    correct: true
  - text: Men are never victims
  explanation: 'Tomlinson (Ch. 4): a victim wants it to stop; a perpetrator wants her punished. Walters: ask about fear and safety changes; most male callers’ victim framing does not survive the call, but some men, especially with disabilities, are exploited. Mudd: “Where’s your responsibility in this?”'
- id: sc-app-design
  prompt: You are designing a family location-sharing feature. Which ideas from the book should shape it? Select all that apply.
  type: multiple
  options:
  - text: Abusers use surveillance to demonstrate omnipotence and locate women who have fled
    correct: true
  - text: Location data cannot be misused within families
  - text: Visible indicators and easy audits of who can see your location can help victims
    correct: true
  - text: Victims may be unable to refuse sharing without punishment
    correct: true
  - text: Tracking often begins early, as an early foray into control
    correct: true
  explanation: 'Chapter 1: omnipotence through surveillance and stalkerware; Chapter 8: refuge workers find most women tracked, trackers cost little, and surveillance “starts before the perpetrator becomes overtly abusive”. Safety-by-design means assuming a member of the “family” may be the threat.'
- id: sc-policy
  prompt: A minister proposes a ten-year awareness campaign as the main response to domestic abuse. What would Hill add?
  options:
  - text: Campaigns alone will end abuse
  - text: Cut refuges to fund the campaign
  - text: Attitude change is essential but too slow alone; fund refuges and housing, target perpetrators now (e.g., focused deterrence), criminalise coercive control with training, and set a measurable target such as halving domestic homicide
    correct: true
  - text: Do nothing until attitudes change
  explanation: 'Chapter 10 critiques the attitudes-only plan as “capitulation”, cites RBT and smoking as rapid behaviour change, and High Point and Bourke as models. Chapter 8: refuge cuts are a false economy (£66bn cost vs £393m to fix refuges); Scotland shows law plus training.'
- id: sc-change
  prompt: A man in a behaviour change programme says, “I’ll change if she quits her job and stays home so we can rebuild.” How does Rob Sanasi’s story help interpret this?
  options:
  - text: It is a reasonable compromise
  - text: It proves therapy works quickly
  - text: It resembles Rob’s early manipulative use of therapy; real change meant relinquishing control and then entitlement — putting her needs first even when it felt unfair
    correct: true
  - text: It shows genuine progress
  explanation: Rob first said all the right things, then slipped in “if you would just quit that job and come home” — “Sneaky, sneaky stuff” (Ch. 5). Deb saw control end on a specific day; then Rob spent two years letting her needs come first. Women are often persuaded to stay by men “willing to change” who have no intention of doing so.

## Learning objectives

- Use the book's concepts to reason about realistic situations

## The ideas

These scenarios ask you to apply concepts rather than recall details. Each explanation points back to where the idea is developed. They are for understanding, not for assessing real situations; anyone worried about their own or someone else's safety should contact a specialist service (see the first section).
