## Learning objectives

- Connect the book's central ideas across chapters
- Apply them to new situations

## The ideas

1. **Pattern, not incident.** Domestic abuse is a pattern of power and control (Biderman, Stark). Incident-based thinking misleads surveys (CTS), police (Kelly Thompson), courts (Olivia and John) and families (Glen's one relationship). Course-of-conduct laws (Scotland; England and Wales) and *Re H-N* try to fix this.
2. **Fear and entitlement.** Coercive control is held together by fear; its engine is a sense of entitlement to control, formed by patriarchy (Kimmel, Pence). Women's violence is mostly situational, expressive or resistant; coercive control is overwhelmingly male.
3. **Look powerful, feel powerless.** Many abusers (not Cobras) act from shame and fear of abandonment — humiliated fury — and entitlement turns that pain outward (Lewis, Gilligan, Websdale, Kimmel). Explanation is never excuse.
4. **Victims resist.** Masochism, learned helplessness and Stockholm syndrome were invented, not discovered. Victims strategise; obstacles are largely social and institutional; staying can be strategic.
5. **Leaving is not safety.** Separation is the most dangerous time; abuse continues through stalking, technology, money and courts.
6. **Children are victims.** Their brains adapt to threat; their voices are discounted; family courts' pro-contact culture and "alienation" claims can hand them to abusers.
7. **Systems decide outcomes.** Refuges, housing, police culture, report-writers and secrecy shape whether women can escape. Shift the burden from victim to state.
8. **It can be reduced now.** Focused deterrence (High Point), community-led justice reinvestment (Bourke), women's police stations, credible community sanctions (Yungngora, No More), alongside long-term attitude change.
