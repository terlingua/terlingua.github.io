## Learning objectives

- Explain why Jasmine went back, and Kirkwood's "web of abuse"
- Explain why domestic abuse needs intimacy, and what "love-bombing" is
- Explain why victims may not recognise they are being abused
- List Ferraro and Johnson's six rationalisations and Herman's "doublethink"
- Explain what helps, and why leaving does not guarantee safety (Sarah's story)

## The ideas

**Why Jasmine went back** (pp. 55–56): fear of retaliation against her or Ruby; incessant calls from Nelson's friends saying he was suicidal; hope he'd finally be the man "he'd always promised he could be"; his insistence Ruby needed a father, which touched her own fatherless childhood; and complex trauma after a decade with no adult life apart from him. She describes "a cobweb": dangling outside, "but your safety is in the cobweb". Catherine Kirkwood's "web of abuse": components "interwoven; no strand could be considered in isolation" (p. 56).

**Intimacy.** "Unless the woman is there by force or arrangement, domestic abuse needs intimacy in order to thrive" (pp. 56–57). Intimacy gives trust, insight into her vulnerabilities, and her belief that the "true him" is the man she fell for. Abusers often rush this stage — **love-bombing**. Leslie Morgan Steiner's partner adored her and confided his childhood abuse; she later realised "the first stage of any domestic violence relationship is to seduce and charm the victim" (p. 57). Many traumatised men do not set out to control; some are terrified of abandonment and are "triggered" by closeness. By the time abuse starts, "their captor is not just part of their family, but part of them" (p. 58).

**Not knowing.** Victims often do not see it as abuse. Deb Sanasi googled "emotional abuse" and "saw my whole life in a list on a computer" (p. 58). Steiner, despite guns to her head, "never once thought of myself as a battered wife". Unlike hostages, for whom captivity interrupts life, "the relationship in which they are captive is their life" (p. 59).

**Six rationalisations** (Ferraro & Johnson, 100+ US women): (1) "I can fix him"; (2) "It's not really him" (drink, drugs, unemployment); (3) "It's easier to try to forget"; (4) "It's partly my fault"; (5) "There's nowhere to go"; (6) "Until death do us part" — duty to God or tradition; some religious leaders still pressure women to stay (pp. 59–60). Escape via drugs, alcohol, eating disorders or self-harm can make victims look untrustworthy to courts later.

**Doublethink.** Herman: captives become "adept practitioners of the art of altered consciousness" — dissociation, thought suppression, minimisation, denial — holding two contradictory beliefs (p. 60). It is a survival skill: she must see the world through his eyes to stay one step ahead. It is fragile; escalation or an outsider witnessing can break it. "The best thing friends and family can do is give judgement-free, unqualified support"; "Connections with family and friends are the best protection a victim has" (pp. 60–61).

**Leaving is not safety.** "Whether a woman stays or leaves is irrelevant to a man determined to punish and control her" (p. 61). Sarah, a trauma doctor, told everyone; professionals saw "small improvements" while he escalated, raping her and shaking their baby. He received a diversion programme, then a fine. After leaving, his abuse became unpredictable — five moves in a year, five concurrent court cases, near-poverty. She considered returning because a court might give him unsupervised access, and "He would need to get through me to get to her" (p. 65).
