## Learning objectives

- Explain the "underground" metaphor and why domestic abuse is not only "behind closed doors"
- Describe what the Camden court scene reveals about the dilemmas courts face
- Explain why the question "why doesn't she just leave?" is a culturally constructed piece of "common sense"

## The ideas

**The underground.** Hill uses "the underground" for the hidden world of women living under domination: "They walk on isolated paths … unseen and alone. They are us" (p. 40). The Office for National Statistics data imply almost 5 million British women have lived there at some point. Hill admits she "used to think I didn't know anyone who'd been through domestic abuse. Now I know that was never true."

**Not only behind closed doors.** Louisa's partner went through her trolley at the supermarket in front of strangers, removing things she was "not allowed", like baby wipes; she asked, "it's not normal, is it? Is it normal?" (p. 40). At an Australian rules football match a man broke his wife's eye socket and jaw because she "disrespected" him in front of his mates, and "Not one person intervened." People do intervene — an off-duty officer, Stephanie Bochorsky, saved children set alight by their father in a drug-induced psychosis (p. 41). Hill tells these to give the "visceral sense" #MeToo gave of harassment, not to shock.

**The court.** At Camden's fortnightly domestic violence list, "Everybody looks so normal" (p. 42). Mr Pearson attempted to force sex on his sleeping wife, slapped her, held a kitchen knife to her and threatened to kill her; to police he said, "I'm a man, you know" (p. 44). His wife's letter called him "a loving, respectful and hard-working man" and said the family needed his income. Hill asks what anyone can know from that letter: did he really switch from treating her "like a queen"? Was it written under pressure? What are her real options? The magistrate imposed imprisonment served as an intensive correction order (community-based). The scene shows how economic dependence, children and fear shape what victims say and what courts can do.

**"Common sense".** "Why don't they just leave?" is the knee-jerk response. But we all have forgiven a lover against our better judgement; victims' perspectives are "scrambled by the forces of degradation and coercion" (p. 45). Even survivors judge others: Kay Schubach, listening to another victim of her own abuser, thought "this stupid girl" — until "this penny drops. It was exactly my story, word for word" (p. 46). Hill: "Common sense is constructed for us, brick by brick … Every cliché we have about victims—from women's masochism to learned helplessness—was invented by somebody first" (p. 46). The next section traces those inventions.
