## Learning objectives

- Recount how "Stockholm syndrome" was coined and why Hill calls it a myth
- Describe the "true victim" stereotype and its effects in courts
- Explain "survivor theory" and the evidence that victims resist and seek help
- Explain strategic staying

## The ideas

**Stockholm, 1973.** Jan-Erik Olsson took four bank clerks hostage in Norrmalmstorg for six days. Police, untrained in negotiation, bungled the response; psychiatrist Nils Bejerot led it. Hostage Kristin Enmark, 23, built a relationship with Olsson's accomplice Clark Olofsson; Olsson later said he "could 'easily' have killed the hostages" early on and that they survived partly because captors and hostages got to know each other (p. 52). Enmark was refused a conversation with Bejerot, told Prime Minister Olof Palme she trusted the robbers and feared a police attack, and was told she would have to content herself with dying "at your post". She replied: "I don't want to be a dead hero." Afterwards she criticised police and Bejerot. Without ever speaking to her, Bejerot attributed her fear of police to an emotional or sexual attachment — "Norrmalmstorg syndrome", later Stockholm syndrome (pp. 52–53). The media, finding her too "fresh and alert", accepted it. Enmark: "Is it strange that one is afraid of those who are all around … ready to shoot?"

**A myth.** A 2008 literature review found most diagnoses were made by the media, not clinicians; research was poor and could not agree on what the syndrome was (p. 53). It has no diagnostic criteria. Allan Wade calls it "a myth invented to discredit women victims of violence" by a psychiatrist with a conflict of interest. Yet Jennifer Wild of Oxford is quoted calling domestic violence "a classic example" (p. 51).

**The "true victim".** In the 1980s–90s Stockholm syndrome, battered woman syndrome and learned helplessness dominated. The resulting stereotype: "a middle-aged, working-class white woman, a good mother and a devoted wife who has done everything in her power to appease her abuser" (p. 53). Women who are "difficult", addicted, violent in self-defence or visibly traumatised may be judged as guilty as the abuser; strong, independent women may also be disbelieved. Courts want "the standard melodrama of a virtuous female protagonist and a one-dimensional male villain".

**Survivor theory.** A study of 6,000 women at fifty Texas shelters found them "precisely the opposite of 'helpless'": most had been "extremely assertive" in trying to stop the abuse; later studies found sophisticated coping and frequent help-seeking (p. 54). The obstacles were social, not psychological — police and welfare services failed them. Linda Gordon's study of Boston agency records (1880–1960) shows resourceful women resisting and sometimes overpowering abusers. Resistance includes arguing back; survivors calculate constantly: "He has taught me strategy. It was about survival." POWs also resisted subtly, and some "defections" were strategic (pp. 54–55).

**Strategic staying.** Terri stayed until her children were old enough to be heard by a court, fearing that leaving meant unsupervised weekends with him (p. 55). Nicole Lee: portraying victims as cowering makes real victims think "But me? I should have shut my fucking mouth."
