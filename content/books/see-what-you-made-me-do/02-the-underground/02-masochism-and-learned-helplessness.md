## Learning objectives

- Trace how expert opinion explained victims' staying, from pity to masochism to helplessness
- Explain the role of Freud, Deutsch and *The Wifebeater's Wife*
- Explain Paula Caplan's rebuttal
- Explain Lenore Walker's battered woman syndrome and learned helplessness, and the critiques

## The ideas

Hill first tells Jasmine's story in full: after Nelson's savage assault, threats against their baby and rape (p. 47), she reported it but did not proceed, returned within two months and exchanged loving messages; she finally escaped after he abducted Ruby. She then asks what the story evokes and why Jasmine stayed (p. 48).

**From pity to masochism.** In the late nineteenth century, victims were pitied as wives of brutal alcoholics (though courts often scolded them for "provoking"). When, in the 1930s, wives complained and sought divorce, they became "a threat to the sacred family unit" (p. 48). Cue the theory that they liked it. Freud held women to be innately masochistic; Helene Deutsch (*The Psychology of Women*, 1944) listed masochism with passivity and narcissism as essential feminine traits. In the 1940s–50s social workers believed battered women sought abusers. *The Wifebeater's Wife* (1964) interviewed thirty-seven abusers — not the wives — and concluded the wives secretly wanted the violence, which "probably" helped them with guilt over their own "controlling, castrating behaviour" (p. 49).

**Caplan.** In *The Myth of Women's Masochism* (1985) Paula Caplan argued women are acculturated into self-denial, which is then labelled masochism: "Once females have been trained [to be nurturant, selfless and endlessly patient], this behavior is then labeled masochistic" (p. 49). Some women are bonded "not to the abuse … but to the occasional affection". By the 1980s, research asking victims found "women rarely provoked assaults and could do little to prevent them" (p. 50).

**The ghost remains.** Two-thirds of Americans and 51% of Australians believe most women could leave if they really wanted to (p. 50).

**Learned helplessness.** Lenore Walker's *The Battered Woman* (1979) made the model victim a "helpless child". She drew on Martin Seligman's dogs, which, shocked unpredictably, stopped trying to escape. Walker's cycle — tension building, acute violence, honeymoon — supposedly wore down the woman's motivation until she was "blind to the opportunities she had to leave" (p. 50). It restored sympathy but created a new stereotype and still blamed the victim: "The batterer, spurred on by her apparent passive acceptance of his abusive behaviour", does not try to control himself (pp. 50–51).

**Critique.** Degraded victims can lose the ability to complete basic tasks and may believe they cannot survive without him, but "they are not trapped simply because they can't see how to escape. … most victims are constantly strategising and resisting" (p. 51). Allan Wade: Walker's theory became popular "because it failed to question the status quo in any meaningful way".
