## Learning objectives

- Explain why "every woman could be a victim" can marginalise some women
- Describe abuse affecting migrant women: visas, religious abuse, dowry abuse
- Explain the link between poverty and domestic abuse in the British data
- Describe economic abuse: its prevalence, three abuser types, and costs of leaving

## The ideas

**Health professionals.** Studies suggest very high prevalence among nurses and doctors (60% of nurses in a Delhi study; 45% of female medical staff in an Australian study; UK nurses three times more likely) (p. 66). "The underground does not discriminate. But it is deeper for certain types of women" (pp. 66–67) — where abuse compounds with racism, poverty, stigma, transphobia, insecure residency and disability.

**Respectability.** Advocates successfully made the typical imagined victim a middle-class white woman, so policymakers would fund protection; but Leigh Goodmark warns the "every woman could be a victim" rhetoric "pushed the concerns of marginalised women further to the sidelines" (p. 67).

**Migrant women.** Yasmin Khan runs a volunteer service for culturally and linguistically diverse women. "Vivian", Fijian, was kept virtually imprisoned by her older Australian husband, given no money, and faced losing her visa after he destroyed every document proving the relationship (pp. 67–68). Many perpetrators use little physical violence; abuse includes control of earnings, religious abuse ("you can't wear the hijab" or "you have to"), barriers to religious divorce, and abuse by in-laws. **Dowry abuse**: the groom's family demands payments after marriage, holding the bride to ransom; in the UK dowry is legal and has been linked to suicides and murders (p. 69).

**Poverty.** The sector rejects the idea that violence is born of poverty, but the British Crime Survey found women in poor households "over three times more likely" to be living with domestic violence (p. 70). Researchers revived the idea that men frustrated by lack of power at work, or money stress, may be more violent. Melissa Lucashenko's essay on Logan shows poverty and violence compounding: "You are 'free' but you're not really free" (p. 72).

**Economic abuse.** Even wealthy victims may have no access to money. In Australia leaving takes on average around 141 hours and costs around $18,000 (p. 72). A 2014 survey found fear of destitution the leading reason women feared leaving, ahead of physical violence. 52% of British survivors could not afford to leave; in one US study 99% of survivors had experienced economic abuse. Three kinds of abuser: **controllers** and **exploiters** (use financial abuse to control and meet their needs) and **schemers** (enter relationships to strip assets, then leave) (p. 73). Erin begged for dentist money while John secretly earned AU$250,000 a year. Legal costs can be weaponised: "he is going to use everything in the court system to destroy me financially". Women are often resourceful money managers once free (p. 74).

**Why we resist understanding.** We need to believe in love and that threats come from strangers; asking "Why doesn't she leave?" lets us separate "us" from "them" (p. 74). The better questions: why does he stay, and why does he do it?
