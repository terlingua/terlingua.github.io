## Learning objectives

- Describe child-to-parent abuse and its common pattern
- Explain why siblings can respond differently, and how abusers' influence persists without contact
- Describe children who kill abusive parents
- Explain the gendered pattern in how children carry abuse into relationships, and teen dating violence figures
- Explain why there is no straight line from childhood abuse to perpetration or victimisation

## The ideas

**Child-to-parent abuse.** After Liz left, her son Blake checked receipts like his father, held his sister by the neck, then punched Liz so hard police could "see the inside of your mouth"; others criticised her for calling police (pp. 162–163). NSW Police call it the state's fastest-growing category of domestic abuse, mostly aged ten to sixteen. Counsellor Eddie Gallagher (almost 500 families): the most common pattern is "a boy abusing a sole mother post-domestic-violence" — half his families; 70% of violent children are boys (p. 163). Siblings can differ: one "parentified" and protective, one anxious and withdrawn, one copying Dad. Some boys side with the father; others condemn him but copy him anyway (p. 164). Influence persists without much contact: seeing Mum belittled causes "losing respect"; siding with the perpetrator is a survival mechanism.

**Children who kill.** An Ohio fourteen-year-old shot her abusive father after police kept returning her; a Perth seventeen-year-old stabbed his stepfather who was strangling his sister (pp. 164–165). Some abusive parents also use single incidents to obtain orders against children who have long been victims.

**The child in love.** Boys more often adopt the abuser's behaviour; girls more often internalise the victim narrative — even when the abusive parent was the mother (Frankie) (p. 165). Frankie's boyfriends hit and raped her; she realised "I was drawn to guys that were similar to Mum" (p. 166). A quarter of girls and 18% of boys aged 13–17 report physical violence from a partner; in England and Wales, 10% of women aged 16–19 experienced domestic abuse in the survey period versus 6% aged 55–59. Later, Frankie's partner threatened suicide when she tried to leave and read her phone: "Normal scares me" (p. 167).

**No straight line.** Gallagher: assuming a link "is a dangerous assumption": of nearly a thousand abusive men he met, perhaps half had grown up with abuse, "But a lot of them hadn't" (p. 166). Joe feared becoming his father and is not; Finley, nine, fears it too.

**Running away.** In 2017, 103,000 young people in the UK sought council help with homelessness, more than half without meaningful help (p. 168). Refuges like Lillian's in Sydney (around 90% from violent homes) are rare; Anna slept rough at fifteen.
