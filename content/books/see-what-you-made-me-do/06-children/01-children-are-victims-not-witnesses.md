## Learning objectives

- Summarise Carly's story and what it shows about children's voices in family law
- State the scale of children's exposure to domestic abuse in the UK and Australia
- Explain why calling children "witnesses" or "exposed" misdescribes them
- State the co-occurrence of domestic abuse with child physical and sexual abuse
- Explain the costs of silencing children

## The ideas

**Carly.** After her GP told Erin, "If you don't leave, you're as bad as he is", she left John, who had choked her in front of their children (p. 145). The children refused contact; their psychiatrist warned a change of custody would cause "grave psychological damage". A family report-writer instead questioned Erin and recommended Zac live with his father without contact with his mother. An interim hearing without Erin's knowledge gave John sole custody; Carly threatened suicide; Erin fled with the children for nine months (pp. 146–147). Carly wrote: "I feel like I'm screaming in a soundproof room because my voice has been stolen from me" (p. 147). Returned, she was forbidden contact with her mother; she ran away at fifteen.

**Scale.** In England around 830,000 children live in households that have reported domestic abuse; 130,000 in high-risk households across the UK; the NSPCC estimates as many as one in five children are exposed — 2.4 million in England (p. 150). UK social services assess 692 families a day where a child is at risk of domestic abuse; domestic abuse is the most common factor in children being taken into care. In South Australia one in four children are reported to child protection by age ten — verified against records of 300,000 children (pp. 150–151).

**Not bystanders.** Children hide, comfort, call police, are recruited as spies, blame themselves and fear becoming abusers (p. 150). Herman: they must "preserve a sense of trust in people who are untrustworthy, safety in a situation that is unsafe" (p. 151). "These children are not bystanders. They are victims in their own right." UK law includes witnessing domestic abuse in the definition of "harm" (Adoption and Children Act 2002). Domestic abuse co-occurs with about **55% of physical abuse** and **40% of sexual abuse** of children; a victimised mother can also become abusive (p. 151).

**Silencing.** Media and advocates often avoid interviewing children; family law treats children "as little more than parental property" (p. 152). Ruth Clare, whose father returned violent from Vietnam, waited in the police car to be asked what happened; police asked about her grades and budgie: "The story they didn't want to hear now felt unspeakable" (p. 153). In England and Wales teenage suicides rose from 112 (2010) to 187 (2017); Australia's National Children's Commissioner found domestic violence a significant risk factor for youth suicide.
