## Learning objectives

- Explain why the view of babies as oblivious is obsolete (Gopnik, Bunston)
- Explain how chronic fear shapes developing brains, and the caveat of plasticity
- Describe children as "behavioural detectives" and the McCrory brain-imaging study
- Describe children's survival strategies: hiding, imagination, dissociation
- Explain Herman's account of why children blame themselves

## The ideas

**Babies are not oblivious.** Alison Gopnik: babies have "lantern consciousness" — less focused than adults' "spotlight" but absorbing many sources at once; they "learn more, imagine more, care more and experience more" than thought (p. 154). Wendy Bunston: infants are "sensory sponges"; in a violent home even the abused caregiver may be a source of threat if routinely afraid or unavailable; babies sometimes reach for facilitators over mothers. A fearful infant's brain "will build restricted pathways that serve the purpose of survival" (p. 155) — a hair-trigger amygdala. A footnote stresses plasticity: it is not "necessarily a life sentence", though healing may take years. Quiet, "good" babies who don't cry when left can be the most alarming: they have learned no one comes, or that crying brings violence (pp. 155–156).

**Behavioural detectives.** Herman: children "learn to recognize subtle changes in facial expression, voice, and body language" (p. 156). Finley, nine, read his father's face "like 'an algorithm'"; rules were "valid for ten seconds"; his father stonewalled the family for two years. Michele could "literally tell by my father's face whether he was going to be beating my mother up that night" (p. 157).

**Like combat veterans.** Eamon McCrory's UCL team (2011) scanned 43 children (20 from violent homes) viewing angry faces: their **anterior insula** and **amygdala** responded like the brains of soldiers exposed to combat — "hard evidence" of conditioning to threat (pp. 157–158). Nightmares and flashbacks follow.

**Strategies.** Anna learned not to intervene after her father "belted" her as a toddler; later she got him drunk so he'd sleep, and mapped hiding places, practising hiding "as though our lives depended on it" (pp. 158–159). Bruce Perry: children go to "a different place". Will imagines the beach; his brother calls their father "the prison guard". **Dissociation** — Olga Trujillo describes splitting and floating to the ceiling — is "a highly effective survival technique" that preserves some functioning (pp. 159–160).

**Self-blame as agency.** Herman: the child concludes her "innate badness is the cause" because it preserves "meaning, hope, and power": "If she is bad, then she can try to be good" (pp. 160–161). Blame may also be imposed: Michele's mother, herself abused, beat and gaslit her children and blamed Michele; Michele became an obsessive diarist to hold onto reality (p. 161).
