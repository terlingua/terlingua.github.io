## Learning objectives

- State the evidence on resilience among children exposed to domestic abuse
- Explain how PTSD became a diagnosis and why complex PTSD was proposed
- List symptoms of complex PTSD and explain why it is "caused by betrayal"
- Describe the DSM and ICD histories of complex PTSD
- Explain why C-PTSD is an adaptation that becomes maladaptive

## The ideas

**Resilience.** An American review of 118 studies found more than one-third of exposed children were doing as well as or better than children from non-violent homes; why is unknown — perhaps one teacher, friend or neighbour (p. 169).

**From PTSD to C-PTSD.** In 1980 PTSD entered DSM-III, recognising Vietnam veterans' symptoms: re-experiencing, nightmares, flashbacks, hypervigilance, irritability, detachment (p. 170). Judith Herman and Bessel van der Kolk saw childhood abuse survivors who didn't fit: ongoing abuse by trusted people produced extra symptoms — clinging, recklessness, chronic shame, self-harm, memory gaps, disembodiment, oversharing, somatic illness — often misdiagnosed as bipolar, depression or especially borderline personality disorder (p. 171). They proposed **complex PTSD** (or DESNOS, "Disorders of Extreme Stress, Not Otherwise Specified"). At its core it is a condition "caused by betrayal": children expect trauma to recur, misinterpret events, cling or defy, distrust, and are "out of touch" with feelings (pp. 171–172). Symptoms include distrust, suicidal thoughts, detachment, isolation, guilt, shame, helplessness, self-harm and substance abuse.

**Politics of diagnosis.** A DSM-IV committee voted 19 to 2 to include it, but it was blocked higher up — partly, Hill says, because symptoms overlapped many disorders, and C-PTSD is treated with therapy rather than medication (pp. 172–173). In 2018 the WHO recognised complex PTSD in ICD-11; the DSM still has no equivalent. Van der Kolk: such children get labels like "oppositional defiant disorder" (p. 173). (The book says DSM-IV appeared "in 2000"; DSM-IV was published in 1994 and its text revision in 2000.)

**Adaptation.** "C-PTSD is not a disorder, in the classic sense of the word. It is an identity that has formed around defence and survival" — brilliant in danger, maladaptive when safe; dissociating while crossing the road puts you in danger (pp. 173–174). Anna, diagnosed after a suicide attempt, felt relief: "you can work on it".

**Carly's ending.** Under a recovery order, police came; Carly locked herself in a car and threatened self-harm; she spent almost three weeks in hospital, then months in a refuge without schooling (pp. 174–175). Now seventeen, she lives with her mother and tops her class. Zac still lives with his father; he drew his mother a heart: "Don't stop trying, Mum."
