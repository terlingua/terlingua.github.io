## Learning objectives

- Explain how abusers can control victims through systems after separation
- Explain why survivors describe family courts as "through the looking glass"
- Explain why survivors and children can present poorly and why abuse dynamics seem implausible
- State the prevalence of abuse allegations in family court cases and the training gap

## The ideas

**Harry.** Nine-year-old Harry told doctors, police and psychologists his father hit and swore at him; he hid, and once called emergency services from a stranger's garden (pp. 241–242). When he refused to enter court, a judge ordered police to bring him by force. A report-writer accepted the father's claim that the mother had taught Harry to fear him. The judge gave the father sole custody and barred the mother from contact for six months, reasoning that leaving the children with her "would effectively remove the father from their lives" (p. 243). Harry's letter: "Why are you making me go? Wold you like to live there?"

**Systems as weapons.** "If the perpetrator is hellbent on maintaining control, they don't need the victim in physical proximity: they can control them through the system" — courts, child support, social security, tribunals; and "no system is as punishing—or as dangerous—as the family law system" (p. 244). The repeated narrative: "Mother alleges abuse, father claims she's alienated the children, court believes the father: mother loses the children." Survivors call it walking "through the looking glass": she becomes the one the children need protecting from. Reports across the UK, Australia and the US document failures to detect abuse; in 2018 the US Congress resolved that "abusive parents are often granted custody or unprotected parenting time"; in 2019, 120 British MPs demanded an inquiry (p. 245).

**Why victims look worse.** Traumatised survivors may seem disoriented; trauma disrupts memory, so accounts sound chaotic, while the father "may present as calm and rational" (Kelsey Hegarty) (p. 245). Abuse dynamics are counterintuitive: a family man playing sadistic games, a victim returning, a mother who encouraged contact then changed her mind, a child who loves the abuser or discloses then denies. "None of this makes sense—unless you understand the dynamics of domestic abuse."

**Core business, no training.** In England and Wales, allegations of domestic abuse feature in **62%** of child arrangement applications, yet there is "little to no compulsory training" for judges, lawyers or staff (p. 246). Mothers seeking no-contact orders are warned they risk being seen as hostile.
