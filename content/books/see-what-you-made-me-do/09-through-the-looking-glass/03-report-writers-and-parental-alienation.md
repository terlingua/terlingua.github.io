- Explain Richard Gardner's Parental Alienation Syndrome and why it was discredited

- State Joan Meier's finding on alienation claims

## The ideas

**Single experts.** In complex cases a court-appointed expert (UK: Cafcass "Section 7" reports and sometimes independent psychologists or psychiatrists; Australia: "single experts") writes a family report that often becomes "the most important piece of evidence" (p. 253). Children cannot testify; reports are how they are heard (p. 254). The pool is small; Parkinson: "The gods of the Family Court are psychiatrists". Australian report-writers have witness anonymity.

**Tina and Lucy.** Eight-year-old Lucy disclosed sexual abuse by her father. "Dr X" interviewed her with her father present, asked point-blank about bad touching, then concluded abuse unlikely, blamed an "anxious" mother (possible psychosis, without testing), and recommended continued contact — contradicting a 2000 judgment that the father was "a controlling and abusive man" (pp. 254–255). Lucy: "when Dad's sitting in the room, you don't want to tell". Tina consented to overnight contact for fear of losing custody; the abuse escalated (p. 256). Between 2006 and 2010, a fifth of women using Australian family law services felt "forced" or "bullied" into equal-time arrangements (p. 257).

**Alienation.** Accused parents counterclaim that the other parent "alienated" the children; it may be dressed as "enmeshment", "brainwashing" or "parentification" (p. 257). **Richard Gardner** proposed Parental Alienation Syndrome in the mid-1980s, claimed 90% of sexual abuse allegations in custody disputes were false without statistics, and prescribed moving the child to the alleged abuser, cutting contact with the mother and even jailing her (p. 257). He also called adult–child sex "ubiquitous" and society's reaction the problem (p. 258). Hill concludes he "devised Parental Alienation Syndrome to hide child sexual abuse from the eyes of the law". PAS as a syndrome is discredited, but "parental alienation" is widely accepted in courts (p. 259). Cafcass (2017) now recognises it, saying risks like domestic abuse must be considered first; but its checklist (one-sided views, no ambivalence, unprompted criticism) also fits abused children (p. 260). Indoctrination rarely turns children; Gardner's "cure" of severing contact persists.

**Sandra.** Children's consistent disclosures; child protection found the father a risk; an after-hours ex parte hearing on Dr X's advice removed them from school to their father (pp. 261–262). Teachers recorded children who froze and became withdrawn (pp. 262–263). Years later Tim returned to his mother; "Dr X is a liar" (p. 265).

**Meier.** When mothers allege abuse they lose custody in about a third of cases; when fathers cross-claim alienation and courts accept it, mothers lose custody in **73%** (p. 266).
