## Learning objectives

- Contrast the views of two child psychiatrists on false allegations and assessment methods
- Explain why abusers cannot be profiled and why observing a child with the accused proves little
- State the evidence on report-writers' training and report quality
- Explain how secrecy laws affect accountability
- Summarise calls for reform and child homicide data

## The ideas

**Two psychiatrists.** Christopher Rikard-Bell, a prolific Australian single expert (around 2,000 families), told Hill he estimated about 90% of sexual abuse allegations in a narrow, "highly conflicted" group were unfounded, often from anxious parents' questioning (p. 267). (His lawyer later said he does not hold that 90% of allegations in family law generally are false.) Carolyn Quadrio noted Gardner gave the same 90% with no data; research suggests "something like 80 to 90 per cent [of allegations] have a reasonable foundation" when investigated. On suggestibility: false memories of being lost in a mall can be implanted, but that is not the same as persuading a child of a specific sexual act (p. 268). Rikard-Bell described raising allegations in joint interviews with the child and accused; Quadrio said such interviews need forensic interviewers and recording (pp. 268–269). Australian family reports need not be recorded.

**No abuser test.** Kim Oates: abusers "can be model citizens"; "There's certainly no clear type"; abused children may relate well to the abuser (p. 270). Rikard-Bell had described likely abusers as having troubled histories — a pure psychopathology view.

**Expertise.** Deputy Chief Justice Faulks said minimum qualification is being a psychologist, psychiatrist or sometimes social worker (p. 271). Magistrate Matthew Myers: experts "rarely have the training"; a 2018 study found almost half of psychiatrists had less than two hours' family violence training; a 2012 UK study rated two thirds of 126 psychological reports poor or very poor, a fifth of authors unqualified, only a tenth in clinical practice (p. 272). No process reviews a child's safety after orders. Alex, placed with his father at six, later had PTSD (pp. 272–274).

**Secrecy.** In Australia and the UK publishing identifying details of family cases is a crime; UK reporting is "completely unworkable" for accountability (Tickle) (p. 265). Protection for children can hide bad decisions.

**Reform.** Barrister "Nikki": "Until they sort out the Family Court, people should stop telling women to leave" (p. 275). Calls for a royal commission in Australia; Women's Aid found nineteen children killed by fathers who perpetrated domestic abuse in contact-related circumstances (2005–2015) (p. 276). Claire Throssell's sons died in a fire set by their father during access.
