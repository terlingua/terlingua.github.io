## Learning objectives

- Explain how family courts shifted from caution to a pro-contact culture
- Evaluate the belief that mothers commonly fabricate abuse allegations (the Canadian data)
- Describe the Tolson appeal and the secrecy of family courts
- Explain how pro-contact culture "makes the domestic violence go"

## The ideas

**Draconian remedies.** Patrick Parkinson saw more cases where experts persuaded courts abuse hadn't happened and care was switched to the father with the mother cut off — "the most draconian remedy imaginable"; lawyers now warn clients that making allegations risks losing the child (p. 246). In a 2020 English case a 12-year-old was moved to his father within hours, with no direct contact with his mother for three months (pp. 246–247).

**The lying mother.** 43% of Australians believe women in custody battles often make up or exaggerate abuse claims (p. 247). Fathers 4 Justice popularised the narrative. But a Canadian study of thousands of child protection cases found fabricated allegations rare (about 4%; 12% in custody disputes), and within those, non-custodial parents (typically fathers) made them most often (43%), custodial parents (typically mothers) 14%, children 2% (pp. 247–248).

**Tolson.** In 2020, Ms Justice Russell overturned Judge Robin Tolson's finding that a mother "was not raped" because she took no physical steps to stop it; his approach implied sex regardless of willingness was acceptable; he had called threatening texts "sexting" (p. 248). Louise Tickle: secrecy lets "offensively vintage attitudes" persist (p. 249).

**History.** Australia's Family Court (1975) gave no-fault divorce — then "still not available in the UK" (p. 249). Fathers' groups claimed bias, but in 1979, 424 of 430 sampled cases were undefended: fathers had forfeited custody by consent. Courts were bombed in the 1980s (four killed); Leonard Warwick was charged in 2015 (p. 250). Late-1990s research emphasising both parents fuelled shared-parenting laws. By the 2000s, denying a father contact was seen as a risk "as serious as the risk of harm from abuse"; the "bad father" gave way to the "implacably hostile" mother (p. 251). Despite allegations in well over half of British cases, under 1% end with contact denied. Rotherham abuser Arshid Hussain was notified of proceedings about a son conceived by rape; four children were killed by fathers during court-ordered access in five years (BBC).

**Making the abuse go.** Samantha Jeffries: "You need to make the domestic violence go … otherwise, how can you possibly legitimise an ongoing relationship with a perpetrator?" — by discrediting the mother (p. 252). Unsupervised direct contact was the most common order (39%) in English cases with abuse allegations.
