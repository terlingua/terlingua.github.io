## Learning objectives

- Describe what male victims' accounts show, and which element is usually missing
- Explain the two research camps: family conflict and violence against women (VAW)
- Describe the 1975 National Family Violence Survey and Steinmetz's "battered husband syndrome"

## The ideas

**Southport.** At Queensland's specialist domestic violence court almost a quarter of protection orders in the first half of 2016 were against women (p. 178). Hill set out to build conclusions "from the ground up", including testing whether men's rights groups are right that women's violence is ignored.

**Male victims.** Patrick's wife shook their baby, advanced with a knife, threw a vase over their daughter; he stayed to protect the children, and was diagnosed with PTSD (pp. 179–180). "Karl" escaped steak knives plunged where he'd been lying; "Ben" knelt to be hit; "NH", in a British study, recorded twenty years of coercive control: blows, an 8-inch knife, Christmas Eve in a car, sleep deprivation, and accusations that *he* was the violent one (pp. 180–182). But typically one element is missing: **fear**, "the lynchpin of coercive control" (p. 181). Men may fear for their children, but rarely the existential terror female victims describe. Coercive control of men is "extremely rare" but "not unheard of". Men usually have money to leave and are not afraid of being killed, but rationalise like women do.

**Two camps.** **Family conflict** researchers say women are as violent as men at home, with credible statistics; **VAW** researchers (feminist scholars, workers, police, medics) say perpetrators are overwhelmingly men, also with credible statistics (p. 183). Public belief is shifting: in Australia only 64% in 2017 agreed DV is "mainly perpetrated" by men, down from 86% in 1995. Outside the home men commit violence at least nine to one (p. 184).

**1975.** Richard Gelles and Murray Straus surveyed 2,143 randomly selected people: "the home the most violent social setting" outside police and military (p. 184). 16% of couples had violence in the past year. Victims nearly equal: 12% of women and 11.6% of men; severe violence 3.8% vs 4.6%; of relationships with a primary aggressor, 27% men vs 24% women (p. 185). Caveats then: men's violence causes the most injury; women have less money to leave; much women's violence may be self-defence. Suzanne Steinmetz's 1977 "The Battered Husband Syndrome" claimed as many battered husbands as wives, hidden by "selective inattention"; critics mocked "battered data syndrome" (p. 185). It founded the "gender symmetry" field; Gelles and Straus later supported it and say they were shunned and threatened (p. 186). Over 100 studies now show symmetry. "The answer depends on how you define 'domestic violence'."
