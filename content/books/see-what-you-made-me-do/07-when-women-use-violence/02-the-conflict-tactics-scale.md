## Learning objectives

- Explain how the Conflict Tactics Scale (CTS) works
- Explain the main criticisms: framing, lack of context, false equivalence, one act equals "violent"
- Explain the "initiation" finding and the Jasmine thought experiment
- Explain Kimmel's war analogy

## The ideas

Almost all symmetry studies use the **CTS**, devised by Gelles and Straus (p. 186). Its script begins: "No matter how well a couple gets along, there are times when they disagree … or just have spats or fights because they're in a bad mood" (p. 187). It asks which acts were used in the past twelve months "to settle differences", ranked by severity: from saying something spiteful, to slapping, kicking, biting, hitting with an object, to threats or attacks with a knife or gun.

**Criticisms.**
- **Framing.** Kimmel: it frames domestic violence as an argument out of control, but coercive control "is not about losing one's temper"; abusers manufacture conflict (p. 187).
- **No context.** It records acts, not why, impact, or role in a dynamic (pp. 187–188). A kick meaning "leave me alone" scores like one meaning "if you try to leave … I'll break all your ribs". Gayla Margolin: a woman's hardest punch, laughed at by her husband, counts as "husband abuse" equal to his jaw-breaking punch (p. 188).
- **One act = violent.** The Dobashes: one trivial act and many serious acts both make someone "violent"; a failed attempt to hit and beating someone unconscious yield "mutual violence".
Family conflict researchers accept this because their question is how couples resolve arguments; Kimmel calls it disconnected from experience: "Who initiates the violence, the relative size and strength … all will surely shape the experience … but not the scores on the CTS."

**Initiation.** In 1985 they asked who started it; women initiated at nearly equal rates (p. 188). Thought experiment: Nelson forcing Jasmine to sleep in the car is not "violence" on the CTS; if he hadn't hit her that year he's non-violent; if she threatened him with a knife she'd be the initiator of "severe violence" and he her victim (pp. 188–189).

**Kimmel's analogy.** "Imagine simply observing that death rates soared for men between ages nineteen and thirty … without explaining that a country has declared war. … Context matters" (p. 189). The CTS has nonetheless been valuable in showing how frequent violence is in couples.

## If you build things

The CTS is an event-counting schema with no session state: a log of discrete events stripped of intent, severity of outcome, initiator history and the surrounding pattern. Any risk or abuse-detection system that counts events per actor will reproduce its errors: false equivalence, misattributed initiators, and blindness to non-event control.
