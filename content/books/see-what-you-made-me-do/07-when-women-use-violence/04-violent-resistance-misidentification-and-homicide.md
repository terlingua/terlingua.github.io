## Learning objectives

- Define violent resistance and explain why fear distinguishes it
- Explain how cross-applications and incomplete police reports turn victims into "perpetrators"
- Explain how mandatory arrest increased arrests of women
- Explain the gender gap in domestic homicide and what it implies
- State Ellen Pence's position on women's violence and the chapter's conclusion

## The ideas

**Violent resistance** (Johnson): women retaliate against a violent, dominating partner for revenge, to stand up for themselves or to stop the abuse; at the extreme, killing him seems the only escape (p. 195). It is "common, but rarely spoken about". In New York State, 67% of women jailed for killing someone close had been abused by that person. Women's violence is likelier to be motivated by **fear** than control. Judy Atkinson's Kungas: a woman labelled a "nuisance caller" after eleven years and 500 pages of police records finally flogged her abuser with a pole and spent eleven months on remand (p. 196).

**Two reports.** Olivia and John: a summary report said both assaulted each other, so both got orders; a detailed charge sheet showed John punched, strangled and headbutted her until she vomited blood, and she scratched and bit to escape (pp. 196–198). Wangmann: we need more than "who did what to whom" and "how many times". Police say "She's as bad as him."

**Mandatory arrest.** From the 1980s in the US, police discretion was removed; arrests of women soared — in Sacramento women's arrests rose 91% (1991–96) while men's fell 7% (p. 199). Women admit what they did ("Yeah, I stabbed him! But this is why"); men deny. Crystallee Crain was arrested in her pyjamas with thirty-four bruises. **Concussion** can look like drunkenness — incoherence, irritability, aggression (p. 200). Rosemary O'Malley: police "see just one scene in a movie. … they don't look at the whole movie."

**The homicide gap.** Teresa Bradford's ex-husband, bailed despite a strangulation charge, killed her and himself (pp. 201–202). Hill: "I defy anyone to find a single case in Australia where a female perpetrator has done this." NSW review: in the decade to 2010, 28 of 29 men killed by female partners had been the abusers (p. 202). Sue Osthoff: women kill partners "almost always in self-defense"; men because "they think they are going to leave". After US refuges appeared, male perpetrators killed by female victims fell 69% (1976–2002) — refuges have "done more to save the lives of the men who terrorise them" (p. 203). One in Three's "one death every 10 days" is "rank propaganda". Male victims "are rarely at risk of being killed".

**Helping men.** Male victims need help; Paula Mudd helps anyone, asking "Where's your responsibility in this?" — perpetrators don't take ownership (pp. 203–204).

**Pence.** "Women are very, very capable of being violent"; the sector "kept saying it didn't exist" and lost credibility (p. 204). Ignoring it leaves a vacuum for disinformation. Conclusion: women can be physically and psychologically abusive and cause trauma, but in coercive control — "suffered by 60 to 80 per cent of women who seek help" — women are "an extremely small minority". "In its most dangerous forms, it is a crime perpetrated by men against women" (p. 205).
