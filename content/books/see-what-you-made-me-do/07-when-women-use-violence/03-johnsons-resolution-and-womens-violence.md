## Learning objectives

- Explain Michael Johnson's resolution: situational couple violence vs intimate terrorism
- Explain why survey and agency data see different phenomena (including non-response)
- Describe what women's violence typically looks like in protection-order data
- Distinguish expressive from instrumental violence
- Explain how perpetrators present as victims with selective evidence

## The ideas

**Johnson's lightbulb.** Penn State's Michael Johnson compared CTS studies with police, hospital and shelter data; agency data showed male violence that was "more frequent, more severe, escalated over time" (p. 189). Both datasets are legitimate; they capture different phenomena (p. 190):
- **Situational couple violence** — most common; erupts from frustration or to win an argument; often mild and sporadic, sometimes escalating or lethal; no one partner dominating; leaving is not usually life-threatening; usually ends when the relationship does. Women are as likely to be aggressors, but impact is gendered: men "do more serious damage". In 1985, 3% of women victims needed medical care versus 0.4% of men.
- **Intimate terrorism** (coercive control) — dominates agency data because it produces the fear that sends victims to agencies; "almost exclusively men", with rare exceptions (a man terrorised by his police-officer wife) (pp. 190–191).
- **Non-response.** Over 40% refused the National Family Violence Survey; Johnson concluded most victims of intimate terrorism are among the non-responders (p. 191).
Both camps use the same term for different things. Neil Frude: "both husbands and wives may be said to be 'aggressive', but many more husbands are 'violent'".

**"A hit is not a hit."** Di Mangan: a "slamming row" where you know you won't be "king-hit" differs from the sector's caseload of women threatened with homicide, tortured, brain-injured or homeless (p. 192).

**What orders against women look like.** Women's Legal Service NSW: 68% of applications against women concerned a single incident; only 6.1% alleged a prolonged pattern; most common allegation a threat (47.6%), then physical violence (39.8%), of which 23% was biting, usually on the arm or hand — consistent with defending against a headlock or strangulation (p. 193). Women's breaches are usually "technical" (prohibited texts); men are likelier to breach physically and stalk.

**Expressive vs instrumental.** MensLine's Mark Walters: most women's violence is "expressive" — tension builds, "bang", meant to push him away; coercive controllers use "instrumental" violence — planning to "stuff the other person up": hiding keys, letting tyres down — "You're mine" (p. 193). About 90% of male callers start as "victims"; about 10% still say so by the end, and intimate terrorism among them is "as rare as hen's teeth" (p. 194). Exception: men with disabilities, who may be exploited. Perpetrators use "strategic moments" — a clip of her with a knife, without what came before. Walters: "there are challenging, complex, difficult women and there are vulnerable men", but for serious harm "Overwhelmingly, women are the victims." A study of 128 police cases found only one female perpetrator tried to create fear and control (p. 195 n.).
