## Learning objectives

- Explain why the appendix is included in the UK edition and what "dadirri" means
- Describe Fiona's experience of violence and of services
- Explain how Biderman's chart can describe the state's treatment of Indigenous people
- State key statistics on violence against Indigenous women

## The ideas

**Why include it.** In the Australian edition this chapter precedes the last; in the UK edition it is an appendix. Hill says it taught her most about why abuse thrives and how to fix it, including "the manmade nature of modern patriarchy" (p. 299). British readers will recognise overpolicing of black communities, underreporting of their homicides, reluctance to interfere with "cultural practices" and erasure of colonial history. **Dadirri**, from Miriam-Rose Ungunmerr-Baumann, is "inner, deep listening and quiet, still awareness" — and listening "should go both ways" (pp. 299–300).

**Tennant Creek.** After a three-year-old was raped in 2018 despite warnings since infancy, Prime Minister Turnbull rode the Night Patrol; Fiona drove him past the house: "I don't want your tears, Malcolm … I want your answers" (p. 300).

**Fiona.** Her partner surrounded her with male relatives; police at first treated it as "just Aboriginals fighting" and pointed a gun at her (p. 301). After a 45-minute attack causing skull fractures and more, he got a good behaviour bond and knocked her out on the court steps. A restraining order was evaded by forcing her interstate. In safety she juggled 27 services; workers cut her daughter's hair, culturally a sign of sorcery, and called her fear "ridiculous" (p. 302). She moved to Tennant Creek's refuge, printed "No More Violence" T-shirts to make the problem visible, and trained everyone (pp. 303–304).

**The state as abuser.** "Just as Biderman's Chart of Coercion describes the tactics … it equally describes the systematic abuses inflicted on Indigenous peoples by the state": surveillance, micro-regulation, degradation, broken promises, blame (p. 305). Fiona: "you've got my people in a domestic violence relationship. Only we can't call the cops on you, because you are the cops." "When we see equity, we'll see change."

**Statistics.** Indigenous Australian women are 35 times more likely to be hospitalised for family violence assaults (up to 80 times in some remote areas) and 11 times more likely to die (p. 306). Celeste Liddle: "the death of Aboriginal women is expected". Remoteness and kinship make leaving harder — his relatives run the shop, the airline office, the council housing list. In one study 59% of married Indigenous women reporting abuse had a non-Indigenous husband (p. 307).
