## Learning objectives

- Explain why claims that Indigenous family violence is "cultural" are contested and how courts used them
- Explain the bride-capture myth and what Phyllis Kaberry found
- Explain Hill's claim that today's domestic abuse has a British cultural heritage
- Describe colonial sexual violence, protection acts, lateral violence and the Stolen Generations
- Explain the "trauma trail", why explanation is not excuse, and examples of community-led change

## The ideas

**"Customary law" defences.** Until 2007, defences argued violence was customary; in a 2001 case a man who raped a fifteen-year-old "promised wife" got twenty-four hours, the judge accepting anthropological evidence (p. 322). Hannah McGlade: whose custom — "the powerful members of the community, the men"? (p. 323). Marcia Langton: "are the Aboriginal legal services … ever going to stop arguing that rape is traditional law?" In 2007 the Australian government prohibited culture as a defence.

**Before colonisation.** Judy Atkinson: pre-contact societies "were not perfect" — all humans have conflict (p. 323). The Seven Sisters story is a cautionary tale about male pursuit. But early accounts were unreliable: **bride capture** began with David Collins (1798) describing what he hadn't seen; historian Liz Conor: "There is no evidence in the colonial archive that Aboriginal men routinely abducted women" (p. 324). Scars were often grief or ritual. The trope served dispossession. Anthropologist **Phyllis Kaberry** (1930s) found economic interdependence, love and loyalty, relative sexual freedom for women, and violence governed by laws and punishment — wives sometimes struck first (p. 325). Women sourced most food, independently, so men couldn't control them through food (p. 326). Atkinson suggests "egalitarian hegemony" rather than patriarchy. Langton: today's chaos "is not the society of old". (A footnote names foetal alcohol spectrum disorder as a factor needing study.)

**British heritage.** Domestic abuse "does have a clear cultural heritage—in Britain" (p. 327). Mill and Taylor described women dying "from incessantly repeated brutality"; Judge Buller's reputed "rule of thumb"; Frances Power Cobbe's *Wife Torture in England* (1878): "The notion that a man's wife is his PROPERTY … is the fatal root of incalculable evil" (pp. 327–328). Robert Hughes: colonial sexism "amplified by penal conditions" (p. 330).

**Colonial violence.** Aboriginal women were treated as available; enslaved on stations; "gin sprees" aimed to rape, maim or kill (p. 330). Aboriginal men punished rapists under their law, triggering cycles of violence (p. 331). Women adapted, including by trading sex, but never with simple choice. **Protection acts** forced people onto missions, banned language and culture and dismantled kinship, producing **lateral violence** — "you turn on your own" (p. 332). The **Stolen Generations**: up to 50,000 children removed 1910–1970 by skin colour (A. O. Neville: "these children grow up as whites"); many were abused (pp. 332–334). *Bringing Them Home* (1997) found it amounted to genocide (p. 334).

**Trauma trails.** Trauma is ongoing across "ten generations"; child removals ten times the rate; youth suicide (p. 335). Caroline Atkinson's study of 58 violent Indigenous men: almost 90% exposed to family violence, 59% met PTSD benchmarks; being listened to was cathartic (pp. 335–336). "But an explanation is not an excuse" — women who also survived trauma demand truth-telling and face silencing (p. 337).

**Community-led change.** In Yungngora, seven women enforced elders' by-laws with three-month exile: weekly DV incidents fell from six to none, crime 60%, school attendance to about 90% (pp. 337–338). Charlie King's "No More" campaign in Ramingining made football "a festival, not a warzone", with women on the board and teams; police reported family violence down 70% (pp. 338–339). State of Origin matches see domestic assaults spike 40%.
