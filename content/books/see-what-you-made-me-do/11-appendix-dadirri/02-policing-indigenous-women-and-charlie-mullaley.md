## Learning objectives

- Explain how police failures and overpolicing punish Indigenous women who seek help
- Describe how unpaid-fine arrests deter reporting
- Explain the Tamica and Charlie Mullaley case and what the inquiry found

## The ideas

**Failures.** Police accepted partners' accounts that Kwementyaye Green and Natalie McCormack had stabbed themselves; a coroner called one investigation an "irrational preoccupation" with the partner's story (p. 307). Lawyer Martin Hodgson: police are "actively punishing Aboriginal women who even dare to speak out" (p. 307): a mother seeking help for her daughter was jailed for weeks over an unregistered-dog fine (p. 308). Police at DV callouts check everyone's ID and drag away family members with petty fines, so women avoid calling (p. 308). Hodgson sleeps in his car outside clients' homes to deter police harassment (p. 309). In Perth a breastfeeding Noongar mother of five was jailed to "pay off" $3,900 in fines at $250 a day (p. 310). Hannah McGlade: overpolicing means the same behaviour others get away with is punished. Arrest is twenty times likelier for Indigenous people (p. 311).

**Tamica and Charlie.** In Broome in 2013, Mervyn Bell bashed Tamica Mullaley and stripped her naked in the street; a nurse called police (p. 312). Police insisted on questioning the injured, distressed victim; she spat at an officer; they arrested her (p. 313). She had life-threatening internal injuries. Meanwhile Bell took her ten-month-old son Charlie (p. 314). Her father Ted begged police to search; they said they had no resources — one car guarding Tamica, the other writing charges against her and Ted. Police described Ted as drunk and aggressive (unsupported by CCTV; he hadn't drunk in thirty years) and refused an $800 phone trace he offered to pay for (pp. 314–315). A file entry recorded a call, which Ted denies making, saying Charlie was safe (p. 316). No missing person report was filed; an alert went out more than thirteen hours after the abduction was reported (p. 318). Charlie was found dead; Bell was convicted of his rape and murder (p. 319). Police then prosecuted Tamica and Ted (p. 320). The Corruption and Crime Commission found failures but no serious misconduct, adding "Bell alone was responsible" (p. 320). The family asked: "What happens when the next Aboriginal grandfather walks into the station and reports his grandson missing?"
