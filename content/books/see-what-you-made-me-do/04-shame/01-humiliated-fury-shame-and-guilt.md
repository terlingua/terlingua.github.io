## Learning objectives

- Explain what Neil Websdale found in reviews of domestic homicides
- Define "humiliated fury" (Helen Block Lewis) and why shame was a "sleeper emotion"
- Distinguish shame from guilt and explain why the difference matters for violence
- Explain why Cobras are not thought to be driven by humiliated fury
- Explain shame as an innate affect with a social function, and how it is gendered

## The ideas

Chapter 4 is written with David Hollier. **Neil Websdale**, head of the US National Domestic Violence Fatality Review Initiative, reconstructs domestic homicides to design interventions — "the haunting presence of the inexplicable" (p. 101). In hundreds of killers he found neither pure power-seeking nor a unifying mental illness, but recurring **shame**, "particularly compromised masculinity". In *Familicidal Hearts* he studied 76 coercive controllers who killed their families; they "didn't actually appear to be powerful" (pp. 101–102). Their abuse brought privileges, but its driver was "an insatiable hunger for intimacy and belonging" mutated by shame. When shame was triggered, they overrode its pain with a feeling of power by lashing out.

**Humiliated fury.** Coined in 1971 by psychoanalyst **Helen Block Lewis**, the first to study guilt and shame clinically: some men use humiliated fury "to protect themselves against feeling powerless and defective; by blaming others" (p. 102). The schoolyard bully daring a smaller boy to repeat what he muttered is the domestic abuser beating his wife for challenging his opinion. Lewis called shame the **"sleeper emotion"** behind depression, obsession, narcissism and paranoia; therapists rarely discussed it, instead diagnosing borderline or narcissistic disorders. A 2011 survey found shame still "easily overlooked or actively avoided".

**Not Cobras.** People with antisocial disorders ("Cobras") are not driven by humiliated fury; they seem "less vulnerable and dependent" with less abandonment anxiety (p. 103). Psychopaths can feel shame but not spontaneously: "If most people feel an emotion between seven and eight on a dial of ten, I feel it between zero and two."

**Shame is not guilt.** Guilt: I did something bad; apology and forgiveness can absolve it. Shame: "I am bad" — "unloved and unlovable"; no one can absolve it but yourself (p. 103). Lewis: shame wants you to be "nonexistent" — "I could crawl through a hole". It "persists like a chemical burn". Studies of prisoners in Germany and the US: guilt makes reoffending less likely; shame "produces a desire to lash out against unfair emotional pain and social blame".

**Innate and social.** Shame is one of nine innate **affects** (with anger, sadness, fear, joy, anticipation, surprise, "dissmell" and disgust) (p. 104). A baby rebuffed by her mother slumps and looks away. Its function, like pain for tissue, is "to prevent us from damaging our social relationships, or to motivate us to repair them" — exile once meant death. It is also why victims hide abuse.

**Gendered shame.** For women, shame is a tightrope of conflicting demands; Brené Brown: "do it all, do it perfectly and never let them see you sweat" (pp. 104–105). For men, one rule: "do not be weak". The closer one identifies with strict gender norms, the more shame at breaking them.

**Not an excuse.** Many men who feel shame do not abuse. Abusers take "the path of least resistance": blame others and use violence for "a phony—and often short-lived—feeling of power and pride" (p. 105). "It's not the feeling that causes all the trouble, it's the way we respond to it."
