## Learning objectives

- Explain how Websdale's review of Kevin's killing of Joanna illustrates humiliated fury
- Explain how protective "chivalry" can mask danger
- Explain the "look powerful vs feel powerful" gap and Jac Brown's emotional sequence
- Describe Kylie Dowse's Insight programme and the link between releasing shame and taking responsibility
- Explain why "nice guys" who kill confound us

## The ideas

**Kevin.** Homeschooled on a farm by a strict Baptist-preacher father, whipped for dishonesty, ashamed of a limp and his short stature, drinking from fourteen (p. 112). Not overtly misogynistic: "always a gentleman". Rodney Vlais (No To Violence): many abusive men frame abuse as **protection** — "I guard the family" — and a woman who refuses protection risks being recast as a "slut" who "deserves whatever she gets" (p. 113). In the air force Kevin idealised Joanna, feared she would "see my weakness … and leave me", and pressed her to set a wedding date. Red flags: jealousy, shoving her into a wall, grabbing her face, heavy drinking (pp. 113–114). In a motel, after she threw the ring at him and told a friend she would leave, he strangled her. He called it "a snap action", but admitted to a detective that at the end "I really wanted to let go, but I knew I couldn't 'cos she'd have jumped up and went straight to you" (p. 114) — a moment of choice. Websdale: shame and humiliated fury at impending abandonment were replaced "with a rage that quickly and temporarily restored a sense of pride and a sense of control". Then came more shame, suicide attempts and confession (p. 115).

**Looking vs feeling powerful.** From the victim's side it looks like ultimate power and control. But "in the moments before a man takes control, he can feel at his most vulnerable and powerless" (p. 115). Jac Brown's study of 24 abusive men: feelings went **vulnerable → scared → angry**, sometimes in a split-second — like a light switch: we see only the light. Gilligan: chronic shame becomes numbness, like the ice in Dante's lowest circle (p. 116). Kevin's fear was "a shame-based fear of being exposed as defective and unworthy of love".

**Insight.** Kylie Dowse, an Aboriginal feminist therapist, began working with men because survivors wanted "the violence to end, not the relationship" (p. 116). In her groups, shame stopped men being honest. Asked what shame would look like in the room, the men said it was **male** and hyper-critical (p. 117). They put shame on a chair, then outside — and abused it. Dowse noticed they had recreated abuse, so they sent shame to a "Shame Support Group" in the broom cupboard. Men then called each other out: "I think your shame got out of the room" (p. 118). Releasing shame made responsibility possible; bravado fell away, tears became possible, accounts matched the women's. "Paul" stopped himself — "my shame must be back" — and said "it's not her fault, it's never been her fault"; he asked Crystal what she wanted, then pleaded guilty so she wouldn't have to testify (pp. 118–119). Other men paid child support or stopped harassing exes. No formal evaluation, but reportedly high retention.

**Nice guys.** Kevin, and Robin Michael — a hospital general manager who killed his wife over an imagined affair and wrote "I can't understand it" — were "normal" men (p. 120). Allan Johnson: such cases unsettle our worldview. Shame is universal; women carry plenty; but men commit most violence. The way we react to shame is gendered; next is patriarchy.
