## Learning objectives

- Name Nathanson's four responses to shame and explain why "attack others" is most destructive
- Explain why narcissism is a defence against shame
- Explain Sapolsky's point that testosterone is a status hormone, not an aggression hormone
- Explain Gilligan's theory that violence aims to replace shame with pride, and why trivial triggers matter
- Explain abusers' sense of victimhood and dependency, and Dutton's account of how shame becomes misogyny

## The ideas

**Nathanson's compass** (*Shame and Pride*, 1992): four responses, each a "library of methods" (pp. 105–106).
1. **Withdrawal** — from looking down to avoiding people; driven by fear of contempt; "actually the most courageous" because it allows processing shame without harm.
2. **Attack self** — from self-deprecating humour to self-loathing, self-harm and suicide.
3. **Avoidance** — most invisible; at the extreme, narcissism: a personality built as "a firewall" against shame, which responds aggressively to any challenge. What might he do to someone who threatens to expose him?
4. **Attack others** — replaces shame with pride, briefly, and can create more shame: shame "at its most destructive" — humiliated fury (p. 106).

**Testosterone.** Robert Sapolsky: when testosterone rises after a challenge, it "prompts whatever behaviors are needed to maintain status" (p. 107). It is "a status-seeking hormone". If status came from toilet-cleaning, testosterone would surge at the bowl. "The problem is the frequency with which we reward aggression." For men today, status lies in appearing in control.

**Gilligan.** James Gilligan, after decades in prisons, found violent men "keeping a secret": they "felt ashamed—deeply ashamed, chronically ashamed, acutely ashamed" (p. 107). They often said "because he disrespected me" — "He dissed me." His thesis: all violence begins with shame; its purpose is to replace shame with pride (p. 108). Synonyms include insulted, disrespected, ridiculed, rejected, "losing face"; envy and jealousy are its "siblings". Triggers are trivial — a bin not square to the kerb (Men's Referral Service), a grievance months old — and their triviality deepens the shame: "ashamed even to reveal what shames them" (p. 109). Nigella Lawson mentioned looking forward to being a grandmother; Charles Saatchi grabbed her throat: "I am the only person you should be concerned with". "Pushing my buttons": any disagreement is an attack, requiring a reaction rather than a response. Judith Graham: "A shame-obsessed person hears ridicule even when none was intended."

**Victimhood.** Feeling shamed, abusers see themselves as victims entitled to strike back or tighten control (p. 109). In Michelle Jones's study "Peter" claimed his partner's refusal of sex was sexual violence against him (p. 110). Helpline staff hear the difference: a woman wants the violence to stop; the man "wants her punished". Matt Boulton: respect "was … one of my rights".

**Dependency.** Men who were controlled as children may vow "I'm never going to be controlled again" (p. 111). Van Altena: men who finally find love hold on tight — "If I let go of the control … she will leave me." Dutton and Golant name a cold, rejecting mother or shaming father; intimacy threatens to show "him and his shame without the makeup"; rage follows; he blames her, then "them" — "an evolving misogyny" (p. 111). Abuse is "a learned means of self-maintenance" (p. 112). Erich Fromm: control over a living being is the "transformation of impotence into omnipotence".
