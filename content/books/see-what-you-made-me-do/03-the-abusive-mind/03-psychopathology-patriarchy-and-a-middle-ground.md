## Learning objectives

- Describe the psychopathology model, its treatment, and the evidence against it as a full explanation
- Describe the feminist model, the "room and floor" metaphor, and the evidence for it
- Explain why family-only abusers challenge a purely feminist model
- Explain Ellen Pence's 1999 revision and the book's integrated view

## The ideas

**Complexity.** The brain has 86 billion neurons with hundreds of trillions of connections; "We can't even say for sure what a 'self' is" (p. 90). Two models dominate (pp. 90–91). (A footnote dismisses the neo-Darwinist view that abuse protects reproduction: abuse often starts or escalates in pregnancy — "Why would a man bent on passing along his genes endanger his progeny?")

**Psychopathology model.** Abuse comes from mental illness, substance abuse and childhood trauma; gender is irrelevant. It asks "Why did *this* man beat his partner?" and looks for disorder; some psychiatrists think if none is found he is probably not abusive (p. 91). Treatment: CBT, communication and anger skills, time-outs — "unlearn" violence (pp. 91–92). Evidence: abusers show higher-than-average personality disorder, but Edward Gondolf's study of 840 abusers found only a small subgroup disordered; most were "no more disordered than regular, non-abusive people" (p. 92). Childhood exposure has only a "weak-to-moderate relationship" with later abuse. Lundy Bancroft: abusers' minds "work logically"; "it is the abuser's value system that is unhealthy—not their mind" (p. 93). Yet US federal funding shifted to DSM-diagnosis research, starving relationship research (Gottman, Holtzworth-Munroe). Arendt's "banality of evil": ordinary minds commit cruelty.

**Feminist model.** Abuse is a by-product of patriarchy, in which men feel entitled to dominate (p. 94). Metaphor: the abuser is a room; gender attitudes are the **floor**; alcoholism, illness, unemployment are **furniture** — they shape the room but sit on the floor. Evidence: men are more prone to abuse if socialised into rigid gender roles, believe men are superior, or feel their authority threatened, especially when women don't comply. Reform: take responsibility, then see entitlement as part of patriarchy. Karen Willis: abusers "want to hurt, humiliate and dominate" (p. 95). Adopted by the UN, WHO, the UK, Australia and others; Australia's National Plan 2010–2022 prescribes equality and attitude change. (Australian programmes often combine CBT and gendered education.)

**Where it falls short.** Coercive controllers score high on misogyny, but family-only abusers are no more misogynistic than non-violent men and rarely disordered (p. 96). Glen likely needs both lenses: misogynistic entitlement and childhood-rooted insecurity. Michael Salter says populist analysis treats perpetrators as surfaces "written upon by TV and pornography" and that treatment based only on liberal-feminist principles "is not working" (p. 97).

**Ellen Pence's swivel.** Co-founder of the Duluth model, Pence wrote in 1999 that "many of the men I interviewed did not seem to articulate a desire for power" (pp. 97–98). She was not saying they did not seek power, but that power was not what they *felt* driving them. Slogans like "He does it for power" were "advocacy jingles". She reframed abuse as rooted in **entitlement** formed by society and connected to patriarchy, and saw that different abusers need different treatment: treating addiction might stop random violence, but not a controlling alcoholic; Cobra types are "singularly resistant to change" (pp. 98–99). The question becomes: *why* do men want power and control?

**Integration.** Feminist scholars also pioneered work on men's vulnerability and dependency (Salter). Hill integrates feminism and psychology, and points to Atwood's line: "Men are afraid women will laugh at them, and women are afraid men will kill them" (p. 100).
