## Learning objectives

- Explain why researchers built "typologies" of abusive men, and the caution attached
- Describe Gottman and Jacobson's "Love Lab" study and its heart-rate finding
- Contrast Cobras and Pit Bulls: emotions, violence, dangers of leaving
- Explain why violence can decrease over time without control decreasing

## The ideas

The chapter opens with a survivor's words — "I want people to stop asking 'why does she stay' and start asking 'why does he do that?'" — and a rape during which the abuser said "I'm doing this because I love you" (pp. 75–76). There is "no such thing as a typical male perpetrator". Explaining abuse "is not an exact science. It's a battle of ideas" (p. 76). Treating "domestic abuse" as one phenomenon is "a terrible mistake", so researchers built **typologies**. Caution: even their authors say individuals often cannot be slotted into a type; men shape-shift (p. 77).

**The Love Lab (1995).** John Gottman and Neil Jacobson at the University of Washington wired 200 couples to monitors and asked them to fight. Among 63 men with histories of coercive control, about 80% showed rising heart rates as they argued; about **20% showed falling heart rates** — outwardly furious, inwardly calm, calmer than when told to relax (p. 77).

**Cobras** (the 20%): calm inside, strike "hard and fast"; "more aggressive and even sadistic"; less emotionally dependent; "frightening … and yet at the same time captivating" (p. 78). "George" beat Vicky over a pizza and said "She was a bitch and she deserved it." He needed not Vicky but "the power to control her". Cobras were hedonistic, impulsive, pathologically entitled, uninterested in intimacy, unafraid of abandonment, likeliest to fool police at the door, and likeliest to have antisocial personality disorders. 78% grew up with violence (vs 51% of Pit Bulls). 38% had threatened wives with a weapon (vs 4%); 9% had stabbed or shot them (vs none) (pp. 80–81). "Life is a game."

**Pit Bulls** (the majority): anger builds until they explode; "codependent and profoundly insecure, twisted by morbid jealousy and paranoia" (p. 79). "Don" was soft-spoken and tender at first, beaten by his preacher father as a child; his violence became daily; he insisted he was the victim and "didn't know he was dangerous" (p. 80). Cobras "knew they were dangerous—they just didn't care." Pit Bulls look like "nice guys"; their abuse surfaces only in intimacy.

**Leaving.** "Although one is safer trying to leave a Pit Bull in the short run … Pit Bulls may actually be more dangerous to leave in the long run" — they stalk and may kill (p. 81). Cobras are most dangerous when about to be exposed (police, court); if she leaves quietly he may move on. After two years almost half the Pit Bull marriages had ended but no Cobra had separated — the wives were "too terrified to leave".

**Less violence, same control.** Violence decreased over time; Gottman: "Once a perpetrator has frightened the victim, they don't have to use as much violence"; a swift head movement and glare suffices — signals invisible to a courtroom (p. 82). Many believed they were "undiscovered geniuses". Successful ones — "CEOs, detectives, judges" — are "really terrifying" because they take revenge when women leave.

**From practice.** Andre Van Altena, after decades in Australian prisons, sees the same split; with the "extreme end" appeals to empathy are futile and may be faked; motivation must speak to self-interest — freedom, quality of life (p. 83).
