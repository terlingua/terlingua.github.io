## Learning objectives

- Describe the replication status of the heart-rate finding
- Name Holtzworth-Munroe's three dimensions and three types
- Explain the link between domestic abuse and mass violence
- Explain the benefits and harms of typologies, and why Glen's case shows dimensions rather than types

## The ideas

**Replication.** Other researchers agree on the descriptions, but "subsequent studies have failed to replicate the results exactly" (pp. 83–84). One replicated the 20% heart-rate split but drew different conclusions. Gottman attributes this to their unusual lab. Amy Holtzworth-Munroe thinks the heart-rate conclusions may be problematic but the basic categories were supported (p. 84).

**Three types (1994).** Holtzworth-Munroe and Stuart considered (1) severity and frequency of abuse; (2) violence outside the family or criminal history; (3) traits matching personality disorders (p. 84).
- **Generally violent/antisocial** — roughly the Cobras: violent childhoods, impulsive, generally hostile, criminal, a threat to the public too; "classic sociopaths, psychopaths and malignant narcissists" or men who act like them.
- **Dysphoric/borderline** — roughly the Pit Bulls: abuse confined to the relationship, codependent, terrified of abandonment, morbidly jealous; "the most likely to kill themselves after they've killed their partners" (p. 85).
- **Family-only** — not coercive controllers; violence vents frustration from life stressors and then seems to disappear; remorse is often real; likelier to complete treatment. Unlike the other two, "not overtly misogynistic", no more than non-violent men (p. 86).

**Mass violence.** Between 2009 and 2016 more than half of American mass shootings began with the murder of an intimate partner or family member; many perpetrators of terror attacks had histories of domestic abuse (Rebecca Traister) (p. 85).

**Uses and harms.** Jane Wangmann: typologies show whether violence is coercive control or conflict-based, one-off, beyond the family, or linked to psychological factors — so one-size-fits-all treatment will fail (p. 86). But do they make victims safer? A judge persuaded a father is "family-only" when he is a coercive controller could endanger children (p. 87). Holtzworth-Munroe was alarmed at judges classifying men from the bench: "even I couldn't do that! … They're more dimensions than clear types." Practitioner Lizette Twistleton: labelling a man a "cobra" casts him out and pushes him "to a more extreme place".

**Glen.** A 21-year-old with a long criminal record grabbed his girlfriend Michelle by the throat; violence went both ways; police assessed him "family-only". But with previous girlfriends he had headbutted, strangled, held a partner hostage and threatened to burn people — "the most dangerous end of the spectrum" (Corr & Gadd) (pp. 87–89). His violence came from paranoia, fear of abandonment and humiliation rooted in a childhood of shame and abuse. Lesson: never categorise an abuser from one relationship.

## If you build things

Typologies are classifiers, and this chapter is a case study in misusing one: labels designed for research populations applied to individuals at the bench, from one relationship's data, with high-stakes outcomes and asymmetric costs of error. A "family-only" false negative can put a child with a coercive controller. Risk tools in policing and child protection face the same problem.
