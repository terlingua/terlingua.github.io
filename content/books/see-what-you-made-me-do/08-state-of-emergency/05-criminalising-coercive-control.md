## Learning objectives

- Explain why incident-based laws miss coercive control
- Describe the England and Wales offence (2015) and early experience
- Explain the Sally Challen case accurately
- Describe the Scottish Domestic Abuse Act's design and why it is called a "gold standard"
- Explain why law alone is not enough

## The ideas

**The gap.** Where coercive control is not a crime, police can arrest for discrete violent acts but not an ongoing pattern (p. 234). Governments tell women abuse "isn't just physical" yet the law says otherwise. In a 2014 UK survey 94% of victims said coercive control was the worst part. Evan Stark: justice systems respond to "the men's definition of violence"; "Women … have never equated violence with physical violence only" (p. 235).

**England and Wales, 2015.** Controlling or coercive behaviour became an offence (Serious Crime Act 2015, s. 76), up to five years. Recorded crimes rose over 100% between 2016–17 and 2018–19, but referrals, prosecutions and convictions stayed low (pp. 234–235). Convictions have involved confiscating phones, dictating food or sleeping on the floor, banning work, deleting male contacts, and threatening self-harm to prevent leaving.

**Sally Challen** killed her husband Richard with a hammer in 2010 after decades of "controlling, isolating and humiliating" abuse and was jailed for life in 2011 (p. 235). After a campaign by her sons, the Court of Appeal in February 2019 quashed the murder conviction. (The book compresses what followed: the court ordered a retrial; in June 2019 the prosecution accepted her plea to manslaughter by diminished responsibility and, having served her sentence, she walked free.) Women's Aid called it a sign that judges are recognising coercive control's impact.

**Scotland, April 2019** — the Domestic Abuse (Scotland) Act 2018, called a "gold standard", drafted with the women's sector, backed by training of 14,000 officers and staff and a thousand "champions", and of judges and sheriffs (pp. 235–236). It requires a **course of conduct** (two or more incidents) that a reasonable person would consider likely to cause physical or psychological harm, including fear, alarm and distress. Behaviours include isolation, depriving needs, monitoring and spyware, taking wages, threats to publish information or harm a child or pet, criminal damage, coercing crime, and abuse of children to encourage self-blame (p. 236). All harms fall under one charge; no physical violence is required; it covers both intention and **recklessness** — matching instrumental and spontaneous abusers (pp. 236–237). Children can be harmed even if they didn't see or hear (aggravation). Prosecutors find it easier to prove because everyday evidence (phones, routines) becomes admissible (p. 237). Early convictions followed within a month. Marsha Scott: "The law we have in Scotland now actually prosecutes what hurts people" (p. 238). The book gives the maximum as fifteen years; the Act sets fourteen years on indictment.

**Limits.** Police response remains inconsistent ("That's not illegal, that's just a bad relationship"). "New laws on their own are not enough—it is the culture … that needs to change" (p. 239).
