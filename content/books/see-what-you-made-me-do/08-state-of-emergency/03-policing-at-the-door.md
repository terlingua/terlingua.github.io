## Learning objectives

- Explain why the moment police arrive is critical
- Describe what protective policing looks like
- Describe common police failures and the cases of Laura Stuart and Kelly Thompson
- Explain the "incident-based" mindset and police attitudes towards victims
- Describe Victoria Police's reforms

## The ideas

**Snakes and ladders.** Women "journey away" from abuse step by step and can slip back (p. 220). Calling police is fraught: will they believe me, take my kids, make it worse? When police arrive, "the perpetrator is no longer the most powerful person in the room" (p. 221).

**Protective policing.** Genelle Warne, Blacktown's DV liaison officer, with dedicated staff, made follow-up visits; one woman disclosed six years of torture; Warne persuaded her to leave and drove her to her first counselling session (p. 221). Such officers give personal numbers, take statements off-duty, accept messy chronologies, resist manipulation, and "don't make their protection contingent on the woman's willingness to leave, or even cooperate" (p. 222).

**Failures.** Nidhi, confined with her daughter to an unfurnished room, was removed by police to a hotel at her abuser's request (p. 222). Heather Douglas's study of 65 Australian survivors found police responses "inconsistent at best"; one officer told a woman she might be "putting too much pressure on him" (p. 223). UK domestic violence protection orders were used in only 1% of cases by 2018; Theresa May (2016) spoke of "shameful attitudes" (p. 224).

**Laura Stuart** reported Jason Cooper eighteen times; police never arrested or interviewed him; efforts went to making her act (p. 224). **Kelly Thompson**: a strangulation incident was dismissed as "just a domestic", recorded as Wayne told it, rated low-risk; breaches weren't charged; she was told it took "ten breaches"; she called police 38 times in 39 days; a neighbour's midnight call was brushed off; she was found stabbed (pp. 224–226). The coroner said she took "all the right steps". Accountability is rare (Rachel Horman).

**Attitudes.** A survey of 204 officers found genuine victims existed "on a purely hypothetical plane", outnumbered in officers' minds by "imposters, liars and timewasters" (p. 227). Context: Ken Lay's single-day log shows the volume (pp. 228–229). Many officers want a "gotcha" crime with a clear victim and perpetrator; but abuse may involve no discrete crime, and victims are bound by love, loyalty, children and trauma (pp. 229–230). Douglas: police must aim to protect even "uncooperative" victims, not revert to "incident-based, 'beyond reasonable doubt'" thinking.

**Reform.** Victoria Police, after Kelly Thompson, said family violence would be pursued as urgently as terrorism and created family violence investigation units with detectives and analysts targeting repeat offenders (pp. 230–231).
