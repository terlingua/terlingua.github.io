## Learning objectives

- Explain the origins of women's police stations in Brazil and Argentina
- Describe how they differ from ordinary police
- State the evidence on their effects, and the argument about early reporting
- Explain the "structural problem" Kerry Carrington identifies

## The ideas

More than 80% of women "underground" have never reported to police (p. 231). Women in post-dictatorship Brazil and Argentina distrusted police who had abducted, raped and tortured them. Brazil created the *delegacia da mulher* — brightly painted converted houses in neighbourhoods, led and mostly staffed by women (p. 231). Argentina's first opened in 1985; Buenos Aires province has 128 stations and about 2,300 officers, reporting through their own commissioner.

**Different mission.** Their purpose is "not to enforce the law; it's to protect the victims" (p. 232). "The police there are completely guided by what the woman wants to do": investigate and prosecute if she chooses; otherwise orders, removal of the abuser, or a talk with him. Welcoming spaces with childcare; lawyers, social workers and psychologists under one roof. Outreach: visiting hospitals, leafleting outside churches, Christmas toy runs, rural units, a 70,000-person march.

**Early reporting matters.** A decade-long US study found perpetrators 89% less likely to reoffend after police were called; only 11% of Australian perpetrators kill within the first year of a relationship — so early reporting is a window (p. 233). Brazil: a study of 2,074 barrios found homicides of women fell 17%, and 50% for young women aged 15–24 in metropolitan areas. A 2011 UN Women evaluation in Latin America found improved access to justice and services; many people believed they reduced violence (77% in Brazil). Now in 13+ countries.

**Structural problem.** In Australia, 85% of sworn officers are men while 85–95% of victims of gendered violence are women (p. 233); in the UK women were 30% of officers in 2019; in the US under 13% (p. 234). Carrington: telling women to report won't fix it; stations "don't need cells" and are cost-effective.
