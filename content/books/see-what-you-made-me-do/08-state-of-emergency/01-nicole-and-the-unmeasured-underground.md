## Learning objectives

- Explain what Nicole Lee's story shows about disability, mental health and missed moments
- Explain why most of the underground is never measured, and what is
- Describe trends in severity and the idea of perpetrator backlash

## The ideas

**Nicole.** Nicole, who uses a wheelchair, depended on her husband "Greg" as carer. "You're told your whole life, being disabled, it's going to take someone really special to want to love you" (p. 207). She developed anorexia and was diagnosed with bipolar and borderline traits: "No, my mental health is suffering because of the violence" (pp. 207–208). Anorexia was a survival tactic — hospital was the only escape. In family therapy Greg said "it's because I rape her in her sleep sometimes" (p. 208). She was offered police and refuge, but no one said her kids could come; she refused and was discharged to him (pp. 208–209).

**Critical moments.** When private abuse becomes public, the victim glimpses an alternative; "These moments must be seized immediately. If they are missed, her belief that nobody can help her is confirmed" (p. 209). Inaction made her doubt herself: "Maybe it's not as bad as I think." After a suicide attempt, a hospital called child protection; police removed Greg: "They didn't take the decision of leaving off me—they took it off him" (p. 210). Alone without a carer, she nearly asked for the order to be lifted; eight weeks later the **disability** sector's family violence package, not the DV sector, made staying apart possible (p. 211). Greg contested orders from prison; charges required dates, so only nine of countless rapes could be charged (p. 211 n.). He pleaded guilty "to save him time in prison" and got two years six months (p. 212).

**What we can't measure.** Hospital data rarely record cause; an Ohio State study found 81% of help-seeking survivors had a head injury — how many brain injuries go unseen? (p. 212). What we can measure: 19% of homicides in England and Wales are intimate partner related; two men a week kill a current or former partner; police get over 100 domestic abuse calls an hour; only 17% of women experiencing partner abuse in the year to March 2018 reported it (pp. 212–213).

**Helplines** are perhaps the best source on what abuse looks like (p. 213). Safe Steps records risk factors (strangulation, weapons, threats to kill, sexual assault, stalking): severity and frequency rising since the mid-2010s; 67% of callers in 2016–17 needed immediate protection (p. 214). O'Malley: "violence that's really tantamount to torture". Annette Gillespie predicted backlash: men "have never had less control in society", so home becomes "the only place they can safely have control".
