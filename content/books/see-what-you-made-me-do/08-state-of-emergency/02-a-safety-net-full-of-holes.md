## Learning objectives

- Explain what refuges provide and the effect of funding cuts in the UK and Australia
- State the Femicide Census findings on separation and timing
- Explain why motels and B&Bs are poor substitutes, using Grace and Yvonne
- Explain the cost argument (£66bn vs £393m) and housing shortages
- Describe how tech-facilitated stalking works and why it matters for refuges

## The ideas

"We've spent millions on campaigns urging women to leave … But instead of being there to catch women and children when they jump, we're holding out a safety net that's full of holes" (pp. 214–215). Reports to police in England and Wales rose to 1.3 million incidents in 2018–19.

**Refuges** began with Erin Pizzey in Chiswick, 1971: safety, sleep, a shower in peace, and trained caseworkers with expertise (p. 215). In Australia women are rarely placed directly in refuges now — motel vouchers instead; South Australia's threshold was "high risk of imminent injury or worse" (p. 215). In the UK, austerity cuts (2010–17) reduced refuge spending by 24% across most local authorities and one in five refuges closed; in 2017, 60% of referrals had to be declined, more for black and minority ethnic women; Refuge's funding for safe houses was cut by a third (pp. 215–216). During Covid-19, Vera Baird warned of an "epidemic inside this pandemic".

**Why it matters.** More than half of Refuge's 2018 residents had a life-threatening injury; 44% had been attacked with a weapon (p. 216). Femicide Census 2018: **41%** of women killed by partners had left or taken steps to leave; **30%** of those were killed within the first month and **70%** within the first year. A refuge with space only in Orkney — 600 miles away; Sunderland lost all refuge funding.

**Substitutes.** Grace, a Korean woman, was left in a motel without interpreter or visa help; her husband found her (p. 217). Yvonne, refused emergency accommodation, returned to her abuser and died that night of a head injury; the review said a refuge bed would have saved her (pp. 217–218). Motels can be squalid and unsafe.

**Housing and money.** 12% of those helped with homelessness in 2017 were escaping domestic abuse; 145,000 social homes needed a year, under 6,000 built in 2017–18 (pp. 218–219). Abuse costs England £66 billion a year; fixing refuges about £393 million — cuts are "the ultimate false economy" (p. 219).

**Tech abuse.** Julie Oberin: refuges no longer ask if he is tech-savvy — "You can just YouTube instructions" (p. 219). A perpetrator ran behind a secret refuge: likely a tracker — test by leaving the car; if he still finds you, "it's the phone" (p. 220). At one refuge, 80–85% of arriving women were being tracked; car trackers cost about $10; phone spyware about $45 a month and can read encrypted messages because encryption protects messages in transit, not on the device. Surveillance often starts early, "an early foray into control".

## If you build things

The encryption point is worth getting right: end-to-end encryption protects content between devices; a compromised endpoint (stalkerware, a shared account, a cloud backup the abuser can log into) defeats it. Safety features that help: alerts for new logins and linked devices, location-sharing indicators, quick account audits, and avoiding defaults that silently share location within a family plan.
