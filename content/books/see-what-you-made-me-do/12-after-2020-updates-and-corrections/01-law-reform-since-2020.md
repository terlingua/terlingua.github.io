## Learning objectives

- Describe the main changes made by the Domestic Abuse Act 2021 in England and Wales
- State when the non-fatal strangulation offence and post-separation coercive control coverage took effect
- Describe the spread of coercive control offences in Ireland and Australia
- Describe family-law changes in England and Wales and Australia since the book

## The ideas

This chapter is not in the book. It records developments after the UK edition (2020) that bear directly on the book's arguments, checked against official sources.

**Domestic Abuse Act 2021 (England and Wales).** Royal Assent in April 2021. It created a statutory definition of domestic abuse that expressly covers controlling or coercive and economic abuse, and recognises children who see, hear or experience the effects of abuse as victims in their own right — the point Chapter 6 makes. It established a Domestic Abuse Commissioner and barred abusers from personally cross-examining victims in family proceedings.

**Non-fatal strangulation.** Section 70 of the 2021 Act inserted a specific offence of non-fatal strangulation or suffocation into the Serious Crime Act 2015 (s. 75A), in force from **7 June 2022**, maximum five years' imprisonment. Chapter 1 noted that this conduct was often charged as common assault.

**Coercive control after separation.** The original s. 76 offence applied only where partners lived together if they were no longer intimate partners. Section 68 of the 2021 Act removed the "living together" requirement, so the offence covers ex-partners regardless of cohabitation, for conduct from **5 April 2023** — reflecting the book's point that abuse often continues after leaving.

**Beyond Britain.** Ireland criminalised coercive control from **1 January 2019** (Domestic Violence Act 2018, s. 39) — so the book's statement that "outside the UK, coercive control is not a crime" was already inaccurate when written. Tasmania had offences of economic abuse and emotional abuse or intimidation since its Family Violence Act 2004. New South Wales's coercive control offence commenced **1 July 2024** (maximum seven years; current or former intimate partners). Queensland's commenced **26 May 2025** (maximum fourteen years). Scotland's maximum, which the book gives as fifteen years, is fourteen years on indictment (Domestic Abuse (Scotland) Act 2018, s. 9).

**Family law.** In England and Wales, the Ministry of Justice's "Harm Panel" report (June 2020) found a "pro-contact culture" and that allegations of domestic abuse were often minimised — echoing Chapter 9. The Court of Appeal in *Re H-N and Others* [2021] EWCA Civ 448 told family courts to focus on patterns of coercive and controlling behaviour rather than isolated incidents. No-fault divorce arrived under the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020, in force from **6 April 2022**. In Australia, the Family Law Amendment Act 2023 repealed the presumption of "equal shared parental responsibility" from **6 May 2024**; the court notes there was never a requirement for equal time. Australia's 2010–2022 national plan was succeeded by the *National Plan to End Violence against Women and Children 2022–2032*.

**Sally Challen, precisely.** The Court of Appeal quashed her murder conviction in February 2019 and ordered a retrial; on 7 June 2019 the prosecution accepted her plea to manslaughter by diminished responsibility; she was sentenced to nine years and four months, already served, and freed.

**Mental health classification.** ICD-11, which includes complex PTSD, came into effect on 1 January 2022. The American DSM-5-TR (2022) still has no separate complex PTSD diagnosis.
