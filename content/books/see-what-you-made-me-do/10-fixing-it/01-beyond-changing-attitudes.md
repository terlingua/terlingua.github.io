## Learning objectives

- Explain Hill's critique of relying mainly on long-term attitude change
- Explain the argument that funding shortfalls are a political choice
- Describe the precedents of random breath testing and smoking
- Explain the proposal to target a measurable reduction in domestic homicide

## The ideas

**"Total capitulation."** UK strategy since 2010 centres on changing gendered attitudes; laudable but slow (p. 277). In 2019 Australia dropped its target of significant reductions by 2022, saying rates would likely be unchanged for a decade while attitudes shift — Jane Gilmore: at least 600 more women murdered first (p. 278). Lore Heise's 1994 paper framed abuse as needing to redefine what it means to be male and female. Hill: important, but "as a primary strategy … horribly inadequate". "Why aren't we confronting perpetrators as our first and most urgent priority?"

**Money is a choice.** Jocelyn Bignold's 24-hour crisis refuge didn't know, fourteen weeks before funding expired, whether it would survive; Scott Morrison said funding wasn't "a simple matter of going to the ATM" (p. 279). "But the nation does have an ATM … It's called a budget" — the UK found £1 billion for Northern Ireland to secure DUP votes in 2017. Richard Denniss: governments make women's groups feel poor and greedy. "Governments have the money. Withholding it is their choice" (p. 280). Victoria's royal commission (2015) made 227 recommendations; the state committed to all, $1.9 billion over four years.

**Precedents.** Random breath testing in NSW (1982) cut fatal accidents 48% within two months despite fierce backlash (p. 280). UK smoking bans, plain packs and a smoke-free 2030 goal — fewer than one in six adults smoke (pp. 280–281). The WHO treats domestic violence as a public health issue.

**A target.** At least twice a week in England and Wales a man kills a current or former partner. "When will we see a brave politician … say, As a nation, we are going to halve the domestic homicide rate?" (p. 281). Most perpetrators are never held to account. Attitude work and respectful-relationships education remain essential, but perpetrators abusing "today" need stopping (p. 282).
