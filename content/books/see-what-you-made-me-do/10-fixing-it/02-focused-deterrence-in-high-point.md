## Learning objectives

- Explain focused deterrence and its origin in Boston's Operation Ceasefire
- Describe High Point's call-in, the A–D offender lists and the "Al Capone" approach
- State the six goals and how the risks to victims were weighed
- State the evaluation results and why the approach shifts the burden from victims

## The ideas

**Origins.** David Kennedy's "focused deterrence" ("pulling levers") began with Boston's Operation Ceasefire against youth gun violence in the 1990s: a small group commits most violence; tell them the community cares and will help them change, but violence will bring swift, severe, coordinated consequences (p. 285). Results: 60% reduction in youth homicide, 25% fewer gun assaults (p. 286).

**High Point, North Carolina.** Two murder-suicides by known offenders in 2008 prompted Deputy Chief Marty Sumner to act; domestic violence was a third of homicides (p. 285). Every one of the 17 recent domestic killers had a criminal record and every victim had sought help (p. 286). A working group spent two years designing the programme.

**Lists.** **A**: most violent — immediate prosecution, on any available charge (the "Al Capone approach": Capone went down for tax evasion) (pp. 284, 287). **D**: police call, no arrestable offence — a visit and a letter. **C**: arrested — a detective's visit explaining consequences; flagged so judges set tougher bail; victim gets safety planning. **B**: reoffending C — a public **call-in** at City Hall (Feb 2012): community members say "we care … but it's gotta stop"; law enforcement promises consequences (pp. 282–284). Victims are consulted on letters; a "proximity informant" may watch. Victims said the key was that the city, not they, initiated it. Kennedy: the approach treats offenders "as rational, responsible adults" (p. 287).

**Goals** (p. 288): protect the most vulnerable from the most dangerous; move the burden from victims to the state; focus deterrence and support on the most dangerous; counter the "experiential effect" (weak responses teach impunity); use any offence; avoid adding risk to victims. Risks considered: revenge, or a man so controlling she stops calling. Advocate Shay Harger: "Things were already going horribly wrong." Fortnightly inter-agency meetings (p. 289).

**Results (2017 evaluation)**: intimate partner violence arrests and injuries down 20%; domestic homicides from 18 in six years to 9 in a decade (under one a year); 8 of the 9 killers were unknown to police; of 2,300 notified offenders, 16% reoffended vs 45–64% elsewhere (pp. 289–290). Spartanburg's adaptation: 14 homicides (2015) to 3. In London, a perpetrator-focused Scotland Yard strategy (Laura Richards) cut domestic homicides 58% (pp. 283–284).

## If you build things

This is a data-driven intervention: tiering offenders from existing records, flagging across systems so a traffic stop in a neighbouring town surfaces the flag, and a fortnightly cross-agency review. The working group explicitly modelled failure modes — including one where a metric (fewer calls) would look like success while the victim was more endangered.
