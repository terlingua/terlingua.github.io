## Learning objectives

- State the core statistics the Introduction uses to show the home is the most dangerous place for women
- Explain why there is no "victim type"
- Explain why domestic abuse is "not just violence" and not quite a "crime" in the usual sense
- Explain the contradiction of perpetrators who see themselves as victims
- State the book's thesis about entitlement to control, and why "real men don't hit women" misses it

## The ideas

**The most dangerous place.** "In every country around the world the home is the most dangerous place for a woman" (p. 1). Of 87,000 women killed globally in 2017, about 30,000 were killed by an intimate partner and 20,000 by a family member (UNODC). In England and Wales two women a week are killed by a man they have been intimate with. Hill's line: "it's not the monster lurking in the dark that women should fear, but the men they fall in love with" (p. 2).

**2014 as a turning point.** In Australia, Rosie Batty's statement after her ex-partner killed their son Luke at a cricket ground in February 2014 made the country "ready to believe in domestic abuse" (p. 3). The same year saw US campus sexual-assault action, the Isla Vista killings by Elliot Rodger, #YesAllWomen and #WhyIStayed.

**Scale in Britain.** Almost 9 million British adults have experienced domestic abuse; one in six refuges closed after 2010 (pp. 4–5).

**No victim type.** People assume only poor, vulnerable or "victim-minded" women are abused. Some groups are overrepresented (women on insecure visas, those who grew up with abuse, the young, the disabled, rural women), but across thousands of studies no researcher has found a victim "type". A review concludes "there is no evidence that the status a woman occupies, the role she performs, the behaviour she engages in, her demographic profile or her personality characteristics consistently influence her chance of intimate victimisation" (p. 5).

**Not just violence; not quite a crime.** Domestic abuse "is not just violence. It's worse": the perpetrator uses the partner's love, trust and secrets "as a blueprint for their abuse" (p. 5). Crimes are incidents; domestic abuse is "a terrifying language that develops slowly and is spoken only by the people involved". Many of its acts are not offences — controlling clothes, money, family contact, gaslighting — yet these "show up as red flags for domestic homicide" (p. 6). Repeated trauma can produce "mental captivity".

**Why does he stay?** We ask why she stays; we rarely ask why a man who seems to hate his partner stays, or kills her when she leaves. Perpetrators often "believe with all their heart that they are the victim" — a doublethink that lets one share "real men don't hit women" posts and still abuse (p. 6).

**Entitlement, not permission.** "Men don't abuse women because society specifically tells them it's okay. Men abuse women because society tells them they are entitled to be in control" (p. 6). Evan Stark: "Addressing control … is far more difficult than stopping men from being violent" (p. 7). So leaders saying "real men don't hit women" sidestep the norm of male control they themselves often embody.

**Backlash and gender.** Awareness campaigns may make some abusers more dangerous; Safe Steps heard from women whose partners went "nuts" every time an anti-violence ad aired (p. 7). The book focuses on men's violence because of scale and severity: of 270 UK women killed by partners or ex-partners in 2015–18, 96% were killed by men; of 96 men killed, over half were killed by men (p. 8). Same-sex abuse exists and is held in place by heterosexism and homophobia; Claire Renzetti finds the greater the power difference, the more severe the abuse. Some men in heterosexual relationships are victims too.

**Bystanders.** Judith Herman: "All the perpetrator asks is that we do nothing … The victim … asks the bystander to share the burden of pain" (p. 9).
