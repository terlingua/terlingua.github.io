## Learning objectives

- Explain why the "brainwashing" explanation for Korean War POW collaboration was wrong
- Name Biderman's three elements of coercion and his eight techniques
- Explain why the chart has no category for physical violence
- Explain how researchers linked the chart to domestic abuse, and why that clears victims of the charge of weakness
- Explain Herman's point that a domestic victim is "taken prisoner gradually, by courtship"

## The ideas

The chapter opens with Rob and Deb Sanasi. Rob, never physically violent, criticised and bullied Deb, made it hard for her to work or see family and controlled the money. Deb's observation: abusers act as if they had "studied some kind of domestic abuse handbook" (p. 13); the perpetrator becomes your "main frame of reference … very much like a cult". When women first fled to refuges in the 1970s (the first in Chiswick), their stories were so alike that one worker could "finish a woman's story halfway through it" (p. 14).

**Korea, 1953.** American POWs returning from North Korean camps had informed on fellow prisoners, made false confessions and broadcast communist propaganda; twenty-three defected to China. Newspapers and even the head of the CIA blamed "brainwashing", a supposed new mind-control weapon. Albert Biderman, a US Air Force social scientist, found instead that the captors had used "age-old methods of coercive control", based "primarily on simple, easily understandable ideas of how an individual's physical and moral strength can be undermined" (p. 15). The airmen were unprepared because no one had seen them used in war.

**Dependency, debility, dread.** Biderman's three elements, achieved by eight techniques:

1. isolation
2. monopolisation of perception
3. induced debility or exhaustion
4. cultivation of anxiety and despair (threats)
5. alternation of punishment and reward (occasional indulgences)
6. demonstrations of omnipotence
7. degradation
8. enforcement of trivial demands

Seen alone, each act looked isolated; seen together they formed a system (p. 16). The chart has **no category for physical abuse**: violence was used but was not "a necessary nor particularly effective method"; skilled interrogators avoided it and relied on "vague threats" (p. 16).

**Afterlives.** In 2002 US military trainers used the chart to teach "coercive management techniques" at Guantanamo, apparently missing that these techniques were famous for producing false confessions; Congress banned them in 2005. In 1973 Amnesty International included the chart in its *Report on Torture*.

**The domestic connection.** Diana Russell, in *Rape in Marriage*, put the chart beside common abuser tactics: "virtually identical". The difference: captors used the techniques tactically, while husbands appeared to replicate them "unconsciously" (p. 16). Herman: "The [coercive] methods that enable one human being to enslave another are remarkably consistent" (pp. 16–17); the perpetrator becomes "the most powerful person" in the victim's life. Threats against loved ones convince the victim "that the perpetrator is omnipotent, that resistance is futile".

**What this clears.** Herman, Lewis Okun and Evan Stark showed these techniques match those of kidnappers, hostage-takers, pimps and cult leaders. So "there is nothing uniquely weak, helpless or masochistic about victims"; their responses equal those of trained soldiers (p. 17). Resistance is even harder at home: a hostage sees the captor as an enemy, but a domestic victim is "taken prisoner gradually, by courtship". To resist she would have to suppress affection she already feels and resist arguments that "one more proof of her love" will end the violence (p. 18). "What should surprise us … is not that a woman can take a long time to leave, but that she has the mental fortitude to survive."

**Not monsters.** Abusers "are rarely simple thugs or sadists"; they can be loving, kind and charming. Culture teaches us to read obsession, jealousy and possessiveness as passion (p. 17).
