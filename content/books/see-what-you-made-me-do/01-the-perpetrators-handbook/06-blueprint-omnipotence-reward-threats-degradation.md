## Learning objectives

- Explain how surveillance and strangulation demonstrate omnipotence
- State the strangulation risk figures in the book
- Explain how alternating punishment and reward binds a victim (the "Cycle of Violence")
- Explain the "willing slave" fantasy of total control
- Describe threats (including to pets) and degradation, and Herman's point about being "broken"

## The ideas

**6. Demonstrate omnipotence.** Relentless surveillance makes her feel escape is impossible and no authority can protect her. If she has safe places — work, church, the supermarket — "he colonises them". Stark: abusive relationships filmed in slow motion "would resemble a grotesque dance whereby victims create moments of autonomy and perpetrators 'search and destroy' them" (p. 30). Omnipotence can be bought: a hidden phone app (about £160 a year) gives remote access to texts, calls, photos, email, browsing and GPS.

**Strangulation** proves he controls whether she lives. It "seldom leaves a mark", and by the time police arrive he may look like the victim because she scratched and bit to breathe (p. 31). US research: more than 65% of domestic abuse victims will experience near-fatal choking. After one Australian state made non-fatal strangulation punishable by up to seven years in 2016, almost 800 people were charged in a year. At the time of writing, UK strangulation was often prosecuted only as common assault. Researchers have likened it to waterboarding; victims can die days or weeks later from internal injuries; "abusers who strangle are seven times more likely to end up killing their partner" (p. 31).

**7. Alternate punishment and reward.** Captors were "quick-change artists". Domestic abusers mix violence with "the appearance (or performance) of a healthy relationship" (p. 32). Hill's **Cycle of Violence**: explosion → contrition → promises and pursuit → false honeymoon → tension → standover → explosion. Kindness bonds her, lowers her guard, prompts her to share secrets he may later use, and restarts her search for what "sets him off". Herman: the perpetrator seeks to instil "not only the fear of death, but also gratitude for being allowed to live", until he may seem her "savior" (p. 33).

**Total control.** John Stuart Mill (1869): men want "not a forced slave but a willing one". Orwell's O'Brien: "We convert him." Herman: "the desire for total control over another person is the common denominator of all forms of tyranny" (pp. 33–34). Because subjugation is not natural to women, modern abusers need coercive control to recreate it. Not all are conscious of this: cold controllers run a conscious system; jealous controllers recreate it unconsciously; insecure reactors move "in and out of the control regime, as if switching channels". What unites them: "a super-charged sense of entitlement" (p. 34).

**8. Threats.** Threats to kill her, the children, family or pets "render a woman captive" (p. 34). Nelson made Jasmine sleep in the car with their baby and threatened to kill her, the baby, her family and her cats. Abusers often know the limits of their power; hurting a pet is less risky. In studies worldwide about **half** of victims said animals were threatened or abused (p. 35). Covert threats (tampering with brakes) and threats of suicide also work. Kay Schubach: "It's like being in a house with an assassin."

**9. Degradation.** Captors reduced POWs to "animal level" to "make the cost of resistance more damaging to self-esteem than capitulation" (p. 35). Domestic degradation is "obscenely targeted" because he knows her secrets. Karen Willis: women say "Give me a black eye any day … It's the words that hurt", and 99% of her service's trauma work is "reversing the impacts of those words" (p. 36). Degradation can be a whisper at a party, renaming her "Slut", dehumanisation (eating off the floor, wearing a leash), or sexual coercion and rape. David Livingstone Smith: "You don't have to be a monster or a madman to dehumanise others." At the extreme, Herman says, the victim is forced "to violate her own moral principles and to betray her basic human attachments" — for example in relation to her children — and is then truly "broken" (p. 37).

**Why each stays.** The strong woman believes only she can help him; the woman from a violent home thinks she deserves no better; the religious woman holds marriage sacred; the migrant fears deportation; by the time she sees the threat, leaving "feels impossible, or has become too dangerous" (pp. 37–38).

## If you build things

The stalkerware described here is ordinary software: hidden icons, remote wipe, message mirroring, GPS. Design choices such as visible indicators when location is shared, alerts on new device logins and easy audits of who can see what are safety features for people living with an abuser.
