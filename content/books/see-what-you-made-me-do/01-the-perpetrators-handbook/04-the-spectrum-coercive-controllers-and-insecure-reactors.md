## Learning objectives

- Describe the "spectrum of power and control"
- Distinguish coercive controllers (and their two kinds) from insecure reactors
- Explain Stark's "patriarchy in miniature" and why coercive control is held together by fear
- State the book's tests for when relationship conflict becomes domestic abuse
- Explain why "mutual violence" can be a dangerous misreading

## The ideas

**Spectrum.** Perpetrators "exist on a spectrum: from family men who don't even realise they're being abusive, to master manipulators" (p. 18). Some are explicitly tactical — Hill quotes Facebook users advising men to "behave for the first 6–8 months" and isolate a partner — but most "reinvent the techniques of coercive control accidentally and spontaneously". Whether "a cunning sociopath or a 'normal' man afflicted by morbid jealousy", the methods converge (pp. 18–19).

**Coercive controllers** sit at the high end: they micromanage lives, block friends and family, track movements, impose unique rules. Also called "intimate terrorism". Two types: the **calculating** abuser who knowingly degrades his partner to dominate her, and the **paranoid, emotionally dependent** abuser who grows more controlling because he fears she will leave (p. 19). Evan Stark, who popularised the term, says each such household becomes "a patriarchy in miniature, complete with its own web of rules or codes, rituals of deference, modes of enforcement, sanctions and forbidden places" (p. 19). Victims are "frequently deprived of money, food, access to communication or transportation". Because it is rooted in historic power imbalance, in heterosexual couples it is "almost exclusively perpetrated by men". Stark estimates **60 to 80 per cent** of female victims who seek help have experienced coercive control. Its aim is "total domination, rather than simply to win compliance on a particular issue"; it is "a strategic campaign of abuse held together by fear" (p. 20). Example: "Tom" and "Melissa" — isolated on a farm at seventeen, dragged by her hair, force-fed in pregnancy as he played "protector", locked out after her first night out in thirteen years.

**Insecure reactors** sit lower: they "don't completely subordinate their partners", but use emotional or physical violence to win arguments, get privileges they feel entitled to, or "exorcise their shame and frustration" (p. 21). Stark calls this "simple domestic violence" — still dangerous; insecure reactors can kill. Women also perpetrate this kind (Chapter 7). Susan Geraghty describes their "self-righteousness": "I have every right to show my displeasure and punish you". They are also the men most likely to confront their behaviour. Example: "Nick", who threw his wife out of bed and told police "I'm not a criminal", then learned in a behaviour change programme that verbal, emotional and financial abuse count.

**Blurred lines.** Insecure reactors may become coercive controllers; coercive controllers may pose in court as men who merely overreacted; some fit neither (e.g., violence driven by psychosis) (p. 22).

**The tests.** In healthy relationships power is negotiated across money, housework, sex and so on and is "reasonably evenly distributed". In abusive ones, "if one were to animate these couples, the abuser would be depicted as twice the size". Outsiders who see both fighting and call it "mutual" make "a dangerous mistake" (p. 22). The test: "relationship conflict becomes domestic abuse when one partner uses violent, threatening, or coercive behaviour to gain power over the other person" (p. 23). A second test is fear — though fear may build slowly, like heat for the "frog in the boiling pot".
