## Before you start

This companion follows Jess Hill's *See What You Made Me Do: Power, Control and Domestic Abuse* (revised UK edition, Hurst, 2020; first published by Black Inc., 2019). Page numbers are those of that edition. The book contains detailed accounts of physical and sexual violence, including against children, and so do some explanations here. They are included only where the concept cannot be understood without them.

**If any of this is close to home.** In an emergency call 999 (UK), 000 (Australia) or 911 (US). The book's own list (p. 341) gives, for England and Wales, the National Domestic Abuse Helpline, 0808 2000 247 (24 hours); for Wales, Live Fear Free, 0808 80 10 800; for Scotland, the Domestic Abuse and Forced Marriage Helpline, 0800 027 1234; for Northern Ireland, 0808 802 1414; Childline, 0800 1111; and the National LGBT+ Domestic Abuse Helpline, 0800 999 5428. Men in the UK can also call the Men's Advice Line on 0808 801 0327. In Australia, 1800RESPECT is 1800 737 732; in the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233. If someone else may check your phone or browser, remember that this site stores your quiz progress in the browser, and that browsing history can be read.

## The ideas

**Abuse, not violence.** Hill replaced "domestic violence" with "domestic abuse" wherever possible "because in some of the worst abusive relationships, physical violence is rare, minor or barely present" (p. ix). She credits victim advocate Yasmin Khan, whose clients often say "he's never laid a hand on me" before realising they have been abused for years. Calling it "violence" sends the message that it only counts when someone is hit. UK police already used "domestic abuse"; the book's subtitle was changed to match.

**Victim, survivor, perpetrator.** People who have lived through abuse may prefer "victim", "survivor", both or neither; someone can be both at once, and abuse can continue after leaving. "Perpetrator" is "vexed" because it flattens a person, but Hill keeps it: there is no efficient alternative, and seeing the abuser "in two-dimensional terms" can free a survivor to stop trying to help him and begin "deprogramming themselves from the perpetrator's influence" (p. ix).

**Handing power back.** Journalists normally control what is published. Hill let survivors review their stories, suggest revisions and delete details, especially for safety. Some cultural backgrounds are omitted for protection, or because Australian law (section 121 of the Family Law Act) forbids identifying parties to family law proceedings. Alleged abusers in the family-law chapter were not contacted because that was judged unsafe; Hill relied on their affidavits instead.

**The gender frame.** Much of the analysis applies to same-sex relationships, but the book writes largely in a male/female binary "because men's abuse of women is by far the most serious" (p. x). Hill acknowledges the cost of that choice to survivors of same-sex abuse. Chapter 7 deals with women's violence directly.

**Scope.** The UK edition updates Australian material with British statistics, mostly for England and Wales because policing and parts of criminal law are devolved in Scotland and Northern Ireland. Hill says some ideas in the book are "heavily contested", and she says so where it matters. This companion does the same, and adds a later chapter on what has changed since 2020.
