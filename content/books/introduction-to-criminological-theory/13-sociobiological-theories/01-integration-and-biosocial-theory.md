## Learning objectives

## The ideas

**Three ways to develop theory** (p. 264): (1) consider each theory on its own, accepting, modifying or rejecting it on evidence; (2) **theory competition** — "the logical, conceptual, or empirical comparison of two or more theories to determine which offers the best explanation" (Liska et al., 1989); (3) **theoretical integration** — identifying commonalities "to produce a synthesis that is superior to any one individual theory" (Farnsworth). Evidence rarely leads to total rejection, and no theory explains all crime; in practice "most theories have come to at least tacitly... incorporate concepts and notions from their supposed competitors" — even Lombroso, whose fifth edition attributed 80 per cent of his explanation to social factors (p. 265). The glossary: integrated theories incorporate "elements from different approaches in an attempt to provide a stronger explanatory tool than that offered by one individual theory" (p. 505).

**Liska et al.'s types** (p. 265): **conceptual integration**, where concepts from one theory have meanings similar to those in another (Durkheim's anomie and Marx's alienation); **propositional integration**, relating propositions — showing two theories make the same predictions (anomie and conflict theories both predict higher lower-class crime) or placing their variables in a causal sequence (social disorganization explaining variation in attachment).

**The integration debate.** Hirschi (1989): some "'integrated theories are merely oppositional theories in disguise'"; he favours linking theories only if they are effectively the same, and urges oppositional theorists to be "stubborn in its defense. Finally, they should never smile" (p. 266). Akers (1989) agrees that careless integration produces "'theoretical mush'", but insisting on separation "carries... the risk of ignoring similarities and overlap" — producing theories "different in name only".

**Biosocial theory.** Contemporary biologists "propose that physiological characteristics of an individual are only part of the explanation of criminal behaviour; factors in the physical and social environment are also influential" (glossary, p. 503). Biological factors "may increase, but not determine absolutely, the likelihood" of offending (p. 276).

- **Mednick**: "all individuals must learn to control natural urges that drive us towards antisocial and criminal behaviour" (p. 276), learning in the family through punishment-induced fear that becomes a reward when the urge is inhibited. Those with **slow autonomic nervous system (ANS)** responses learn this inhibition poorly; criminals are those with slow ANS responses (p. 269) — a biological factor working through a social learning process.
- **Jeffery (1977)**: individuals are born with characteristics that "not only may predispose them to, but also may actually cause, certain forms of behaviour" (p. 276); his biological criminology is not "neo-Lombrosian" because nature and nurture interact through the physical environment and biochemical feedback. "It is poor people who tend to experience a poor-quality, vitamin-deficient diet and are more likely to be geographically exposed to pollutants", which the body transforms into neurochemical compounds affecting behaviour. Deficiencies in nutrients have been linked to depression, mania, cognitive problems and so violence; researchers link contaminant exposure to aggressive and antisocial behaviour (pp. 269, 276).

## Software lens

Theory competition versus integration is the monolith-versus-composition argument. Competing theories are rival architectures benchmarked against each other; integration composes components that share interfaces (propositional integration chains one theory's output into another's input). Hirschi's warning matches the danger of a "unified platform" that is really one team's design wearing a neutral label; Akers's "theoretical mush" is the big ball of mud. Jeffery's diet-and-pollutant argument is a reminder that environment works through biology: noise, poor air, sleep deprivation and chronic stress in on-call rotations degrade cognition and self-control — environmental causes with physiological pathways.
