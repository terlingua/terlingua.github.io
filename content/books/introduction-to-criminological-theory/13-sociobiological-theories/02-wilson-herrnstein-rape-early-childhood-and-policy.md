## Learning objectives

- State Wilson and Herrnstein's three elements of the causes of crime
- Explain Thornhill and Palmer's evolutionary account of rape and why it is contested
- Explain research on early-onset antisocial behaviour, pre- and perinatal risks and the cumulative risk hypothesis
- State the policy implications of sociobiological theories and their ethical issues

## The ideas

**Wilson and Herrnstein (1985).** Taken up by right realists (Chapter 3), their "definitive account of the underlying causes of crime" has three elements (p. 277):

1. **Constitutional factors** — not necessarily genetic but with some biological origin; crime is disproportionately committed by young men, and maleness and youthfulness likely have a constitutional basis; also intelligence and temperament.
2. **Social learning factors** — the presence or absence of positive and negative reinforcement.
3. **The conscience** — internalized prohibitions whose strength varies with constitution and learning environment.

"The interplay between these factors" produces offending (p. 271). The approach blames ineffective (especially single-parent) families and reinforces the conservative agenda.

**Sociobiological theories of rape.** "Probably the most contentious" (p. 277). Evolutionary psychologists ask whether rape is a by-product of other adaptations or itself an adaptation increasing rapists' descendants. **Thornhill and Palmer (2000)** argue all human behaviour results ultimately from evolutionary adaptation and "the underlying motivations of rapists evolved because they were at one time conducive to reproduction", noting most victims are of childbearing age (p. 273). "Many biologists are strongly opposed to these sociobiological theories of rape" (p. 277): feminists see rape as violence and domination, not sex; victims include children, the elderly and men; and evolutionary stories are hard to falsify.

**Early childhood.** US researchers have focused on "antisocial behaviour that is seen to emerge early in childhood in some individuals and persists into adulthood" (p. 277); such individuals are hard to rehabilitate and likely recidivists (Kazdin; Moffitt, 1993). Raine proposed a biosocial model; risk factors include maternal smoking, alcohol and drugs in pregnancy, prenatal and perinatal complications impairing central nervous system development, and later family adversity (p. 274). The **cumulative risk hypothesis**: "the number of environmental stressors rather than the particular combination of stressors has been associated with child behaviour problems both in the short and long term" (glossary, p. 504).

**Conclusions and policy.** The biological tradition shows "its rejection of its old predestined actor model pretensions and a willingness - however grudgingly - to incorporate notions from the other two models" (p. 276). Biosocial criminologists advocate nurturing strategies: "pre- and post-natal care, the monitoring of infants and young children through the early developmental years, paid maternity leave and comprehensive nutrition" programmes, reducing exposure to toxins, and early identification of at-risk children (p. 276). Ethical issues persist: early identification risks labelling; predictive screening raises the medicalization and discrimination concerns of Chapter 5; and the right realist use of biosocial ideas can justify neglecting structural causes.

## Software lens

The cumulative risk hypothesis is directly useful for reliability engineering: incidents often correlate with the *number* of stressors present (unfamiliar service, night-time, recent reorg, missing runbook, fatigue) more than with any particular combination. Risk scoring for changes and on-call shifts can count stressors rather than hunt for a single cause. The rape debate is a cautionary example for evolutionary "just-so" explanations of behaviour in tech culture ("engineers are naturally competitive"): plausible adaptive stories are easy to tell and hard to test, and can launder harmful behaviour as natural.
