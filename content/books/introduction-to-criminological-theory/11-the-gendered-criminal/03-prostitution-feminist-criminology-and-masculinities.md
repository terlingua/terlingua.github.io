## Learning objectives

- Compare liberal, Marxist and radical feminist views of prostitution
- Explain the debate over whether there is, or should be, a feminist criminology
- Explain Connell's multiple and hegemonic masculinities and Messerschmidt's account of masculinity and crime
- State the policy implications of the gendered criminal

## The ideas

**Prostitution.** "Different feminist perspectives have differing perspectives on prostitution" (p. 243):

- **Liberal feminists** see prostitution as involving choice for adults; many women work independently; criminalization marginalizes the prostitute; prostitution is "a private business transaction" in which a woman is "free to enter into contracts or not" (pp. 235–6). Personal rights predominate; the injustice lies in gender roles and laws.
- **Marxist feminists** identify **poverty** as the crucial motivation, making prostitution a rational choice in conditions of economic oppression (as Bonger argued); subjectively women choose, but objectively they are oppressed workers in an exploitative capitalist scheme.
- **Radical feminists** see prostitution as "the product of patriarchal society", an exploitative relationship in which women are exploited by pimps and clients and men's sexual access to women's bodies is affirmed.

**Is there a feminist criminology?** Carol Smart (1977) considered creating one but later decided it was unnecessary — there were "more important goals" than constructing a sub-discipline; feminism had "transgressed criminology" (pp. 238–40). Others argue for distinct feminist methodologies or a broader feminist social science. Postmodern feminists resist any single account.

**Taking masculinity seriously.** "It was in response to feminist discourse that a growing literature began to emerge that sought to 'take masculinity seriously'" (p. 243). Cain asked what it is about maleness that leads so many men to crime. **Connell** (1987, 1995) recognized **multiple masculinities** — "black as well as white, homosexual or heterosexual, working class or middle class, with all subject to challenge and change over time" (p. 240). A dominant (**hegemonic**) masculinity, based on heterosexual power and authority, exists, but male power "is not absolute but is historically variable and thus a social construction". **Messerschmidt** (1993) applied this to youth crime: offences are patterned by interpretations of masculinity generated by "structures of labour and power" — crime is a resource for 'doing masculinity' when other resources (work, status) are unavailable. Because masculinities are not biologically determined, women may 'do masculinity' and men femininity (Connell: "sometimes masculine conduct or masculine identity goes together with a female body", p. 241); Messerschmidt says it is important to examine masculinities by women and girls and their relation to crime (p. 243).

**Policy implications** (pp. 242–3). Gender shapes the whole criminal justice process; practice should address women's distinct **pathways** into the system, patterns of offending, experiences and responses to programmes. Re-examine gendered effects of policies criminalizing substance abuse (US mandatory minimums for drug offences have devastated women and their children); recognize that gender-neutral correctional procedures ignore many female offenders' histories of abuse; develop gender-responsive programmes.

## Software lens

Messerschmidt's idea that crime is a resource for 'doing masculinity' when legitimate status routes are blocked helps explain some toxic behaviours in tech communities — gatekeeping, harassment and status-seeking through intimidation — as performances of a particular hegemonic masculinity rather than individual quirks. Connell's multiple masculinities warns against a single "brogrammer" stereotype; there are many ways of being a man in engineering, some hegemonic and some marginalized. The prostitution debate models a recurring policy dilemma — choice versus structural constraint versus exploitation — that also appears in debates about gig work and content moderation labour.
