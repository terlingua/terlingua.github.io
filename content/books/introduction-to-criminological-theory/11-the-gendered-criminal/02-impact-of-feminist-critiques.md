## Learning objectives

- Explain feminism's influence on the study of crimes against women and on criminal justice
- Describe the threefold influence of separate studies of women
- Evaluate the 'female emancipation leads to crime' debate (Adler versus Simon)
- Explain why the leniency hypothesis was invalidated
- Describe gender-based theories such as Heidensohn's control theory

## The ideas

**Crimes against women.** Feminism has been "particularly influential... in focusing our attention on the nature of crimes committed against women by men with the two areas most frequently studied being rape and domestic violence" (p. 243). Changes include anonymity for rape complainants, limits on testing their character in court (though Adler, 1982, found few women actually protected) and specialist rape suites, improving reporting (p. 233). Feminists made use of victim surveys, though they did not always accept how the findings were applied.

**Threefold influence** of separate studies of women (p. 243): "(i) the development of different explanations of female criminality and conformity; (ii) a 'gendering' of crime which includes gendered explanations of certain male criminality; and (iii) a recognition of different female 'experiences' of crime, victimization and the criminal justice system".

**Three theoretical contributions** (Downes and Rock, p. 233):

1. **'Female emancipation leads to crime'.** **Freda Adler (1975)** and **Rita Simon (1975)** noted rising, and more aggressive, female crime in the late 1960s and 1970s. Adler: liberation brought women into the illegitimate world too — robbery and violence previously associated with men. **Simon**: emancipation increased *opportunities*, particularly for financial and property crimes, not violence (p. 234). Critics found little evidence: the women offending were rarely the liberated middle class, increases started from low bases, and economic marginalization explained more; "a simple causal relationship between female emancipation and criminality was never likely to exist".
2. **Invalidating the leniency hypothesis.** Pollak's chivalry claim was tested. Farrington and Morris (1983) found apparent leniency reflected women's lesser criminal records; Carlen (1983) found Scottish sheriffs more readily imprisoned women they saw as having 'failed' as mothers. Downes and Rock conclude that, rather than being treated leniently, women are judged by gender expectations — punished for deviance from femininity as well as for the offence (pp. 234–5).
3. **Gender-based theories.** Heidensohn (1985) adapted Hirschi's control theory: there are so few women criminals because of the **formal and informal controls** that constrain them in male-dominated society — at home, in public (fear of male violence), at work (p. 235). Carlen's work on women's imprisonment combined class and gender control.

## Software lens

The emancipation debate is a lesson in causal claims during demographic change: when a group's representation in a field rises (women in engineering leadership, say), changes in that group's rate of misconduct may reflect opportunity (Simon), measurement and base rates, or economic pressure — not a change in "nature". The leniency debate shows why you should control for prior record before claiming bias, and also why apparent leniency can hide harsher treatment for violating role expectations. Heidensohn's control theory has a direct workplace analogue: women in tech often report more informal monitoring of their behaviour, which shapes risk-taking — including willingness to bend rules, ship bold experiments or self-promote.
