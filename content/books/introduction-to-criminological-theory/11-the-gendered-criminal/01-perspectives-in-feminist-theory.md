## Learning objectives

- State what all feminist perspectives share and why feminism is not a unitary theory
- Distinguish liberal, radical, Marxist, socialist, black and postmodern feminism
- Explain the feminist critique of 'malestream' criminology and early explanations of female crime

## The ideas

**Shared starting point.** "Feminists argue that it is men who are the dominant group in society and it is they who make and enforce the rules, which are invariably to the detriment of women" (p. 243). But "Feminism is not a unitary system of thought but a collection of different theoretical perspectives with each explaining the oppression of women in a different way". The book identifies six contemporary variants (pp. 229–31):

- **Liberal feminism**, rooted in Enlightenment individual rights: subordination stems from unequal rights and opportunities; the remedy is legal reform. Its achievements are sex discrimination and equal pay legislation; critics call its gains limited and superficial.
- **Radical feminism** (1970s) centres **patriarchy** — "the 'set of hierarchical relations between men, and solidarity between them, which enables them to control women'" (Hartmann, p. 229). The personal is political: it exposes the private sphere (reproductive freedom, pornography, domestic violence, child abuse) and some advocate separatism. Critics: it treats women's experience as universal, ignoring class and ethnicity.
- **Marxist feminism** locates subordination in capitalist exploitation of women's domestic role; women are a reserve army of labour, drawn in and expelled as capital requires. Criticized for over-economic explanation, but more relevant as more women enter paid work.
- **Socialist feminism** synthesizes radical and Marxist views: both capitalism and patriarchy oppress women (**dual systems theory**).
- **Black feminism** examines domination at personal, cultural and institutional levels in black women's lives, where race, gender and class intersect; its accusations of racism against white middle-class feminism opened "a discourse of difference".
- **Postmodern feminism** rejects universal, grand feminist theory and stresses diversity and the indeterminacy of truth — which some see as a weakness.

**The critique.** Feminist criminologies "challenge the male-centredness of criminology and propose that the main weakness of traditional 'malestream' criminological theory is the failure to understand the important significance of gender and sex roles" (p. 243). Criminology was "the 'most masculine of all the social sciences, a speciality that wore six-shooters on its hips and strutted its machismo'" (Rafter and Heidensohn, p. 231). The main concern: "'intellectual sexism in theories of female crime and the institutional sexism in the juvenile and criminal justice systems'" (Daly and Chesney-Lind, p. 231). Bertrand (1967), Heidensohn (1968) and Klein (1973) first exposed women's neglect and the stereotypes (Chapter 8) in the work that did address them.

## Software lens

Feminist critiques of 'malestream' criminology parallel critiques of products designed around a default user who turns out to be male: crash-test dummies, voice assistants trained on male voices, health apps without menstrual tracking, safety features that ignore domestic-abuse scenarios (location sharing, account recovery by a controlling partner). The feminist variants give different design diagnoses: liberal (equal access and rights — accessibility, equal pay), radical (patriarchal control in private life — tech-facilitated abuse), Marxist/socialist (unpaid and undervalued labour — care work, invisible maintenance), black feminist (intersection of race, gender and class — face recognition error rates highest for darker-skinned women), postmodern (no single "women's experience" to design for).
