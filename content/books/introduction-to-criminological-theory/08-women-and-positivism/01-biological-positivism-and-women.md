## Learning objectives

- Explain why female offending was neglected and how early theory framed it
- Describe Lombroso and Ferrero's account of the female offender
- Explain generative phase (menstrual) theories, PMT and infanticide, and their consequences for women in court
- Explain why 'leniency' based on biology is a double-edged sword

## The ideas

**Neglect.** Explaining female criminality was "for many years a neglected area of criminology not least because of the apparently low levels of female involvement in crime" (p. 181). Early explanations came from the predestined actor model, especially biological positivism.

**Lombroso and Ferrero (1885).** Women were seen as inferior and uniform: "even the female criminal is monotonous and uniform compared with her male companion, just as in general woman is inferior to man", being "atavistically nearer to her origin than the male" (p. 168). Lower female crime was explained by women having *fewer* anomalies, as less differentiated life forms. Women are "inherently passive and conservative because their traditional sex role in the family prepares them for a more sedentary existence" (p. 181). The female offender who does exist is doubly abnormal — more masculine, more cruel — and prostitution was treated as women's characteristic form of crime, attributed to sexual nature rather than economic necessity.

**Generative phases.** The **generative phases of women theory** is "based on biological changes connected to the menstrual cycle" (glossary, p. 505): menstruation supposedly reminds women "they can never become men" and the distress causes offending (p. 169). Dalton's studies linked premenstrual tension (PMT) to offending, but results "appear inconclusive". Yet "the law has accepted the condition as constituting mitigating circumstances in some instances", as Victorian courts did.

**Infanticide.** "If a mother kills her child within its first year as a result of post-natal depression or breastfeeding, she has a partial defence to murder" (glossary, p. 505). Women who kill infants are treated much more leniently than men even when they used more violence; even in manslaughter, women often receive probation (p. 170).

**The double edge.** Men's hormonal imbalances (Chapter 5) do not normally affect conviction or sentence; women can plead them even in homicide. This helps the individual woman, "but for women in general it allows the continuation of the enduring biologically positivist notion that has been in existence since at least Lombroso that they are incapable of controlling themselves" (p. 170). It implies women should be treated for 'sickness' rather than punished, and "removes from women the possibility that they might rationally choose to commit criminal behaviour in the socio-economic circumstances in which they find themselves".

## Software lens

The double-edged leniency point applies to how organizations explain behaviour by group. Attributing a woman engineer's outage or angry message to "emotions" or hormones, while attributing a man's to a bad deploy or a stressful week, looks kind but denies her agency and reinforces the idea that her judgement is biologically unreliable. A useful audit question for performance reviews and incident write-ups: are we explaining the same behaviour in different registers — technical and situational for one group, biological or personal for another?
