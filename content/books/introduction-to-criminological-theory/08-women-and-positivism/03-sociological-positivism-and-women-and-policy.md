## Learning objectives

- Explain why 'general' sociological theories turned out to be theories of male behaviour
- Assess the applicability to women of Merton, Cohen, Miller, Cloward and Ohlin and Sutherland
- Explain sex role theory (Parsons) and its problems
- Explain how changes in women's roles challenge older explanations, and the policy implications

## The ideas

**General theories, male patterns.** Sociological theories "claim to be general explanations of criminal behaviour" but "invariably tend to be explanations of male patterns of behaviour and appear - at least at first sight - to have little or no relevance for explaining female criminality" (p. 176, Leonard, 1983).

**Merton.** Merton never applied anomie to women. If women's goal was marriage and relationships rather than money, anomie theory struggles to explain why so many women without successful marriages do not offend (pp. 177–8). Later he acknowledged affluent people can feel pressure to innovate and that the powerful determine what counts as deviance.

**Cohen and Miller.** Cohen's status frustration was a working-class *boy's* problem; Miller's focal concerns (toughness, smartness, excitement, fate, autonomy) "are predominantly male preoccupations" — if they applied to lower-class life in general, male and female rates would be similar (p. 178).

**Cloward and Ohlin.** Differential opportunity is "more amenable to a consideration of gender": women's lower crime may reflect limited access to *illegitimate* opportunities, and girls have less subcultural support. But the theory ignores societal reaction, fails to explain offending by women who reached their goals, and does not explain why such structural gender differences exist (p. 178).

**Sutherland.** Differential association explains low female crime by associations: "girls are supervised more carefully and behave in accordance with anti-criminal behaviour patterns taught to them with greater care and consistency than in the case of boys" (Sutherland and Cressey, 1960: 115, quoted p. 178). Females "encounter more anti-criminal patterns (within the family, where they are isolated and controlled) over a longer period of time (owing to external supervision)" (p. 182). But more recent research suggests some women "actually learn criminal behaviour within the family".

**Sex role theory.** From the late 1940s, masculinity/femininity theories held that proper socialization follows physical sex — "maleness equals masculinity and femaleness equals femininity" — and women offend when this 'natural' process breaks down (p. 179). Talcott Parsons explained gender differences in offending by different socialization in the nuclear family: boys, with absent working fathers, rebel into 'compulsive masculinity'; girls learn expressive roles from mothers at home. Like their predecessors, these writers portray women as "passive, gentle, dependent, conventional and motherly".

**Changing roles.** Many women have moved from "the happily married and economically dependent housewife (if that role ever existed for many women)" to joint or sole breadwinners "with the pressures of economic success placed on them" (p. 182) — making strain theories newly relevant and raising the 'emancipation' debate of Chapter 11.

**Conclusions and policy.** Positivist explanations of women share a reliance on biological assumptions about women's nature, the personalization and medicalization of female crime, and neglect of economic circumstances. Policy followed: treatment and resocialization into appropriate gender roles rather than punishment or structural change, and intrusive welfare interventions for girls. Feminist critiques (Chapter 11) challenged all of this.

## Software lens

"General" theories built on one population and silently assumed to apply to all are the social-science version of benchmarks run only on one hardware profile or user research run only on one demographic. Sutherland's account is a useful reminder that differential supervision produces differential behaviour: if one group of engineers is reviewed more closely and taught norms more consistently, lower violation rates may reflect supervision, not virtue. The changing-roles point shows how a theory's applicability can change as the population's circumstances change — revalidate models when the underlying conditions shift.
