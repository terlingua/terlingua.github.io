## Learning objectives

- Define critical race theory and distinguish the long tradition from the specific US legal movement
- Explain racial formation theory and how it contrasts with demographic, biological and colourblind approaches
- Explain colourblind racism and 'racial realism', and the role of law in producing colourblind policies
- Explain intersectionality as used by critical race theorists

## The ideas

**Definition.** "Critical race theory refers to an historical and contemporary body of scholarship that aims to interrogate the discourses, ideologies and social structures that produce and maintain conditions of racial injustice" (p. 261). It "analyses how race and racism are foundational elements in historical and contemporary social structures and social experiences" (p. 251). The book distinguishes the long tradition of critical theorizing about race from a specific body of **US legal scholarship** that emerged in the 1970s and 1980s in response to the successes and failures of the Civil Rights movement.

**Racial formation.** A core framework (Omi and Winant, 1994; Winant) analyses race "as an organizing system of knowledge and power that combines both discursive and institutional elements" (p. 254). It contrasts with three alternatives: **demographic** approaches treating race as a quantitative variable; **biological** approaches rooting race in genetics; and **colourblind** approaches that "wish to abandon the study of race concepts altogether". Race is "a concept that signifies and symbolises socio-political conflicts and interests in reference to different types of human bodies".

**Colourblind racism.** Critical race theorists analyse "new forms of colourblind racism that enable and conceal the reproduction of racial inequality without direct reference to social constructions of race" (Bonilla-Silva; Williams). Colourblind racisms "assume that racial inequalities are the outcome of natural, economic and/or cultural differences between racialized groups", and advocate not using constructions of race as necessary to end racism (pp. 254–5). The ideology of individualism helps justify changing forms of domination.

**Law as a site.** Law and legal institutions are "crucial sites for the production of colourblind policies and practices". **Derrick Bell**, "considered to be the intellectual inspiration" for critical race theories of law, showed that segregation had ideological and pragmatic foundations (p. 255). Sociologists (Brown et al., 2003) attacked **'racial realism'**, a variant of colourblind ideology claiming "racism is largely over and the racial inequalities that remain are the result of the natural proclivities and cultural pathologies of black people". Coyle (2010) argues for building critical race analysis into criminology.

**Intersectionality.** Some critical race theorists, "particularly black feminist theorists", developed "an intersectional theoretical approach to analysing the ways in which systems of gender, sexuality and nationalism are implicated in the production and maintenance of racial subordinations" (p. 254; Collins).

**Relevance to criminology.** Critical race theory explains disproportionate policing, prosecution and imprisonment of racialized groups as products of institutions and ideologies, not individual prejudice alone — a theme developed with Wacquant in Chapter 23.

## Software lens

Colourblind racism is the conceptual core of algorithmic fairness debates. A model that excludes race as a feature can still reproduce racial inequality through proxies (postcode, name, device, purchase history) and historical training data — "without direct reference to social constructions of race". "We don't use race, so the system can't be biased" is the colourblind argument; racial formation theory says race is built into the institutional data. Intersectionality appears in evaluation practice: error rates must be checked for intersections (e.g. darker-skinned women), not just each attribute separately, because harms concentrate where systems of disadvantage meet.
