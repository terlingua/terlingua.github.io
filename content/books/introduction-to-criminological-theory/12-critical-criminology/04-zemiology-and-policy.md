## Learning objectives

- Define zemiology and explain why it seeks to move beyond legally defined crime
- Explain the link between power and the visibility of harms
- State the criticisms by Nelken and by left realists, and Henry and Lanier's integrated definition
- Explain the restorative implications of redefining crime as harm, and the policy implications of critical criminology

## The ideas

**Zemiology** is "the study of social harm: for example, sexism, racism, imperialism and economic exploitation" (glossary, p. 509). Its intention is "to significantly extend the legitimate parameters of criminological study away from a limited focus on those injurious acts defined as such by the criminal law" — theft, burglary, criminal damage (p. 256). Many harmful events — poverty, malnutrition, pollution, workplace injury, avoidable disease, state violence — have more damaging consequences than most of what makes up "the 'problem of crime'" (Muncie, 2000). By the 1990s social harm and human rights denial were on the criminological agenda; animal rights, tobacco and food companies' negligence, housing, environmental and economic policy began to be described in a crime discourse (p. 257).

**Power and visibility.** "For zemiologists, a conception of crime without a corresponding conception of power is meaningless. The power to cause certain harmful acts to become visible and define them as 'crime', while maintaining the invisibility of others... lies at the heart of the problem" (p. 257). If the aim is to reveal harms, "the concept of 'crime' has to be rejected as its sole justification and object of enquiry".

**Decriminalising criminology.** Muncie's first step: recognize many damaging events are far more serious than the 'crime problem', while much of what occupies law enforcement — petty theft, shoplifting, recreational drug use, vandalism, brawls — scores low on serious harm. Conversely, the risk of many 'serious' crimes is negligible compared with workplace injury and avoidable disease.

**Criticisms.** Nelken (1994): campaigns to extend the criminal label to new injuries "run the risk of reinforcing the concept of crime even when it is seemingly being attacked". Matthews and Young (1992), left realists: comprehensive expansion of criminality "can only lead to nihilism and cynicism", and removing crime as the object "evaporate[s]" criminology into sociology. **Henry and Lanier (1998)** respond with an integrated definition placing "the legally defined and the legally ignored, the serious and the trivial, and the visible and the invisible" on a continuum of harm (p. 257).

**Restorative implication.** "The redefining of crime as harm opens up the possibility of dealing with pain, suffering and injury as conflicts and troubles deserving negotiation, mediation and arbitration rather than as criminal events deserving guilt, punishment and exclusion" (p. 262). Bianchi (1986) proposed defining crime as tort and dispute, replacing criminal law with **reparative law**; crime control is subordinated to a wider social justice agenda.

**Policy implications of critical criminology** (p. 260): as with earlier conflict and radical theories, "the major priority being a significant restructuring of society to address the inherent inequalities based on socio-economic exclusion, gender and ethnic discrimination"; challenge crimes of the powerful; resist criminalization of the marginalized; move toward harm reduction and reparative responses.

## Software lens

Zemiology is a powerful lens for engineering prioritization. Organizations tend to treat as "incidents" only what violates explicit rules (security breaches, policy violations) while leaving larger harms invisible because nobody has the power or the category to name them: dark patterns that cost users money, accessibility failures that exclude disabled users, burnout from on-call design, algorithmic harms with no legal violation. A harm-based severity scale — Henry and Lanier's continuum — would rank these alongside formal violations. Nelken's warning applies too: turning every harm into a "violation" with a punitive process may entrench the punitive model; mediation and repair often fit better.
