## Learning objectives

- Define critical criminology and explain its origins
- Explain how critical criminologists account for crimes of the powerful
- Describe law evasion by multinationals and Box's account of corporate crime
- Explain state crime as an object of critical criminology

## The ideas

**Definition.** "There are different variations of critical criminology, but, in general, it is a perspective where crime is defined in terms of the concept of oppression" (p. 261). The glossary adds that some groups "are seen to be the most likely to suffer oppressive social relations based upon class division, sexism and racism", and that former radical colleagues who became left realists called critical criminologists **'left idealists'** (p. 504).

**Origins.** Critical criminology examines "the structural relations of power in society" and views "crime in the context of social relations and political economy" (Scraton and Chadwick, p. 247). It attends to both "the crimes of the powerful and those of the less powerful, with crime associated with the broad processes of the political economy that affect both groups but in quite different ways" (p. 261).

**Crimes of the powerful.** For the powerful, pressures arise from "the securing and maintenance of state and corporate interests in the context of global capitalism" (p. 261). Critical criminologists argue "working-class crime is insignificant when compared to the 'crimes of the powerful' that largely go unpunished". Building on Sutherland, they situate business crime in a critique of global capitalism (p. 248):

- Swartz (1975): because capitalism maximizes profits, "its normal functioning produces... deaths and illnesses".
- Mars (1982): a fine line between "'entrepreneurialism and flair'" and "'sharp practice and fraud'"; the overcharging electrician is seen as an entrepreneur, not a thief.
- **Law evasion**: factories in countries without pollution or safety controls (Union Carbide, Bhopal); selling goods banned in developed nations to developing ones (the Dalkon Shield). Recipient nations depend on investment, lack resources to check claims and are more susceptible to bribery; multinationals wealthier than host states can shape legislation and enforcement.
- **Box (1983)**: we must penetrate the "mystification" that corporate crime is not serious and harmless; competitive business environments encourage law-breaking, and "the promotion system means that people who rise to the top are likely to have just those personal characteristics it takes to commit corporate crime", reinforced by success freeing them "from the moral bind of conventional values" (p. 248).

**State crime.** Illegal arms dealing, genocide and torture — prominent after wars in the Balkans, Afghanistan and Iraq and with the Hague War Crimes Tribunal — have entered criminological discourse (p. 257), along with corporate negligence by tobacco and food companies.

## Software lens

Law evasion by jurisdiction-shopping has digital equivalents: hosting data or operations where privacy, labour or content laws are weakest, or shipping dark patterns in markets with little regulatory capacity. Box's argument about promotion systems is a sharp question for engineering leadership: if promotion rewards hitting numbers "by any means", the people who rise may be those most comfortable cutting ethical corners, and success itself can loosen conventional moral restraints. Mars's "fine line" shows up in growth hacking — between clever and deceptive is often a matter of who is describing it.
