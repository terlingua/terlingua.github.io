## Learning objectives

- Explain marginalization and criminalization and how they interact
- Explain how criminalization justifies harsher controls and neutralizes political protest
- Explain the 'legitimation crisis' argument and the critical view of prisons and police
- State the criticisms that led left realists to call critical criminologists 'left idealists'

## The ideas

**Two processes.** "Crime among the less powerful in society is explained by reference to an interaction between marginalization, and the exclusion from access to mainstream institutions, and criminalization, which occurs with the intervention of the state authorities" (p. 261).

- **Marginalization**: "the exclusion from access to mainstream institutions for the poor and less powerful" (glossary, p. 506).
- **Criminalization**: "a process in which the law, agencies of social control and the media come to associate crime with particular groups who are subsequently identified, sought out and targeted as a threat" (glossary, p. 504).

Scraton and Chadwick argue criminalization diverts attention from economic and social conditions at times of acute change that might spark political unrest (p. 249). It "can be used to justify the harsher social control measures that are often taken against economically and politically marginalized groups who have few means of resisting these initiatives" (p. 261). Overtly political protests are criminalized and political terrorists termed 'common criminals' to neutralize the political nature of their actions; Hillyard (1987) notes this helps mobilize support for anti-terrorist measures, since it is easier to act against criminals than to repress a cause that might seem just (p. 249).

**Legitimation crisis.** "The recent growing disparities between rich and poor and the expansion in the number of the latter constitute a legitimation crisis for the capitalist system as a whole. Actual deprivation is again seen as the cause of working-class crime" (p. 261), and the state responds with 'law-and-order' politics that intensify the targeting of the marginalized (p. 250).

**Prisons and police.** "The true function of prisons, it is argued, is not to reform criminals but rather to stigmatize them and cause them to be seen as the enemy in our midst (Foucault, 1980)" (p. 251), maintaining the legitimacy of the social order. The police's real function is "to maintain the social order", controlling industrial disputes and demonstrations, and to "widen the net of social control" (Cohen, 1985), which requires powers relatively free of democratic control (Scraton, 1985).

**The critique.** Many find this view "too simplistic and a denial of the reality that most people experience"; individual criminality "cannot simply be regarded as a construction of the state". "Most criminal behaviour is not targeted against the dominant social order, while the criminal law is not just directed at keeping the less powerful in their place. Indeed, many of the weaker and poorer sections of our society need both the law and its agents... to protect them from criminal elements living in their midst" (p. 251). This recognition produced the **left realists** (Chapter 18), who called their former colleagues 'left idealists'.

## Software lens

Marginalization and criminalization interact on platforms too. Users excluded from mainstream channels (no verified ID, no bank card, unusual devices, non-dominant languages) are more likely to trip automated abuse rules, and once a category of user is associated with abuse in policy, metrics and internal lore, they are sought out and targeted. Net-widening is visible when a monitoring system built for one threat quietly extends to cover ever more behaviour and people. The left realist counterpoint is equally important: the same marginalized users are often the main *victims* of scams and harassment and need effective enforcement — the answer is fair, accountable enforcement, not none.
