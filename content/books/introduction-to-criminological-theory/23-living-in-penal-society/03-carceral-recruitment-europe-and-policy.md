## Learning objectives

- Explain how the hybrid and carceral models illuminate the role of middle-class moral entrepreneurs within ethnic groups
- Explain how UK and European strategies differ from those in the USA
- Explain the claim that the socio-control matrix expands with economic pressure
- State the policy implications and the role of the progressive criminologist

## The ideas

**Carceral recruitment and authority.** The historical schema of peculiar institutions "should not... be seen as an inescapable" story of white domination alone: within ethnic groups, a new middle class "had an often expert interest as 'moral entrepreneurs' (Becker, 1963) in the control and indeed suppression of the unskilled and dispossessed elements of their own ethnic group", which the carceral surveillance and left realist hybrid models explain (p. 473). Control is exercised by many hands, including members of the controlled group.

**The UK and Europe.** "Many black people in the UK are the targets of tutelage and discipline strategies as part of a socially excluded underclass that incorporates people from all ethnic groups but that, at the same time, is invidious and pervasive" (p. 480). The European system "is as invidious and pervasive in its own way as the system the other side of the Atlantic. It is just different" (p. 478): more tutelage and welfare-based discipline, less mass incarceration — economic policies "devastating for certain, usually poorer, segments of the population, who then need to be controlled for their individual moral failings" (p. 477).

**Expansion.** "It is becoming increasingly apparent that the socio-control matrix of the carceral society continues to incrementally expand in close parallel with increasingly insurmountable economic pressures" (p. 480). "More and more groups are being absorbed into the net of surveillance and tutelage" (p. 478).

**Policy implications** (pp. 479–80). Wacquant (2009b): the rise of the penal state "is a response to rising social insecurity, not criminal insecurity"; restrictive workfare and expansive prisonfare form "a single organizational apparatus to discipline the precarious fractions of the post-industrial working class"; an all-inclusive carceral system "is not a deviation from, but a constituent component of, the neoliberal Leviathan state". The prison should be understood "not... as a technical implement for law enforcement but as a core political capacity, whose selective and aggressive deployment in the lower regions of social space violates the ideals of democratic citizenship". Hence "progressive criminologists opposed to these penological trends should challenge policies that help to reinforce the disciplinary-control-matrix, particularly when these are being promoted by moral entrepreneurs who have little or no understanding of how their humble policy or strategy contributes to the overall surveillance schema" (p. 479). Experts — including criminologists — carry responsibility for their part.

## Software lens

The progressive criminologist's role translates directly to engineers: challenge features that reinforce the control matrix, especially when championed by well-meaning "moral entrepreneurs" who do not see how their small feature (a new tracking signal, a data-sharing integration, a stricter verification step) fits into a larger surveillance schema. The European point — "as invidious and pervasive in its own way... just different" — warns against assuming softer, welfare-like controls (nudges, conditional access, "wellness" monitoring) are automatically benign compared with hard bans. And the expansion-under-pressure claim is observable: monitoring and control features multiply during downturns, layoffs and cost-cutting.
