## Learning objectives

- Explain Wacquant's 'government of social insecurity' and the upsizing of the penal state
- Explain why Wacquant says we must break out of the 'crime and punishment' paradigm
- Describe the four 'peculiar institutions' and the prison–ghetto relationship
- Explain workfare and prisonfare as two sides of the same coin and their export to Europe

## The ideas

**Government of social insecurity.** "Wacquant (2009a) identifies a new 'government of social insecurity', which is targeted at shaping the conduct of the men and women caught up in the turbulence of economic deregulation in advanced societies" (p. 480). The expanded use of imprisonment is part of an **'upsizing'** of the penal sector alongside **'downsizing'** of social welfare, aiming "to enforce a regime of flexible and casual wage labour as a norm for unskilled segments of the post-industrial working class" (p. 472). This government of poverty "weds the 'invisible hand' of a deregulated labour market to the 'iron fist' of an omnipresent punitive apparatus" — anchored not by a 'prison industrial complex' but by **gendered institutions**: men handled by the penal wing, women and children by a revamped welfare-workfare system.

**Beyond crime and punishment.** "We need to break out of the narrow 'crime and punishment' paradigm and examine the broader role of the penal system as an instrument for managing dispossessed and dishonoured groups" (p. 480), and take a longer historical view of ethno-racial domination in the USA (p. 471).

**Race.** "Crucial to this disciplinary-tutelage agenda in the USA has been the need to control an increasingly economically excluded but enduringly problematic and potentially dangerous black population" (p. 480). Welfare dismantling was accelerated by "a cultural and political conflation of blackness and undeservingness" (Gilens), and the "'great confinement' of the rejects of market society - the poor, mentally ill, homeless, jobless and useless" was presented as a 'crackdown' on a pariah group (p. 472).

**Four peculiar institutions.** "The task of defining, confining and controlling African-Americans in the USA has been successively administered by four 'peculiar institutions': (i) slavery; (ii) the Jim Crow system; (iii) the urban ghetto; and latterly (iv) the organizational complex formed by the remnants of the ghetto and the expanding carceral system" (p. 472). "The prison today resembles the ghetto for the simple reason that an overwhelming majority of its occupants originate from the racialized core of the major cities and return there upon release" (p. 480). In New York, segregated districts supply around 25,000 inmates a year while 23,000 ex-convicts return to them (p. 474).

**Workfare and prisonfare.** "Workfare and prisonfare are simply two sides of the same coin" (p. 480): both are punishments; they are "the areas where the neoliberal state can still assert its authority once depleted of its economic and social policy functions" and fulfil economic and symbolic functions as the state fights a crisis of legitimacy (p. 476). The War on Drugs blocks the one exit from poverty — the illegal economy — available to those excluded from wage labour (p. 477). "This double regulation of poverty - through workfare and prisonfare - has been exported to Europe" via Thatcherism, Kohl and Chirac, and even left-of-centre parties (p. 477).

## Software lens

Wacquant's argument that penal systems manage "dispossessed and dishonoured groups" rather than simply responding to crime is a lens for platform governance: risk scoring, account restrictions and "trust" programmes can function to manage the precarious — gig workers, sellers in poorer markets, users without stable identity documents — through combined surveillance and conditional access (a digital workfare/prisonfare). The gendered and racialized division of institutions he describes reminds builders to examine *which* populations each enforcement subsystem actually handles. And the prison–ghetto loop has a data analogue: users flagged and restricted return to the same low-trust cohorts, generating more flags — a feedback loop that looks like evidence of risk.
