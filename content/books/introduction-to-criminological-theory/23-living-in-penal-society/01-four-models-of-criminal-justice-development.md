## Learning objectives

- Explain why the question "in whose interests does the criminal justice system operate?" matters
- Describe the orthodox social progress model in its purist and revisionist (Rothman) versions
- Describe the radical conflict model (Rusche and Kirchheimer, Melossi and Pavarini, Ignatieff)
- Describe the carceral surveillance society model (Foucault, Cohen) and the left realist hybrid model

## The ideas

The criminal justice system is "an important structural restriction" on offenders' lives, so "in whose interests this operates is extremely important" (p. 457). Hopkins Burke (2012, 2013) proposes four models.

**1. The orthodox social progress model** — "the standard non-critical explanation that considers the development of law and the criminal justice system to be predominantly non-contentious. These institutions are thus seen to operate neutrally in the interests of all" (pp. 457–8). Rooted in Enlightenment and social evolutionist ideas of progress (Comte, Spencer), it sees reform as driven by "ideals, visions, theories, intentions and advances in knowledge"; "All change is 'reform'"; all reform is motivated by "benevolence, altruism, philanthropy and humanitarianism" (Cohen, 1985). The 1829 professional police served everyone's need for protection; childhood and youth controls reflected genuine humanitarian concern. Failures are fixed by more resources, better staff and better science. Cohen calls it "the most important model" because it is mainstream opinion. **Revisionist version (Rothman)**: the US asylum after the War of Independence was a utopian experiment that degenerated into custody but survived because of **'convenience'** — it kept a dangerous population off the streets — and benevolent rhetoric; "operational needs ensured survival while benevolent rhetoric buttressed a long discredited system" (Cohen, p. 460). Still liberal: things can be improved by learning from history.

**2. The radical conflict model.** Society is conflict-ridden (Weber; Marx). The criminal justice system was not a failure: it "served more than adequately the requirements of the emerging capitalist order for the continued repression of recalcitrant members of the working class" while persuading everyone that changes were fair (p. 461). Police were "'domestic missionaries'" (Storch); zero tolerance continues that tradition. **Rusche and Kirchheimer (1939)**: forms of punishment correspond to the mode of production — when labour is scarce, punishment makes the unwilling work; with a surplus 'reserve army', harsh punishment controls the workless (p. 462). **Melossi and Pavarini (1981)**: the prison's function is **discipline** — creating "a socially safe proletarian who has learned to accept being propertyless". **Ignatieff (1978)** offers a softer version: reformers' complex beliefs — religion, guilt, political self-interest — produced the penitentiary as a response to a specific class problem in the crisis years of industrialization; the system was "remarkably successful" (p. 463).

**3. The carceral surveillance society model.** Philanthropists were often sincere (Becker's moral entrepreneurs), but outcomes reflect diffuse power. From Foucault, "power is not simply the privilege of an all-powerful state"; it is pervasive, and "power and knowledge are inseparable" — criminology, psychiatry and philanthropy are tied to its exercise in a "power/knowledge spiral" (p. 464). Semi-autonomous realms (communities, occupations, families) have surveillance without the state. Agents at micro, mezzo and macro levels "are not always aware of their contribution to the 'grand design'". Cohen: the "'great incarcerations'" of the nineteenth century — thieves into prisons, lunatics into asylums, conscripts into barracks, workers into factories, children into school — formed a grand design of discipline and surveillance (p. 465).

**4. The left realist hybrid model** "provides a synthesis of the orthodox social progress, radical conflict and carceral surveillance society models but with the added recognition of our interest and collusion in the creation of the increasingly pervasive socio-control matrix of the carceral society. The radical conflict perspective nevertheless demands prominence because we are all subject to the requirements and demands of the economy" (p. 480). Since the start of modernity, the public has had a material interest in order and has supported controls.

## Software lens

The four models map onto how engineers explain the growth of monitoring and control systems at work. **Social progress**: every new control (EDR on laptops, DLP, productivity analytics) is a neutral improvement for everyone. **Revisionist**: controls fail at their stated aims but persist out of convenience and good intentions. **Radical conflict**: they serve the employer's interest in a disciplined workforce. **Carceral surveillance**: power is diffuse — security teams, HR, vendors and well-meaning engineers each build a piece of a monitoring apparatus nobody designed as a whole. **Left realist hybrid**: all of the above, plus *our* collusion — we want protection from attackers and bad colleagues, so we help build and accept the matrix.
