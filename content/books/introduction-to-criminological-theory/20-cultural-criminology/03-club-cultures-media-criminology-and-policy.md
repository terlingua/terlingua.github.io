## Learning objectives

- Explain Carrington and Wilson's account of global club cultures and cultural borrowing
- Define media criminology and explain mediatisation
- Explain media constructions of crime, moral panics and Surette's social construction frames
- State the policy implications of cultural criminology (Hayward and Young)

## The ideas

**Club cultures.** "Ben Carrington and Brian Wilson (2002) observe that, like all youth cultures, and especially those formed through associations with music cultures, the evolution of 'club cultures' around the world can be attributed, in part, to the ongoing global processes of cultural borrowing" (p. 408). Rave moved into licensed nightclubs — "that is how ravers, in effect, became clubbers" (p. 395). Global music reproduction, migration, media and tourism create "a new epoch of global culture contact" (Chambers), with a hazy relation between global club culture and local scenes; "post-rave tourists" make local cultures transient and diverse (pp. 395–6). Drug use, licensing and policing of these cultures show the shifting boundaries between leisure, transgression and criminalization.

**Media criminology.** Greer defines it as "'the complex and constantly shifting intersections between crime, criminalization and control, on the one hand, and media, mediatisation and representation on the other'" (2010: 5, p. 408). In a media-saturated world "crime and criminal justice are fundamentally mediatised phenomena" (Greer, 2010: 8, p. 399).

**Constructing the crime problem.** Media coverage emphasizes violent and street crime, often neglecting corporate harm and "the systemic negative consequences for the well-being of the rest of society" (p. 400). Moral panic is a "situation in which public fears and state interventions greatly exceed the objective threat posed to society" (p. 400). Surette (2007) identifies four stages in the social construction of crime and five contemporary crime and justice frames — fully developed templates that let claims-makers succeed; the media's version can become "the socially constructed reality" (p. 402). Surette is ambivalent about media technologies: they can support both due process and crime control, and surveillance programmes show deterrence effects but their ability to work "without displacement remains unproven" (p. 405).

**Policy implications.** Hayward and Young (2012) answer the charge that cultural criminology has little policy potential: it is "an appreciative approach that totally eschews 'correctionalism'", though this "does not inevitably mean to romanticize the offender, nor does it necessitate a non-intervention approach" (p. 407). The emphasis is on cultural meanings to those doing the crime. Young (2011) criticizes the neglect of culture in US positivist orthodoxy and its "toxic data"; the issue "becomes not whether cultural criminology is capable of providing legitimate policy interventions but rather whether or not much of the current funding directed at positivistic intervention is simply a waste of money".

## Software lens

Online communities are textbook cases of global cultural borrowing: memes, slang and subcultural styles move instantly across platforms and borders, and local scenes are transient mixes of global influences — which complicates moderation policies written for one cultural context. Media criminology applies to security reporting: breaches are framed through templates (the lone genius hacker, the foreign state actor, the careless employee), which shape what organizations fear and fund. Surette's caution about surveillance — deterrence effects, but displacement unproven — is exactly the right scepticism for monitoring proposals. Hayward and Young's challenge is worth borrowing: before asking whether qualitative, meaning-focused user research can drive policy, ask whether expensive metrics programmes built on "toxic data" actually do.
