## Learning objectives

- Define cultural criminology and explain its debt to labelling theory
- Describe its methods and data sources
- Explain Katz's seductions of crime, including sneaky thrills, the badass and the stick-up man
- Explain Presdee's crime as carnival and O'Malley and Mugford's phenomenology of pleasure

## The ideas

**Focus.** "Cultural criminology seeks to explain crime and criminal behaviour and its control in terms of culture, with the focus on the continuous generation of meaning around interaction where rules are created and broken" (p. 407). The glossary: "crime and the various agencies and institutions of crime control are seen to be cultural and creative constructs and these should be understood in terms of the phenomenological meanings they carry" (p. 504). It "studies the way that some cultures have come to be designated as deviant and thus has been heavily influenced by labelling theories" (p. 407). It "uses everyday existences, life histories, music, dance and performance as databases to discover how and why certain cultural forms become criminalized" (p. 390). Ferrell and Sanders (1995): the aim is "to expand and enliven criminology and to push back the boundaries of accepted criminological discourse".

**The seductions of crime.** Katz (1988) writes of crime "in which disorder becomes in itself a 'delight' to be sought after and savoured and where the causes of crime are constructed by the offenders themselves in ways that are compellingly seductive" (p. 407; glossary p. 508). Crime is examined in its *foreground* — the lived, sensual experience of the act — rather than in background factors.

- **Sneaky thrills** (shoplifting, vandalism): the object exerts a seductive power "like a 'romantic encounter'"; practical conditions involve "being privately deviant in public places"; emotional conditions involve the thrill; metaphors of game, religion, sex and charisma make the seduction irresistible: "it is not the taste for pizza that makes the crime happen but the crime that makes the pizza taste good" (Katz, quoted p. 391).
- **Badasses** (gang violence): projecting symbols of impenetrability and toughness — the "accidental bump", hogging the pavement.
- **Stick-up men** (armed robbery): a prototypical "breeding ground" for crime; seeking "continuous action", even embracing a death wish, with a sense of superior perceptual competence in exploiting weaknesses in victims or architecture.
- **Persistent offenders** ('heavies') pursue continuous action — crime, drugs, sex, gambling — to exhaustion; their problem is "the transcendence of chaos", and arrest can come almost as a relief (p. 391).
- Katz links crime's attractions to being male and being black — establishing gendered, ethnic identity (p. 392).

**The carnival of crime.** O'Malley and Mugford (1994) call for "a new phenomenology of pleasure" recognizing crime as transgression and transcendence of the mundane. **Presdee (2000)**: crime as carnival, "a site where the pleasure of playing at the boundaries is clearly catered for. Thus, festive excess, transgression, the mocking of the powerful, irrational behaviour and so on are all temporarily legitimated in the moment of carnival" (p. 392). "Breaking rules is a source of joy, of humour, of celebration". He finds carnival fragments in sado-masochism, raving, joyriding, **computer hacking**, recreational drug use, reclaim the streets parties, gang rituals and extreme sports. Thornton (1995) shows a shifting exchange between acceptability and illegality, subcultural authenticity and media manufacture.

## Software lens

Cultural criminology is unusually direct about hacking: Presdee lists computer hacking among the carnivalesque transgressions in which "breaking rules is a source of joy, of humour, of celebration" and "the mocking of the powerful" is temporarily legitimated. Katz's foreground analysis fits early-career offensive hacking and griefing — the thrill, the sense of superior perception of weaknesses (the stick-up man's "competence"), the identity it confers — far better than cost-benefit models. It also suggests channels: CTFs, bug bounties and red teams legitimate the carnival and the craft. For defenders, understanding the meaning of an act to the actor (status, play, protest) predicts behaviour better than assuming profit motives.
