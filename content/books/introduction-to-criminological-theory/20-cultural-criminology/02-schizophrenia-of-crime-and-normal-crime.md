## Learning objectives

- Define Hopkins Burke's 'schizophrenia of crime' and explain its two sides
- Explain the claim that criminality is widespread "to the virtual point of universality"
- Explain Presdee's 'second lives'
- Explain Garland's observation that crime has become normal, and the blurring of boundaries between organized crime, corporate crime and legitimate business

## The ideas

**The schizophrenia of crime.** Hopkins Burke (2007) uses the term "to refer to the apparently contradictory attitude to criminal behaviour where, on the one hand, there is a widespread public demand for a rigorous intervention against criminality, while, on the other hand, criminality is widespread to the virtual point of universality" (glossary, p. 508). It is a "duality of attitude to criminal behaviour that has become endemic in contemporary societies characterized by the postmodern condition" (p. 407). (The book uses 'schizophrenia' in its colloquial sense of a split attitude.)

**Near-universal criminality.** "Many people have consequently committed criminal offences at some stage in their life and a great many continue to do so" (p. 393). White-collar, corporate and business crime is extremely widespread; everyday offences include tax fiddles, illegal downloading, recreational drug use and driving beyond the speed limit ("virtually compulsory through peer group pressure on motorways"). Hence "the notion of the virtual universality of criminality is not as implausible as it may at first seem" (p. 393).

**Second lives.** Hopkins Burke draws on Presdee's notion of **'second lives'**, "where the usually law-abiding and pillars of straight society enjoy an alternative part-time existence" of transgression (p. 393) — the respectable person who demands law and order by day and indulges in illicit pleasures by night or online.

**Crime as normal.** "David Garland (1996) has observed that, as crime has come to be more frequent, it has ceased to be an exceptional or pathological event that surprises us when it occurs, but has become instead a standard, normal, background feature of our lives" (p. 407). Crime rates have fallen but from "unprecedented high levels" and remain historically high (pp. 393–4). This echoes Durkheim's 'crime is normal' but in a new key: crime is now an everyday risk to be managed (Chapter 21).

**Blurring boundaries.** "This increasing blurring of boundaries has become nowhere more apparent than in the realms of organized crime, corporate crime and legitimate business" (p. 407). Legitimate firms launder money, evade regulation and engage in fraud; organized crime invests in legitimate businesses; the categories interpenetrate.

## Software lens

The schizophrenia of crime is visible in tech culture: loud demands for harsh penalties against "hackers" and pirates coexist with near-universal minor rule-breaking — password sharing, unlicensed software, ToS violations, scraping, ignoring data-retention rules. Recognizing this duality helps write realistic policies: if a rule is violated by almost everyone, enforcing it selectively becomes a tool for targeting the unpopular. The blurred boundary between legitimate and criminal business is concrete in the digital economy: bulletproof hosting, data brokers selling scraped data, ad-tech fraud, and "grey-market" tools used both by penetration testers and criminals.
