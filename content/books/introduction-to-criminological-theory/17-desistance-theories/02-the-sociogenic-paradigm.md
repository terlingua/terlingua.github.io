## Learning objectives

- Explain the sociogenic paradigm and how it differs from the ontogenetic
- Describe Trasler's and Sampson and Laub's turning points and informal social control
- Explain the roles of employment, marriage and peers
- State the weaknesses of the sociogenic paradigm

## The ideas

**Sociogenic explanations** attribute desistance to changes in a person's *social* circumstances and relationships rather than to internal maturation. As adult roles become available, "the majority of young people easily move away from their weak affiliation with offending" (p. 329).

**Turning points.** Trasler (1979, 1980) and Sampson and Laub (1993) describe **turning points** that redirect life paths away from delinquency. Trasler: "'as they grow older, most young men gain access to other sources of achievement and social satisfaction - a job, a girlfriend, a wife, a home and eventually children'" (p. 329), which make offending both less attractive and riskier.

**Sampson and Laub's age-graded theory of informal social control** (1993; reanalysing the Gluecks' data) extends Hirschi's social bond across the life course: adult social bonds — especially stable **employment** and a cohesive **marriage** — provide informal social control, structured routines, and investments (social capital) that would be jeopardized by offending. It is not the event itself (getting married, getting a job) but the *quality* and strength of the bond that matters. These bonds can redirect even those with troubled childhoods — so change in adulthood is possible, against the stability claims of Gottfredson and Hirschi.

**Peers and routines.** Warr argues that desistance follows from changes in peer associations: marriage reduces time spent with delinquent friends. Routine activities change too (less time in criminogenic settings).

**Weaknesses** (pp. 330–1, and summary). The sociogenic paradigm cannot easily explain why the same events (a job, a marriage) are turning points for some but not others; causal direction is unclear (people already inclined to desist may be the ones who marry and keep jobs — selection); it treats people as passive recipients of social forces; and it neglects subjective meaning — how events are perceived and interpreted. These weaknesses motivate the narrative approach.

## Software lens

Sampson and Laub's emphasis on the *quality* of bonds rather than events is a useful correction to engagement programmes. A new title or a team move (the "event") does not stabilize an engineer who was drifting toward risky behaviour; a supportive manager, meaningful ownership and a team that would be let down (the bond) can. Turning points exist in careers too: becoming a mentor, owning a critical system, or joining a team with strong norms. The selection critique applies to retrospective analyses: engineers who "settled down" after taking ownership roles may have been chosen for those roles because they were already settling down.
