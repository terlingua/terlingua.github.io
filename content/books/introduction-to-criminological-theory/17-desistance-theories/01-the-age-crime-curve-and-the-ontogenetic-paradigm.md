## Learning objectives

- Explain why desistance is "as widely acknowledged and as poorly understood" as any phenomenon in criminology
- Describe the age-crime curve and the typical age range of desistance
- Explain the ontogenetic paradigm from Quetelet to the Gluecks' maturational reform and Gove
- State why age alone cannot explain desistance

## The ideas

**The puzzle.** Maruna (1997): "few phenomena in criminology have been as widely acknowledged and as poorly understood as why it is people desist from doing crime" (p. 341). Shover: "although it is conventional wisdom that most offenders eventually desist... criminology textbooks have little or nothing to say about this process" (p. 326). Early studies were few and mostly atheoretical.

**The age-crime curve.** "For most individuals, participation in 'street crimes' generally begins in the early teenage years, peaks in late adolescence or young adulthood, and ends before the person reaches 30 or 40 years of age" (p. 342). "At some stage in their life course, usually between 18 and 35 years of age, even serious offenders tend to cease criminal behaviour". Leading explanations fall into a dichotomy of **ontogenetic** and **sociogenic** paradigms (Maruna, 1997).

**The ontogenetic paradigm** explains desistance by maturation — an age-graded, natural process within the individual.

- **Quetelet (1833)**: the predilection for crime diminishes with age "'due to the enfeeblement of physical vitality and the passions'" (p. 327).
- **Sheldon and Eleanor Glueck (1940)**: **maturational reform** — "intrinsic criminality naturally declines after the age of 25"; with "'sheer passage of time'" delinquents "'grow out'" and "'burn out'". They urged researchers to "'dissect maturation into its components'", but Shover (1985) found explanation had not progressed much beyond them; maturational reform "continued to be the most influential theory of desistance" (p. 327).
- **Gottfredson and Hirschi (1990)** argue age effects are invariant and need no explanation — crime declines with age everywhere for everyone.
- **Developmental stage theories** treat desistance as "a natural 'stage' in personality development, parallel to the questioning of roles and identity that supposedly takes place for all adults at mid-life" (p. 342).
- **Gove (1985)** appealed to physiological changes with ageing (strength, energy, testosterone).

**Why age is not an explanation.** Maruna: few criminologists would accept "'criminal behaviour peaks at age seventeen, therefore crime is caused by turning seventeen'" (1997: 72, p. 328). Age "indexes a range of different variables, including biological changes, social" transitions, life events and cognitive development (Sampson and Laub, 1992) — it is a proxy, not a mechanism. Analogy: years of schooling outweigh chronological age in explaining cognitive test performance. Physiological accounts fail too: testosterone falls with age, but the age–testosterone curve does not match the age–crime curve. And the ontogenetic paradigm cannot explain why some desist early, some late, some never.

## Software lens

"Engineers stop making that mistake after a few years" is the ontogenetic paradigm of software errors — true as a correlation, useless as a mechanism. Experience indexes many things: accumulated context, better mentors, more stable on-call assignments, promotions that remove people from risky tasks, and identity change ("I'm the person who writes the runbooks"). Treating tenure itself as the cause leads to waiting rather than designing for faster learning. Maruna's quip has a direct equivalent: "incident rates peak in month three of tenure, therefore incidents are caused by month three" — the analysis should ask what changes between month three and month twelve.
