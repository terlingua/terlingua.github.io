## Learning objectives

- Explain the psychometric consensus on the stability of 'criminal personality'
- Explain narrative theory and McAdams's three domains of personality
- Contrast Maruna's condemnation and redemption scripts
- Explain the role of agency — the offender as their own change agent
- List the eight policy themes for supporting desistance

## The ideas

**Personality traits.** "The consensus reached in psychometric research is that 'criminality' - or a 'criminal personality' - is generally constant throughout the life course, even when ex-offenders desist from criminal behaviour" (p. 342). Traits alone therefore cannot explain change.

**Narrative identity.** "In order to achieve a contingent, temporally structured and contextualized understanding of human behaviour, one needs to look at the self-narratives or storied self-concepts of individuals" (p. 342; Bruner, Giddens, McAdams). Understanding a person means understanding their story. **McAdams (1994)**: behaviour is guided by three domains: (i) psychological **traits**; (ii) personal **strategies** (goals, coping); (iii) **identity narratives** or self-stories (p. 332). Traits are relatively stable, but narratives change. "Unlike developmental stage theorists, narrative theorists generally argue that identity is a life-long project, which individuals continuously restructure in light of new experiences and information (McAdams, 1993)" (p. 342).

**Narratives of desistance.** Maruna's Liverpool Desistance Study (2001) found two recurring themes that differed between persisters and desisters (p. 339):

- **Condemnation script** (persisters): they saw themselves as victims of circumstances beyond their control — poor education, poor family background, lack of money — and as condemned to a life they could not escape; little sense of agency.
- **Redemption script** (desisters): a strong sense of self-belief and control over present and future; they too attributed past offending to circumstances but took responsibility for the future, reinterpreting the past as a necessary prelude to a better self — often expressed through **generativity**, helping others (e.g. volunteering, mentoring, counselling).

**Agency.** "When an individual desists from crime, they act as their own change agent (Adams, 1997) and are not merely the product of outside forces of social control or personality traits" (p. 342). Narrative theory "explicitly tries to account for individual choices that appear to be far from 'rational', emphasising that human subjects react differently to stimuli based on how events and constructs are perceived and interpreted".

**Policy: eight themes** (p. 341):

1. Desistance is difficult, with lapses and relapses — supervision should be realistic and handle setbacks constructively; it takes time.
2. It is individualized and subjective — "One-size-fits-all interventions will not work".
3. Building **motivation and hope** is a key task.
4. Desistance is understood within **relationships** — with workers and with those who matter to offenders.
5. Offenders have **strengths and resources**, personal and social, to develop.
6. Desistance involves discovering **self-efficacy** — work "with offenders not on them".
7. **Human capital** (skills) is not enough; build **social capital** — opportunities to practise new identities such as 'worker' or 'father'.
8. The **language of practice** should recognize positive potential and "avoid identifying people with the behaviours we want them to leave behind".

## Software lens

The redemption script has a clear workplace analogue: engineers who recover well from a serious mistake often reframe it as the experience that made them better ("that outage is why I built our chaos testing") and become generative — mentoring, writing runbooks, running postmortem training. The condemnation script — "I was set up to fail; nothing I do matters" — predicts disengagement. Managers can't impose a narrative, but the eight policy themes translate almost word for word: expect relapses, individualize support, build hope, use relationships, build on strengths, work *with* the person, create opportunities to practise a new identity (ownership of a reliability project), and stop referring to them by the incident ("the person who dropped the database").
