## Learning objectives

- Contrast the old penology (penal welfarism) with the new penology (risk management)
- Define actuarial justice and explain net-widening of surveillance (the DNA database example)
- Explain Beck's risk society and the 'boomerang effect'
- Explain the debate over the 'penal turn', penal modernism and postmodernism

## The ideas

**Old and new penology.** Feeley and Simon (1994) "propose that these changes are part of a paradigm shift from the 'old penology' (penal welfarism) to the 'new penology' (risk management)" (p. 434). The old penology identified the individual criminal to ascribe guilt and blame and impose punishment and treatment. The new penology is "'concerned with techniques for identifying, classifying and managing groups assorted by levels of dangerousness' based not on individualized suspicion, but on the probability that an individual may be an offender" (p. 412; glossary p. 506).

**Actuarial justice.** "Interventions are increasingly based on risk assessment, rather than on the identification of specific criminal behaviour" (glossary, p. 502). This brings "preventive detention, offender profiling and mass surveillance... and indeed - when that fails - mass incarceration" (p. 412).

**Net-widening.** McCahill and Norris: thirty years of surveillance technologies to manage dangerous groups — city-centre CCTV, employee drug testing, blanket DNA testing of communities. Justified by monitoring serious 'risk' groups, "once introduced, the concept of dangerousness is broadened to include a much wider range of offenders and suspects" (p. 412). The UK National DNA Database began as a forensic tool for serious crimes like murder and rape, but a 1994 amendment allowed samples without consent "from any person convicted or suspected of a recordable offence".

**Risk society.** For some, these trends indicate "a broader transition from an industrial society towards a risk society (Beck, 1992)" (p. 434) — "a social formation that is organized in order to respond to risks" (glossary, p. 508). Social positions are increasingly defined by exposure to risk (achieved through aversion strategies) rather than by wealth accumulation. Beck's **'boomerang effect'**: producers of widespread risks are themselves exposed to them — the wealthy whose capital creates pollution still breathe the air (p. 413).

**The penal turn and its critics.** Some see a **punitive turn**: mass incarceration, ostentatious punishment (Pratt), the rise of risk management. But "the risk society thesis is far from universally accepted by academics with some recognizing the survival of significant aspects of penal welfarism and some academics rejecting the whole idea of a punitive turn and proposing that recent developments in social control are largely benevolent or benign" (p. 434). Garland's **penal modernism** persists alongside new practices; Hallsworth and Lea note the relation between ostentatious punishment and actuarial risk management "certainly needs further clarification" (p. 417).

**Risk as policy.** Kemshall: "'[t]he identification, assessment, prevention and management of risk have become big business in crime policy, practice and research'" (2003: 1, p. 432). But a risk-based approach is not necessarily popularly demanded; politicians invoke "'mass risk consciousness'" (O'Malley) to justify policy, especially on serious sexual and violent offenders. Ryan argues public voices should be heard "in their complexity and diversity" (p. 433).

## Software lens

The new penology is how modern fraud, abuse and insider-risk systems work: they classify and manage *groups by probability* (risk scores) rather than responding to specific acts by specific people. Actuarial justice's characteristic tools — preventive restriction (holds and limits before any wrongdoing), profiling and mass surveillance — are the platform equivalents of account holds, behavioural profiling and blanket logging. The DNA database story is the canonical **scope creep** warning: a dataset justified for the most serious cases expands, by policy amendment, to cover everyone "suspected" of anything. Design data-retention and access policies as if that amendment will be proposed — because it usually is. Beck's boomerang effect fits systemic tech risks: the companies that create supply-chain or AI risks are themselves exposed to them.
