## Learning objectives

- Define globalization and explain why risk and modernity theorists see global transformations
- List the critical global crimes and explain the power of global crime groups
- Explain Castells's 'black holes of informational capitalism'
- Explain why cybercrime is called the phenomenon of the early twenty-first century
- Explain the unintended consequences of globalizing policies

## The ideas

**Globalization** is "the increasing degree of integration among societies that plays a crucial role in most types of social change" (glossary, p. 505). "Theorists of risk, modernity and postmodernity see many of the processes they are discussing as global transformations" (p. 434). Crime and risk are "a far from local affair in the twenty-first century" (p. 433). Globalizing policies since the 1990s created a global marketplace in which labour, capital, goods and services cross borders more freely — with the unintended consequence of making it easier for criminal goods, money and people to cross too.

**Critical global crimes**: "dealing in illicit drugs; illegal trafficking in weapons; illegal trafficking in human beings; money laundering; corruption; violent crimes including terrorism; and war crimes" (p. 419). Light weapons have caused 90 per cent of war casualties since 1945; homicide rose after civil wars when weapons spread to civilians (El Salvador, Albania, South Africa) (p. 421). Transnational organized crime includes smuggling of migrants, human trafficking, money laundering, trafficking in firearms, counterfeit goods, wildlife and cultural property "and even some aspects of cybercrime", threatening human security and undermining development (p. 423).

**Global crime groups** "have the power to criminalize politics, business and the police, developing efficient networks, and their reach has extended deep and wide" (p. 434). They exploit fast communications, deregulated finance and weak states.

**Black holes.** Castells: unemployment, alienation and youth abandonment — "the 'black holes of informational capitalism'" — provide "the ideal terrain for criminal recruitment of, for example, global drug traffickers" (p. 422), especially acute in post-Soviet Russia where organized crime filled the vacuum left by the state.

**Terrorism as global crime.** International terrorism is "multiform": traditional state-sponsored terrorism (a form of global organized crime) and "a new variant of freelance terrorists" loosely affiliated with extremist ideologies, "all the more dangerous precisely because of their lack of organization" (Eduardo, 2002, p. 421).

**Cybercrime.** "Cybercrime has become the phenomenon of the early twenty-first century and has been created by the vast expansion of computers in the global economy and private households" (p. 435) — and "in particular, the Internet". "There are thus countless individuals with the capacity and intent to use the medium to inflict damage (Bequai, 2002)" (p. 421). The book notes online pornography as a vast industry that terrorist organizations have exploited for funding through corporate fronts.

## Software lens

Cybercrime is the global crime most relevant to engineers, and the chapter's frameworks explain why it scales: the same frictionless infrastructure that lets legitimate services serve a global market lets attackers operate across jurisdictions, launder through crypto and payment rails, and recruit from Castells's "black holes" — regions with skilled but underemployed young people and weak enforcement. Global crime groups' power to "criminalize politics, business and the police" has digital forms: ransomware groups with state tolerance, bulletproof hosting, corrupt insiders at telecoms. Freelance, loosely affiliated actors are harder to model than hierarchical groups — the same lesson as "freelance terrorists" for threat intelligence.
