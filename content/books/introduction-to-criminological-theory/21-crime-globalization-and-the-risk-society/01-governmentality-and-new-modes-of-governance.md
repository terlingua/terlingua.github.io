## Learning objectives

- Define governmentality and explain its link to neoliberalism and actuarial crime control
- Contrast the twentieth-century treatment model with the new governmental style
- Explain responsibilization and 'joined-up' government
- Explain Garland's 'economic forms of reasoning' and the 'criminology of everyday life'

## The ideas

**Governmentality.** "The means to render populations thinkable and measurable through categorization, differentiation and sorting into hierarchies for the purpose of government" (glossary, p. 505). "Criminologists have drawn upon the 'governmentality' literature in order to explore the links between contemporary neoliberal political policy and the growing use of 'actuarial' or 'risk-based' strategies of crime control" (p. 434).

**From treatment to management.** "For most of the twentieth century, crime control was dominated by the 'treatment model' and was closely aligned to the powerful and benevolent state, which was obliged to intervene in the lives of individual offenders and seek to diagnose and cure their criminal behaviour" (p. 434). Its decline (nothing works, fiscal crisis, neoliberalism) produced new governance that recognizes "the insufficiency of criminal justice" and the need "to enrol expertise and resources from non-state actors including the 'responsibilization' of private citizens for their own security (Garland, 2001)" (p. 411).

**Responsibilization.** "Responsibilization strategies are... designed to offload the responsibility for risk management from central government on to the local state and non-state agencies, hence the increasing emphasis on public/private partnerships, inter-agency" working (p. 414; glossary p. 508). O'Malley's **prudentialism**: insurance against future risks becomes "a private obligation of the active citizen". 'Joined-up' government attacks complex problems such as youth offending through multi-agency partnerships.

**Economic reasoning.** Garland (1996) argues "the new governmental style is organized around 'economic forms of reasoning', which is reflected in contemporary rational actor theories that view crime as simply a matter of opportunity and that require no special disposition or abnormality" (p. 434). These are what Garland (2001) calls "criminologies of everyday life": crime is a normal risk arising in everyday situations — "late at night, deserted neighbourhoods, poorly lit streets, shopping malls, football games, bus stops, subway stations and so on" (Garland, 1999: 19, p. 412) — to be managed by situational measures rather than explained by pathology.

## Software lens

Governmentality is the conceptual core of risk-scored platforms: users, transactions and employees are made "thinkable and measurable through categorization, differentiation and sorting into hierarchies" — trust scores, risk tiers, fraud bands — for the purpose of governing them. Responsibilization is the shared-responsibility model in cloud security and the endless push of security duties onto users ("enable MFA, spot phishing, choose strong passwords"): risk management is offloaded from the provider to the individual. Garland's "economic forms of reasoning" are visible whenever abuse is treated purely as opportunity to be priced and reduced, with no interest in who the actors are.
