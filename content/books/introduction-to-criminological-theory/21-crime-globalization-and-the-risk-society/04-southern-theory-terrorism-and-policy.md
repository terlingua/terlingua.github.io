## Learning objectives

- Explain Connell's Southern theory and its critique of Northern (metropolitan) theory
- Explain how political science explains terrorism and why Crenshaw rejects pathology
- Describe sociological theories of terrorism (frustration-aggression, relative deprivation and others)
- Explain how terrorism entrenches the risk society, and the policy implications

## The ideas

**Southern theory.** "Connell (2007) has challenged the domination of social theory - and by implication criminological theory - by those in the metropoles of Europe and North America" (p. 435). This has meant "a view of the world from the skewed, minority perspective of the educated and the affluent, whose views are then perpetuated globally in educational curricula" (p. 425). The South appears as a source of *data*, "but not of ideas". Even theories of globalization often carry an "enduring imperial gaze". Connell looks to the South — Australia, Africa, Latin America, Iran, India — as sites of social theory in their own right. Power imbalances between North and South, tradition and dominant economic power, "provide fertile ground for terrorism and state violence" (p. 426).

**Terrorism** is an "emotive word that emphasizes the extreme fear caused by apparently indiscriminate violent actions of individuals claiming to be operating on behalf of some particular cause" (glossary, p. 509). After 9/11, "Most of the major theories that seek to explain terrorism are derived from theories of collective violence developed in the field of political science" (p. 435).

**Crenshaw (1998)**, a rational choice theorist, "argues that terrorism is not a pathological phenomenon and the focus of study should be on why some groups find terrorism useful and other groups do not" (p. 435). Some groups adopt terrorism after other strategies fail; others choose it early, having learned from others that alternatives do not work — the **contagion effect**, like copycat patterns in collective violence (Gurr). Participants may join when they believe their contribution to the public good outweighs the **free rider** problem (Muller and Opp, p. 429). Religious terrorism rests on persuading believers that a "'neglected duty'" exists in the mainstream religion — a militant interpretation of basic tenets rather than mere fanaticism (p. 429).

**Sociological theories** (O'Connor, 1994; p. 430): the **frustration-aggression** hypothesis ("every frustration leads to some form of aggression and every aggressive act relieves that frustration to some extent"); **relative deprivation** (comparing what one has with real or imaginary others and perceiving an intolerable discrepancy); and others drawing on structural strain and social learning. Anarchism is distinguished from terrorism: most anarchists reject terrorism, though anarchism can theoretically justify attacks on an organized, complacent society (p. 427). State violence and state terrorism are part of the same picture.

**Terrorism and risk.** In the aftermath of terrorist attacks, states "take the issue of state security seriously and the notion of the risk society becomes entrenched and virtually unassailable in public policy discourse" (p. 432).

**Policy implications** (pp. 432–4). Risk management has become "big business" and the dominant orthodoxy; it is not necessarily popularly demanded, yet politicians cite "mass risk consciousness". Public voices should be heard "in their complexity and diversity" (Ryan). Global crime demands international cooperation, attention to the economic exclusion that creates recruitment terrain, and scrutiny of how security discourses expand control.

## Software lens

Southern theory has a direct analogue in technology: products, standards and research are designed in a few metropolitan hubs and exported globally, treating the rest of the world as a market and a data source rather than a source of ideas. Content moderation, identity verification and fraud models built in the North routinely misfire elsewhere. Crenshaw's rational, strategic view of terrorism also models threat actors well: ask why a group finds a tactic *useful*, and watch for contagion — techniques spread because others have shown they work. The risk-society entrenchment after attacks is mirrored after major breaches: security budgets and monitoring expand rapidly and become hard to question, so post-incident measures deserve a later proportionality review.
