## The ideas

**Wilson's position.** *Thinking About Crime* (1975) came after Nixon's 'get tough' mandate; Reagan later made Wilson his special adviser on crime. Wilson accepted liberal claims that more patrols, longer sentences or new officials might have little effect, but was "scornful of those arguments that denied the existence of crime as a real problem" (p. 48). Central contention: "crime is quite simply an evil that requires a concerted and rigorous response".

**Order maintenance.** Wilson and Kelling argue the police are best used "not to reduce crime but to maintain social order". Kelling: "you ignore minor offences at great cost" and "disorder not only creates fear but... is a precursor to serious crime" (p. 48). Focus on incivilities — begging, drunkenness, gang fights, disorderly conduct — harmful in aggregate. (Broken windows itself is developed in Chapter 14.)

**Biosocial roots, pragmatic policy.** Wilson and Herrnstein (1985) propose that inclination to crime varies with internalized self-control, shaped by socialization (attacking single-parent families) and by "not necessarily unchangeable" biology: "biology... establishes the population that are at risk of becoming criminal, while it is socialization, or its failure, that helps to decide whether this will be realized" (pp. 48–9). But because biology and families cannot be changed quickly, Wilson turns to what policy *can* change: the costs and benefits of crime.

**Utilitarian individualism.** Victim surveys show crime falls hardest on the poor, but right realists "deny absolutely" the radical idea of an oppressed class against an unjust society; offenders and victims come from the same community. Wilson's famous line: with legitimate jobs declining and the costs of crime falling, "a rational teenager might well have concluded that it made more sense to steal cars than to wash them" (p. 49). Logically this supports both more jobs (carrot) and more punishment (stick); Wilson concentrates on the stick.

**Certainty over severity.** Recognizing the USA already imprisons more people for longer than lower-crime countries, Wilson stresses **certainty**: a robbery is four times more likely to lead to imprisonment in the UK than California and six times in Japan. "Consequences gradually lose their ability to control behaviour in proportion to how delayed or improbable they are" (p. 50, Wilson and Herrnstein). Felson's hot stove: a real stove burns quickly, certainly and slightly, and you never touch it again; an imaginary stove that burns once in 500 touches, five months later, will not teach you. Solutions: catch more offenders, improve consistency, repair police–public relations in high-crime areas. Wilson and Herrnstein even argue *against* very long sentences: undue severity may feel inequitable and provoke revenge offending, and judges hesitate to impose them.

**Fixed sentences and incapacitation.** Differential sentencing tries to change the offender; deterrence of others needs fixed, certain sentences. Differential sentences also create injustice: young, poor black offenders from unstable families get longer terms than older white middle-class offenders for the same offence. Recidivists commit most known crime, so certain incapacitation — even overnight or weekend — reduces offending. Wilson's conclusion: "Wicked people exist. Nothing avails except to keep them apart from innocent people. And many people, neither wicked nor innocent, but watchful, dissembling, and calculating of their opportunities, ponder our reaction to wickedness as a cue to what they might profitably do" (p. 51).

**Social control.** Rather than decriminalize prostitution or recreational drugs, control them more rigorously; target small-time drug users "attacking the fibre of the community" rather than dealers and addicts "beyond help". Police should act on empty properties, rowdy youths, litter, noise. Resources go to areas *at risk* of becoming high-crime, not to those where crime is already endemic; there, vigorous prosecution, especially of repeat offenders — the root of 'three strikes'.

## Software lens

Felson's hot stove is the best argument for fast CI over occasional audits. A linter that fails the build in thirty seconds trains behaviour; a quarterly compliance review that flags one violation in five hundred, months later, does not — no matter how severe the eventual consequence. The same holds for rate limits and abuse controls: certain, immediate, modest friction beats rare, delayed, dramatic bans. Wilson's three groups map onto threat modelling: a few determined attackers (incapacitate — revoke access), a few reliably honest users, and a large opportunistic middle who watch how violations are handled to decide what is worth trying.
