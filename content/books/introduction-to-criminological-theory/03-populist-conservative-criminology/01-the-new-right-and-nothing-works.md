## Learning objectives

- Explain why the rational actor model returned in the 1970s and 1980s
- Describe the new right's diagnosis of the 'permissive society'
- Explain the role of Martinson's 'nothing works' in undermining rehabilitation
- Explain why the revival is "a contemporary form of the rational actor model... with a distinctly retributive edge"

## The ideas

For most of the twentieth century the predestined actor model dominated: find the causes of crime, then treat and rehabilitate. In the 1970s conservative intellectuals in the USA and UK mounted "a vigorous moral campaign against various forms of 'deviance'" (p. 46), and in 1979 Margaret Thatcher made crime a successful election issue for the first time in post-war Britain, promising a return to 'Victorian values'.

**The new right's diagnosis.** Keep the economic, technological and managerial achievements of modernity, but assault its *cultural and ethical* components. Modernist culture's emphasis on "subjective values and individual self-expression" was undermining the motivation an efficient economy and state needed: people were "increasingly unwilling to achieve and even less prepared to obey" (pp. 46–7, citing Habermas). The remedy: revive tradition — state, school, family and the unquestioned acceptance of authority.

**The two targets.** In criminology the 'liberal indulgence' was embodied by the predestined actor model (treatment, not punishment) and by the radical victimized actor model (critique of an unequal society, with "policy assumptions of understanding, forgiveness and non-intervention"). Worse, the new right claimed this 'progressive theorizing' had *accelerated* permissiveness: crime was blamed on weakened family, school and religion and on a culture of "rights rather than obligations" and self-expression "to the point of self-indulgence" (p. 47).

**'Nothing works'.** The empirical ammunition was Robert Martinson's 1974 paper, which "purported to show that rehabilitation programmes in prison simply 'do not work'" (p. 47). The rationale for a welfare-oriented probation service was called into question, and the slogan was taken up at the British Home Office. What returned was **retributive punishment** — "serious crimes are simply evil, after all" — and the protection of society from danger. Punishment is about devising penalties to fit the crime and ensuring they are carried out, thereby reinforcing social values. The offender is treated "as an offender against social morality and not as a candidate for reform" (p. 47).

## Software lens

"Nothing works" is the organizational equivalent of a single influential benchmark declaring an entire approach dead. Once a slogan like "code review doesn't catch bugs" or "training doesn't change security behaviour" spreads, budgets move and the alternative (stricter enforcement, harsher penalties) gains ground — even if the original study was narrower than the slogan. The lesson for engineers: when one paper becomes a policy, read the paper. Martinson's review was widely read as "nothing can work" rather than "these programmes, as implemented and evaluated, showed weak effects".
