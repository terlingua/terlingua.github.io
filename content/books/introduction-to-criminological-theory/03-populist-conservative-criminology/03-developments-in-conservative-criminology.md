## Learning objectives

- State the two essential questions of contemporary conservative criminology
- Explain why individualistic theories are not inherently conservative, yet why their revival can be called conservative
- Explain the two utilitarian arguments for incarceration
- Describe the 'liberal-authoritarian' politics of New Labour and the Conservatives, and Cameron's 'broken society'

## The ideas

**Moral defect.** Conservative criminology now treats "criminal law [as] a codification of certain moral precepts and that people who break the law are those who are in some way psychologically or morally defective" (p. 53). Its two questions: "'how are morally defective persons produced?' and 'how can society protect itself against them?'" Causes lie in individuals; solutions lie in "a return to basic values". Example: Gottfredson and Hirschi's self-control theory (Chapter 15), where low self-control — short time horizon, low diligence, being "adventuresome, active, and physical", self-centredness — stems from "ineffective parenting".

**A caution.** Not all individualistic theories are conservative. Social learning theories such as differential association differ only in emphasis from sociological perspectives, and many individualists want better rehabilitation and family support. Yet the revival is conservative for two reasons (pp. 53–4):

1. By looking *inside* people it ignores external variables, takes society as given and turns crime into individual pathology — "Society is taken as good, offenders are bad" — so criminology "relinquishes any claims to being 'critical'".
2. The revival did not produce progressive practice; individualistic theories were "used - or at times misused - to justify 'get tough' crime control policies".

**Incapacitation and deterrence.** Policy forms include castration of sex offenders, mandatory urine testing and above all longer and more frequent imprisonment — the US prison population more than doubled in twenty years, passing two million in 2002 (12 per cent of black men in their twenties and early thirties were in prison versus 1.6 per cent of white men). England and Wales rose from 73,688 (2004) to 84,431 (2013). Two utilitarian arguments for incarceration (p. 54):

- **Rational actor**: make crime not pay by raising its cost. (The book notes the road not taken: the same logic implies giving people a sufficient 'stake in conformity' so they have something to lose.)
- **Predestined actor**: some offenders, whether by criminal mind or nature, are beyond rehabilitation and must be incapacitated.

The book also notes the irony of reintroduced capital punishment — contrary to Beccaria — and the "vintage rational actor thinking" of treating children as rational adults: two-thirds of known executions of juvenile offenders in the previous decade were in the USA (2002).

**Liberal-authoritarianism.** New Labour (1997–2010) and the Conservatives presented themselves simultaneously as guardians of freedom and advocates of personal responsibility — like Thatcher's "free economy and the strong state" (Gamble). David Cameron (2008) called knife crime a symptom of a **'broken society'** alongside family breakdown, welfare dependency and failing schools, rooted in a state that denies "a concept of moral choice". Anyone convicted of knife crime should expect jail: "We need a 'presumption to prison'". Family breakdown and 'dysfunctional families' (Duncan Smith, 2007) are causes; marriage is encouraged; causes are seen as "cultural rather than structural", including a "deliberate culture of worklessness" (pp. 55–6).

## Software lens

The "moral defect" framing is the postmortem that ends at "the engineer was careless". It locates the cause in a person, takes the system as given and prescribes discipline. A systems view asks what in the tooling, incentives and on-call load produced the error. Both can contain truth; the chapter's point is that choosing the individual frame tends to make the organization uncritical of itself. The 'stake in conformity' road not taken has a direct analogue: contributors with reputation, equity or a career path at stake have more to lose from abuse than throwaway accounts — which is why platforms invest in identity and reputation, not just bans.
