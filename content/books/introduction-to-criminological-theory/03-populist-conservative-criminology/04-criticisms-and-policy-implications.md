## Learning objectives

- State the six criticisms of populist conservative criminology
- Explain why street crime nonetheless matters to 'realists' of left and right
- Explain Van Den Haag's political defence of right realism
- Summarize the policy implications

## The ideas

**Six criticisms** (pp. 56–7):

1. **Street crime only.** It focuses on street crime and public order to the near exclusion of costly white-collar and corporate crime (mis-sold pensions, untested drugs sold abroad, polluted effluent). But the book concedes that being robbed or burgled "impacts most immediately on individuals and engenders the greatest 'fear of crime'", and that recognizing this "makes them 'realists'".
2. **No structure.** It ignores poverty and social exclusion, focusing on conditioning and socialization. The incivility–crime link is hard to prove: Matthews (1992) found poverty and public services far more significant than incivilities in urban decline.
3. **Abandoned areas.** The worst areas are "not deemed worth saving" (Wilson and Kelling); their residents are punished harshly when they offend and left unprotected when they do not — and blamed for failing to socialize their children.
4. **Unfair order policing.** Policing begging and vagrancy rests on "often dubious legality" and targets disadvantaged groups like homeless beggars.
5. **Accountability.** Clear-up rates are the true measure of police performance; an order-maintenance model makes efficiency hard to measure and a flexible role over incivilities makes accountability and professionalism hard to ensure (Kinsey et al., 1986).
6. **Demonization.** Treating crime as the choice of those raised without proper morals has led to targeting whole groups — New Age travellers, drug users, 'marauding' young males — through an intrusive, punitive 'law and order' state.

**Van Den Haag's defence.** Ernest Van Den Haag (1975), a contemporary of Wilson, dismissed all six objections and was explicit about the politics: capitalism necessarily creates 'winners' and 'losers', and the winners must be allowed to enjoy the fruits of enterprise "without these rewards being illegally taken away by the losers" (p. 57). The core of a polarized society must be protected from criminals and a socially excluded 'underclass' — and that core supplies electoral majorities for these policies.

**Policy implications** (summary points, p. 58): crime is real and requires a rigorous response; catch more offenders and make the system consistent; certainty over severity; imprisonment as incapacitator; retributive punishment to protect society; lawbreakers as psychologically or morally defective; reinforce parental responsibility, marriage and family; ignore social, economic and structural variables in favour of conditioning and socialization.

## Software lens

Criticism 5 is a metrics problem every SRE recognizes. If you replace a crisp outcome metric (crimes cleared) with a broad, discretionary mandate ("maintain order", "keep the platform healthy"), you gain flexibility but lose measurability and accountability. Moderation teams given a vague "remove anything harmful" brief face the same issue: decisions become hard to audit and easy to apply selectively to unpopular groups (criticism 4 and 6). Criticism 1 is a prioritization bias: what users *feel* (a visible outage) dominates over what costs most (a slow data-integrity bug or a vendor's quiet licence violation).
