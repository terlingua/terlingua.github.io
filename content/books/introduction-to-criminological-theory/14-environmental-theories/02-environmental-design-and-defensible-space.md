## Learning objectives

- Explain environmental design theories and the belief in surveillance
- Describe Jane Jacobs's critique of 1950s planning
- Explain Newman's four elements of defensible space
- Summarize the evaluation evidence and the point that defensible space must be defended
- Describe secondary design effects and Design Against Crime

## The ideas

**Environmental design theories** hold that "the nature of the built environment can affect the level of crime both by influencing potential offenders and by affecting the ability of a person to exercise control over their surroundings" (glossary, p. 504). "There is essentially a powerful belief in the capacity of surveillance to help control crime" (p. 285). Key writers: Jacobs (1961), Newman (1972, 1976) and Jeffery (1977, who coined *crime prevention through environmental design*).

**Jane Jacobs (1961)** "was the first person to propose a new way of looking at the relationship between the physical environment and crime, and her work was essentially an attack on the urban planning practices in the USA during the 1950s" (p. 291) — urban renewal and slum clearance that destroyed lively mixed neighbourhoods. Safety comes from busy streets with mixed uses, clear public/private demarcation and 'eyes on the street' from residents and shopkeepers.

**Oscar Newman** argued urban crime reflects "a breakdown of the social mechanisms that once kept crime in check" (p. 291). Defensible space is an environment "controlled... not by the police but by a community of people sharing a common terrain" (Newman, 1976: 2). Four elements (p. 286):

1. **Territoriality** — "'capacity of the physical environment to create perceived zones of influence'": design (narrowed entrances, cul-de-sacs, raised coloured paving) encouraging residents to claim ownership and signalling private space to outsiders. Public thoroughfares and open spaces owned by nobody undermine it.
2. **Natural surveillance** — overlooked lobbies, well-placed windows; avoid high-rise blocks with easy outsider access and enclosed entrances where offenders operate unseen.
3. **Safe image** — conveying a safe, cared-for neighbourhood; stigmatized public housing designed to stand out becomes vulnerable.
4. **Protected milieu** — a neighbourhood within a wider crime-free area, insulated by a 'moat' of safety; mixed land use works if residents can identify with and survey related facilities.

**Evidence.** Evaluation is hard because measures are implemented together. Hartford, Connecticut, combined design, patrols and citizen mobilization: large initial drops in burglary and robbery (Fowler et al., 1979) proved short-lived (Fowler and Mangione, 1982). Other studies find less support (p. 287). Merry (1981): the benefits depend on residents who report or challenge strangers — "'defensible' space must actually be defended".

**Secondary effects.** Traffic calming and one-way schemes prevent quick exits, signal ownership and reduce casual through-traffic that exposes houses to passing burglars. **Design Against Crime (DAC)**, from the University of the Arts London, applies design to products and places to reduce crime (p. 287).

## Software lens

Defensible space is a strong metaphor for system ownership. **Territoriality**: services with clear owners (CODEOWNERS, on-call rotations, named maintainers) are better cared for than shared "everybody's" infrastructure — the digital equivalent of open spaces owned by nobody. **Natural surveillance**: design that makes activity visible to those who care (readable audit logs, dashboards owned by the team) rather than hidden corridors (undocumented admin endpoints). **Safe image**: a well-maintained repository signals that problems get noticed; an abandoned one invites abuse — related to broken windows. Merry's point is the critical one: a monitoring dashboard nobody watches is defensible space nobody defends.
