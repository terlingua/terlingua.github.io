## Learning objectives

- State the Brantinghams' four dimensions of crime and define environmental criminology
- Summarize Guerry and Quetelet and the British post-war ecological studies
- Explain crime pattern theory: activity nodes, paths, edges and awareness space
- Explain the five-part model of victim selection and the distinction between crime generators and attractors

## The ideas

**Four dimensions.** Brantingham and Brantingham (1981): criminal incidents occur when "a law, an offender, a target and a place - are all in concurrence. Environmental criminology is the study of the fourth dimension, the study of where and when crimes occur" (p. 291). Contemporary environmental criminology studies the spatial patterning of crime, the 'journey to crime' and how areas of criminal residence are created and maintained, and combines **routine activities theory**, **rational choice theory** and **crime pattern theory**.

**Early theories.** In France, **Guerry (1833)** and **Quetelet (1842)** mapped conviction rates and found crime varied greatly by area; property and violent crime had different geographies; and patterns persisted over time, suggesting predictability and so preventive policy (pp. 278–9). The Chicago School followed (Chapter 7).

**British post-war studies** (Mays, 1954; Morris, 1957; Wootton, 1959) combined the ecological approach (why people live where they do), the subcultural approach and the labelling approach (p. 279). Grounded in the predestined actor model but "rendered more 'open'" by adding free will — "in the last resort it is the individuals themselves who decide". Rex and Moore showed little real choice over residence in the struggle between **housing classes**; focus shifted to **housing allocation** as the mechanism concentrating problem families. Baldwin and Bottoms (1976) found such studies "strong on description without providing any theoretical explanation" (p. 280).

**Crime pattern theory.** Focuses on movement patterns of victims and offenders (p. 283):

- **Activity nodes** — centres where people "spend the majority of their time": home, school, work, entertainment, shopping.
- **Pathways** — routes connecting nodes (streets, footpaths, transport).
- **Edges** — boundaries that "cannot easily be traversed", physical (rivers, forests, bridges) or perceptual (areas people fear, such as rival gang territory, or areas of sharply different socio-economic status). An inner-city offender might want lucrative targets in affluent areas "but generally would not venture into such localities because anonymity is absent" (p. 283).

Together these form an **awareness space** or cognitive map. "Offenders will commit crimes in their awareness spaces, either along a path, along an edge or around an activity node"; offending outside it risks getting lost, missing escape routes and not knowing where targets are.

**Five-part model of victim selection** (Brantingham and Brantingham, 1978, p. 291): (i) offenders seek and identify a target; (ii) cues for decisions come from the environment; (iii) offenders use these cues for target selection; (iv) they develop cues associated with 'good' targets — a **template**; (v) one offender may have many crime selection templates.

**Generators and attractors.** "Crime generators are places or areas to which large numbers of people are attracted for reasons unrelated to any particular level of criminal motivation" (shopping centres, stations, stadiums), "while crime attractors are places that present well-known criminal opportunities to which strongly motivated criminal offenders are attracted" (drug markets, red-light districts) (p. 291).

## Software lens

Crime pattern theory maps neatly onto attacker behaviour in systems. **Activity nodes** are the services and data stores an attacker already has access to; **pathways** are the network routes and credentials linking them; **edges** are boundaries that are hard to cross — physical (network segmentation, air gaps) or perceptual (heavily monitored enclaves where an intruder's traffic would stand out because anonymity is absent). Attackers operate within their **awareness space** — tools and environments they know — which is why threat intelligence on TTPs is predictive. A public API is a **crime generator** (huge legitimate traffic, some opportunists); a misconfigured, widely publicized exposed bucket is a **crime attractor**. Selection templates are scanners' fingerprints for "good targets".
