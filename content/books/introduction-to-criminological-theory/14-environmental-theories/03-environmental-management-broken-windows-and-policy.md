## Learning objectives

- Define environmental management theories
- Explain the broken windows thesis and its spiral of incivility and crime
- Assess the evidence for broken windows and explain its link to zero tolerance and problem-oriented policing
- State the policy implications of environmental theories

## The ideas

**Environmental management** rests "largely on the premise that certain districts may suffer because they give the impression that their residents no longer care" (p. 291). The glossary: "the activities of a rational calculating individual can be restricted or curtailed by changing his or her surroundings" (p. 504).

**Broken windows.** Wilson and Kelling (1982) describe the problem as witnessed in urban America: "A piece of property is abandoned, weeds grow up, and a window is smashed. Adults stop scolding rowdy children; the children, emboldened, become more rowdy. Families move out, unmarried adults move in. Teenagers gather in front of the corner store..." (p. 289). Incivilities — vandalism, graffiti, litter, drunkenness, begging — signal that nobody cares and informal control has collapsed, inviting serious offenders. "'Broken windows' theory proposes that incivilities lead to crime, the evidence of which causes further incivilities. Environmental management involves striving to remove the evidence of incivilities" (p. 292). Examples: rapid repair of vandalism in schools; New York's immediate removal of subway graffiti, which deprived "artists" of seeing their work displayed.

**Evidence.** "The attraction of 'broken windows' theory has been its plausibility. In fact, it is so plausible that it has been accepted despite very little research support" (p. 289). Recall Matthews (1992, Chapter 3): poverty and public services mattered more than incivilities in urban decline.

**Policing.** Broken windows became "very influential in the introduction and implementation of 'zero tolerance' and 'problem-oriented policing'" (p. 290). **Zero tolerance**, much publicized in the USA and UK in the late 1990s, has police strictly enforce against minor offences to restore order. **Problem-oriented policing** analyses recurring local problems and develops responses in partnership with criminal justice, welfare and voluntary groups (Leigh et al., 1998). The left realist perspective on these is revisited in Chapter 18; critics (Chapter 3) warn of unfair targeting of the marginalized and weakened accountability.

**Policy implications** (p. 290). Environmental theories focus on the criminal event and its location, so policies change or regulate problematic environments: situational crime prevention and target hardening, environmental design and CPTED, rapid repair and clean-up, attention to housing allocation and land use, and focused policing of hot spots. They share the rational actor assumption that offenders respond to environmental cues — with the attendant risk of displacement and neglect of motivation.

## Software lens

Broken windows is the best-known criminological idea in software ("software entropy", from *The Pragmatic Programmer*): a codebase with ignored warnings, flaky tests and dead code signals that nobody cares, and quality degrades further. The criminological debate carries a warning, though: the idea is "so plausible that it has been accepted despite very little research support", and its zero-tolerance policing version raised fairness and accountability problems. The engineering analogues: fixing small issues promptly is cheap and sensible, but "zero tolerance" code policies enforced mechanically (block every PR with any lint warning, punish every minor lapse) can burn goodwill, target the least powerful contributors and divert attention from structural causes such as under-resourcing. Problem-oriented policing — analyse recurring problems and fix root causes with partners — is closer to good SRE practice.
