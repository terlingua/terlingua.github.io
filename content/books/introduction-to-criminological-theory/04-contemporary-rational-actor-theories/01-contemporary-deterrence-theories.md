## Learning objectives

- Define certainty, severity and celerity and explain their relationship
- Distinguish general from specific deterrence with examples
- Summarize the evidence on capital punishment, detection and perceived risk
- State the ethical and empirical objections to deterrence, including recidivism and the limits of incapacitation

## The ideas

**The doctrine.** Contemporary deterrence theory rests on "certainty, severity and celerity (speed) of punishment, proportionality, specific and general deterrence" (p. 60). Celerity is how swiftly sanctions follow the offence; certainty is the probability of apprehension and punishment. Research treats certainty as more important than severity: "The more severe the available punishment, the less likely it is to be applied. On the other hand, the less certain the punishment, the more severe it will need to be to deter crime" (p. 61, Akers).

**General deterrence**: punishing offenders serves "as an example to the general population who will be frightened into non-participation" (p. 61). Examples: drink-driving crackdowns, gang task forces, "Shoplifters will be prosecuted" notices, the death penalty. **Specific deterrence**: the punished offender refrains from reoffending because they realize they will be caught and punished again. Examples: shock sentencing, corporal punishment, mandatory arrest for domestic violence.

**Evidence.** Ehrlich (1975) claimed each US execution deterred seven or eight murders, a much-criticized econometric result at odds with earlier studies (Sellin; Bedau) and with England and Wales after abolition in 1965, where no impact on homicide was found. Domestic murder is rarely a rational choice; contract killing may be. Detection risk matters more than penalty level (Beyleveld), but actual detection is low (burglary detection 9–46 per cent by locality), so *perceived* risk is the real variable — and Gill and Matthews found that none of their convicted bank robbers had even considered being caught.

**Objections.**

- **Proportionality**: the punishment needed to deter varies by person, crime and circumstance, so deterrent sentences may be "totally out of all proportion to the seriousness of the offence" (p. 62) — at odds with the Classical principle that punishment fits the crime.
- **Counterproductive effects**: conviction may leave a person with little to lose (Martin and Webster), close legitimate routes (a labelling-style argument), encourage harder efforts to avoid detection, violent escapes and futile not-guilty pleas, and deter child sex offenders from seeking treatment.
- **Irrational offenders**: punishing people with little control over impulses "would appear to be morally indefensible" (p. 63). Many petty offenders cannot weigh costs and benefits; low self-control and demand for immediate gratification exclude calculation (Keane et al.).
- **Recidivism**: 84 per cent of males aged 14–17 released in 1998 were reconvicted within two years; assaults and drug use continue inside custody.
- **Limits of incapacitation**: the Home Office estimated it would take "a 15 per cent increase in the level of custody to effect a 1 per cent reduction in offending" (p. 63).

## Software lens

Security teams use both kinds of deterrence. General: publishing "all access is logged and violations are prosecuted", or announcing that a contractor was terminated for credential sharing. Specific: revoking a repeat offender's production access. The evidence pattern transfers: what changes behaviour is the *perceived* probability of detection, not the size of the penalty on paper — which is why visible, reliable detection (alerts that actually fire, reviews that actually happen) beats a scary policy document. And the irrational-offender objection applies to people under extreme on-call stress: a penalty they cannot weigh at 3 a.m. does nothing but add harm afterwards.
