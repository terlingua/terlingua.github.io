## The ideas

**The basic claim.** Routine activities theory is "a development and subdivision of rational choice theory" proposing that "for a personal or property crime to occur, there must be at the same time and place a perpetrator, a victim and/or an object of property" (p. 67). The crime can be prevented "if the potential victim or another person is present who can take action to deter it".

**Three variables** (Cohen and Felson, 1979):

1. **Motivated offenders** present in the locality (predominantly young males).
2. **Suitable targets** — 'target' rather than 'victim' because most crime is about property or money. Suitability is **VIVA** (p. 68): **Value** (from the offender's subjective perspective), **Inertia** (physical properties that impede removal — weight, size, bolted down), **Visibility**, **Accessibility**.
3. **Absence of capable guardians**, formal or informal.

"The risk of criminal victimisation varies dramatically among the circumstances and locations in which people place themselves and their property" (p. 68). After the Second World War, changes in work, school and leisure put more people in public places at particular times — more accessible as targets *and* away from home as guardians. Cohen and Felson linked crime rates to a **household activity ratio** (households not husband-wife families, or with wives in paid work), an index of empty, unguarded homes.

**Felson's later work.** Crime is "a private phenomenon largely unaffected by state intervention"; what matters is "the 'quiet and natural method by which people prevent crime in the course of daily life'" (p. 68). "Ordinary people, oneself, friends, family or even strangers are the most likely capable guardians." He extended the theory to four categories: **exploitative** (robbery, rape), **mutualistic** (gambling, prostitution, drug dealing), **competitive** (fighting), **individualistic** (individual drug use, suicide); and added a fourth element: absence of an **intimate handler** — a parent, partner or other significant person who exercises informal control over the *offender*. The offender must escape the handler and find a target free from a guardian.

**Criticisms.** Akers (1997): the theory "fails fundamentally to explain why it is that some people engage in criminal behaviour and others do not" — it assumes motivated offenders exist and says nothing about them (p. 70). Sutton argues 'crime opportunity' can only be known after a crime succeeds or fails, so "opportunity makes the thief" is logically flawed; the notion is "an elegantly precise, perhaps perfect, post hoc description of any successfully completed criminal act. It is nothing more" (p. 70).

**Applications.** Child abduction and homicide risk; growth in child abuse correlated with alcohol abuse; journeys to murder; and Tolson's Clergy Lifestyle Theory — clergy suffer more violence than other community professions, mostly at home rather than in a workplace (pp. 70–1). The glossary's **opportunity theory** formalizes the approach with exposure, proximity, guardianship and target attractiveness.

## Software lens

Routine activities is the threat model triangle: threat actor, asset, missing control. VIVA is asset classification: value (what is it worth to an attacker), inertia (how hard to exfiltrate — a 40 TB dataset has more inertia than an API key), visibility (exposed on the internet? indexed by Shodan?), accessibility (reachable without auth?). Capable guardians are monitoring, on-call responders, and colleagues who notice odd behaviour. The post-war shift has a direct analogue: remote work and cloud moved assets and people out of the guarded perimeter. Felson's intimate handler is the manager or teammate who knows an engineer well enough to notice a slide toward insider risk. Sutton's critique warns against "opportunity" being declared only in postmortems.
