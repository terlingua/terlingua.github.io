## Learning objectives

- Explain Hopkins Burke and Pollock's challenge to ignoring motivation, using hate crime
- Explain how prison populations undermine the rational actor model
- Summarize the evidence on short-term rationality, perceived risk and the Kansas City experiment
- State the policy implications and limits of contemporary rational actor theories, including displacement

## The ideas

**Motivation matters after all.** Contemporary rational actor theory emphasized deterrence and treated motivation as irrelevant "as long as criminal behaviour ceased to occur" (p. 72). Hopkins Burke and Pollock (2004) challenge this with **hate crime**. Hate offenders are "those unaccepting of the heterogeneous nature of the contemporary societies in which they live and primarily characterise social groups according to their visible ethnic, racial or sexual identity rather than their personal attributes"; hate victimization is about "'what' someone is, rather than 'who' they actually are". Legislation and situational measures have reduced incidents, but a colleague summed up the orthodoxy as "'if they aren't saying it and they aren't doing it then that's ok'. But is it ok?" Latent hatred remains and can be activated by new arrivals in an area, a like-minded friend, a few drinks or "surfing the Internet".

**Who is in prison?** If the model were accurate, prisons would be full of rational calculators. Instead, over a third of male prisoners had a significant mental health problem, nearly one in ten had experienced psychosis, one in four had attempted suicide in prison, and most met criteria for personality disorder (Mind, 2006); male prisoner suicide is six times the general rate (p. 73). "The limitations of the rational actor model first identified two hundred years ago are still relevant today."

**Short-term versus long-term rationality.** Doob and Cesaroni: the young are impulsive and focus on immediate rewards. Tunnell's sixty prisoners did not think about criminal consequences; over half did not know the penalty. Since most offenders do not expect to be caught, raising penalties has no prolonged effect; perceived apprehension risk is what deters, and even that is limited — the **Kansas City experiment** found varying patrol techniques had little effect (p. 75).

**Policy implications.** All three variants — deterrence, rational choice, routine activities — descend from Beccaria and Bentham. Deterrence predicts low crime where offending is most painful. Situational prevention aims to convince would-be offenders that the effort, risk or reward is not worthwhile, and draws on Newman's defensible space (Chapter 14). But "while there remains a population of potentially determined and available criminals, there will continue to be an issue of crime displacement": "locking all doors and bolting all windows is invariably a good idea but it is no universal panacea... Situational crime prevention is thus a sensible but incomplete crime control strategy" (p. 351, revisited in Chapter 18).

## Software lens

Ignoring motivation works until it doesn't. A content filter that blocks slurs stops the saying but not the hating; when the filter's wording rules are evaded, latent motivation reappears. Similarly, blocking an attacker's known IPs (situational) displaces them to new infrastructure; it doesn't remove the actor. **Displacement** comes in flavours — to another target, time, method or crime — and any evaluation of a control should check whether attacks fell or merely moved. The prison data is a warning for any accountability system built on a rational-actor assumption: if the people sanctioned are disproportionately overloaded, unwell or unsupported, the policy is punishing incapacity, not choice.
