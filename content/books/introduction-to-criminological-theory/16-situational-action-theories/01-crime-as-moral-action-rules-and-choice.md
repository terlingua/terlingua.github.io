## Learning objectives

- Explain the problem SAT addresses: integrating person and environment explanations
- Define crime as moral action and humans as rule-guided actors
- Explain the perception-choice process and the difference between habit and deliberation
- Explain why SAT claims a single process can explain all kinds of crime

## The ideas

**The problem.** Wikström (2005) identifies two central themes in criminology: involvement in crime depends on who the person is (characteristics and life experiences) and on the environments they encounter (p. 311). **Situational action theory (SAT)** "is a general theory of moral action and crime that aims to integrate person and environmental explanatory perspectives" (p. 323). It seeks *causal* explanations — "why and how acts of crime occur" — and "is based on a realist perspective" (p. 324): mechanisms are real and can be identified.

**Crime as moral action.** "Crime is best considered as moral action, which is defined as action guided by moral rules about what is right or wrong in a particular circumstance" (p. 324). Acts of crime are breaches of moral rules defined in law. Defining crime this way lets one theory explain all crime — from shoplifting to genocide — because all involve the same kind of process; what differs is "the content of the moral context (the action-relevant moral rules) and a person's morality (the action-relevant moral values and emotions)" (p. 314).

**Rule-guided actors.** "Humans are viewed as essentially rule-guided actors and social order is fundamentally based on the adherence to common rules of conduct. It is essentially a moral order" (p. 324).

**The perception-choice process.** Action results from how a person *perceives* their action alternatives in a setting and how they *choose* among them. The crucial filtering happens at perception: most people never see crime as an option in most situations. "People exercise agency within the context of rule-guided choice and the latter can take the form of either deliberation or habit. It is only when people make deliberate choices that they exercise free will" (p. 324). Whether choice is deliberate or habitual "depends on the familiarity of the individual with the circumstances": habits form through repeated exposure to particular settings, while unfamiliar or conflicting circumstances prompt deliberation (p. 315). In deliberation, choices are influenced by **self-control** (internal control) and response to **deterrence** cues (external controls).

**Where SAT sits.** It combines a predestined actor concern with motivation and morality with a rational actor concern with choice and deterrence, and situational interest in settings — a genuinely integrated theory.

## Software lens

SAT's perception-choice process is a powerful lens for security culture. Most engineers never "see" exfiltrating data as an option; it is filtered out at perception by their morality and the setting's rules. Controls (audit, approvals) matter mainly for the minority for whom crime *is* perceived as an option. Habit versus deliberation matters too: in familiar routines engineers follow habits (good or bad), so the reliable path to compliance is making secure behaviour the habitual default — paved roads, secure-by-default templates — rather than relying on deliberate choices under pressure. When a situation is unfamiliar (a novel incident), people deliberate, and then self-control and visible deterrence cues come into play.
