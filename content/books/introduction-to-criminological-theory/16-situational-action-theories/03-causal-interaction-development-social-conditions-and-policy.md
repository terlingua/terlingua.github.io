## Learning objectives

- Explain how propensity and exposure interact as causes through the perception-choice process
- Distinguish the causes of crime from the 'causes of the causes'
- Explain how SAT treats development and broader social conditions
- Summarize Wikström et al.'s findings on deterrence and SAT's policy implications

## The ideas

**Causal interaction.** "Propensity and exposure set the context within which people exercise agency, that is, they act deliberately or by habit. Agency is exercised through the perception-choice process. The interaction of propensity and exposure is the input to this process" (p. 318). Saying they are causes means changes in them change action through their effect on perception and choice. SAT does not propose a simple additive model: propensity matters only in settings that can activate it, and settings matter only for people with relevant propensity.

**Development and change.** Why people have particular propensities, and why they are exposed to particular settings, are questions about the **causes of the causes** — distinct from why an act of crime occurs (Wikström, 2006, p. 320). Propensities develop through moral education and the development of cognitive skills for self-control; exposure changes as people's activity fields change through the life course. Change in either changes offending.

**Broader social conditions.** Social disadvantage, segregation, integration and community characteristics are **indirect causes** — "causes of the causes": they matter by shaping the kinds of people who develop (propensity) and the kinds of settings that emerge and to which people are exposed. "People act and develop in settings" (p. 320). SAT thus links micro-level action to macro-level conditions without treating structure as a direct cause of individual acts.

**Reflections.** SAT is ambitious and comprehensive; it was devised in the first decade of the twenty-first century by Wikström and, at the time of writing, had "no secondary sources, commentary or published critiques" (p. 324) — its evaluation lies ahead. It shares with Hirschi and Gottfredson a role for self-control, with rational choice a role for deterrence and perception of alternatives, and with environmental criminology a role for settings.

**Policy implications.** Wikström et al. (2011) revisited deterrence: many people do not offend not because they fear getting caught but because **they do not see crime as an action alternative**; such people abstain "regardless of whether they assess the risk of getting caught as very high or very low". Among those who *do* regularly see crime as an option, involvement is influenced by **deterrence sensitivity** — those who perceive higher risk offend less often (p. 323). SAT therefore "provides a sophisticated basis for discovering who is likely to offend and under what circumstances" and "considers key predestined actor model motivational issues long neglected by rational choice theorists". Policies should target moral education and self-control development (propensity) and the moral rules and enforcement of settings (exposure), with deterrence aimed at those for whom crime is a live option.

## Software lens

Wikström et al.'s deterrence finding is directly actionable for security programmes. For most engineers, misuse isn't an option they consider, so heavy monitoring aimed at everyone mostly adds friction and erodes trust. Deterrence cues (visible auditing, consequences) matter for the minority who do perceive misuse as an option — contractors with weak ties, people in conflict with the organization. The "causes of the causes" distinction helps postmortems: the immediate cause of an engineer's risky action is the propensity-setting interaction; the causes of the causes are hiring, onboarding, team culture, incentive structures and workload that shaped both. Fixing only immediate causes leaves the pipeline that produces them intact.
