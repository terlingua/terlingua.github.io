## Learning objectives

- Define temptation and provocation as the two main categories of motivation
- Explain crime propensity (morality and self-control) and criminogenic exposure
- Explain the moral filter and the principle of moral correspondence
- Explain the principle of the conditional relevance of controls using Wikström's two-by-two table

## The ideas

**No special motives.** "People commit acts of crime for all sorts of reasons and there are no particular motives that make people breach moral rules" (p. 324). Motivation has "a general directional influence" but is not sufficient.

**Temptation and provocation** — the two main categories of motivation, both outcomes of the person-setting interaction (p. 316):

- A **temptation** occurs "(a) when there is a connection between the desires of a person, that is, their wants or needs, and an opportunity to fulfil a desire; or (b) when there is an opportunity for a person to fulfil a commitment they have made".
- A **provocation** occurs "when a friction, that is, an unwanted external interference, causes a person to feel anger or annoyance" (Wikström, 2006).

**Propensity.** "Individuals will vary in their propensity to engage in a particular moral action depending on their moral values and emotions" (p. 324) — their **morality** (values, and moral emotions such as shame and guilt) — and "the ability of an individual to exercise self-control can also significantly affect their crime propensity".

**Exposure.** "People do not act in a social vacuum, and thus propensities always need some environmental inducement to become activated" (p. 324). **Criminogenic settings** are defined by their action-relevant moral rules and the level of their enforcement (p. 312). Exposure is a person's encounters with such settings.

**Moral filter.** The interaction between a person's morality and the moral context "will serve as a moral filter, determining whether or not they will act upon temptations or provocations" (p. 316). Many people are disadvantaged and frustrated without offending because they lack propensity.

**Moral correspondence.** "Moral action is guided by the interplay between the morality of an individual and the moral rules of the setting in which they operate" (p. 318). When personal morality and setting rules correspond, action follows them.

**Conditional relevance of controls.** Wikström's table (Figure 16.1, p. 318):

| | Setting rules encourage crime | Setting rules discourage crime |
|---|---|---|
| **Morality encourages** | Action likely; controls irrelevant | Action depends on level of **deterrence** (external control) |
| **Morality discourages** | Action depends on capability to exercise **self-control** (internal control) | Action unlikely; controls irrelevant |

Controls "are only causally relevant under certain moral conditions" — when there is a discrepancy between personal morality and setting rules (p. 319). And all depends on an initial motivation (temptation or provocation).

## Software lens

The two-by-two table is a precise design tool for security controls. Where engineers' own norms and the team's norms both forbid an action (bottom right), extra controls add cost without benefit. Where both permit it (top left) — say, a team culture that thinks sharing prod credentials is fine — controls are irrelevant because people don't experience it as wrong; you must change norms. Deterrence (audit, alerts) matters when an individual's morality permits something the setting forbids (a contractor who sees nothing wrong with copying data); self-control and support matter when a setting encourages something a person thinks is wrong (a team pressuring someone to skip review). Temptation (desire plus opportunity) and provocation (friction causing anger — a denied promotion, a hostile code review) are the two triggers to monitor in insider-risk programmes.
