## Learning objectives

- Explain the broadened concept of community and 'communities of care'
- Explain Strang's 'policing by communities rather than the policing of communities'
- Distinguish Anglo-Saxon, French and German conceptions of individualism
- Explain Durkheim's ideal versus forced division of labour and his threefold political project

## The ideas

**Community reconsidered.** "The whole notion of community is complex and extends well beyond the more traditional definitions based on locality - or neighbourhood - and embraces a multiplicity of groups and networks to which, it is believed, we all belong (Strang, 1995)" (p. 455). It builds on **'communities of care'** — "the networks of obligation and respect between the individual and everyone who cares about the person the most - and these are significantly not bounded by geography" (Braithwaite and Daly, p. 445). They suit "contemporary modern living in urban societies because they provide a developed notion of 'community' where membership - or social identity - is personal and does not necessarily carry any fixed or external attributes" (p. 455). Such communities can deliver restorative justice and legitimate informal control: "'strong communities can speak to us in moral voices'" and allow "'the policing by communities rather than the policing of communities'" (Strang, 1995: 20, p. 445).

**Three individualisms** (pp. 447–50):

- **Anglo-Saxon**: emerging with utilitarianism and political economy (Adam Smith); relations between people become subordinate to relations between people and things; free trade replaces the mercantilist idea that one party's gain is another's loss with the idea that exchange benefits both. Individualism as economic self-interest in markets.
- **French**: for Saint-Simon and followers the Revolution and liberalism had "a purely negative, destructive value"; they sought a new organic social order. Individualism here is *moral* — rights and the dignity of the person sustained by society and the state; Durkheim builds on this.
- **German**: Herder sees humanity embodied in distinct cultural communities, each a *Volk*; a person "is what he is... by virtue of his belonging to a given cultural community", laying the basis for the **'ethnic theory'** of nationality against the **'elective theory'** based on consensus (p. 449). Fichte and later thinkers develop holistic, sometimes authoritarian conceptions.

**Durkheim.** With organic society, "it was now possible to believe in self-indulgence - for both the bourgeois and proletarian - the only check would be conflict" (p. 456), because a forced, unequal division of labour gives some groups more power through inherited wealth or position. "Durkheim considered that the division of labour works best if people are in positions where their talents are best optimized - that is, an ideal division of labour. The actual division of labour in society is nevertheless forced, egoistical, anomic and riddled with individual despair and conflict" (p. 456). His **threefold political project** (p. 452): clarify reasonable and acceptable aspirations; integrate the isolated, egoistical individual into an inclusive social network; and remove the conditions that sustain inequality — uniting individuals "into a higher community of which they feel a part". The State is the enabling condition of individualism, with **corporations** (administrative councils for each industry) — but critics ask who sets them up, since the State is in the hands of those who benefit from the forced division of labour.

## Software lens

Communities of care are how modern engineering communities actually work: not bounded by office or city, but networks of obligation and respect around people (maintainers, mentors, on-call partners) — which is why distributed teams can still exert strong informal norms. "Policing by communities rather than the policing of communities" is the difference between a project's own moderators enforcing its norms and an external platform imposing rules on it. Durkheim's ideal division of labour — people in roles that optimize their talents, with inherited advantage removed — is essentially the meritocratic promise tech often claims; his "forced division of labour" is what results when access to roles is shaped by background and networks, producing despair and conflict rather than cohesion.
