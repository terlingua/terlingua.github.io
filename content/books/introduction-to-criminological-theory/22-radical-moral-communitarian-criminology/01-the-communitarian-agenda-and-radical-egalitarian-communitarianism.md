## Learning objectives

- Explain why communitarianism emerged in the USA in the 1980s and how it differs from liberalism
- State the two dominant themes of communitarianism and the 'I' constituted through the 'We'
- Explain Etzioni's balancing of rights and responsibilities
- Explain radical egalitarian communitarianism and its definition of poverty

## The ideas

**Origins.** "Communitarianism emerged in the USA during the 1980s as a response to what its advocates considered to be the limitations of liberal theory and practice" (p. 455). The glossary: "the individual rights promoted by traditional liberals need to be balanced with social responsibilities" (p. 503).

**Two themes** (p. 455): "(i) the individual rights that have been vigorously promoted by traditional liberals need to be balanced with social responsibilities; and (ii) autonomous individual selves do not exist in isolation but are shaped by the values and culture of communities". Communitarians argue the language of rights "impedes genuine discussion and is employed without a corresponding sense of responsibilities" (Etzioni, Glendon, p. 440). They still support traditional liberal rights and their extension in non-democratic or discriminatory regimes, but locate rights within community.

**The 'I' and the 'We'.** Liberal theories of justice and economic and political theory presume an autonomous, unencumbered self. "Communitarians, in contrast, shift the balance and argue that the 'I' is constituted through the 'We' in a dynamic tension" (p. 441). In this purist form, this is not an argument for restoring traditional, authoritarian communities.

**The stakes.** "Unless we redress the balance towards the pole of community, our society will continue to become normless, self-centred and driven by special interests and power seeking" (p. 455) — echoing Durkheim's anomie and egoism.

**Etzioni's agenda** (1993, 1995) emphasized moral voice, family, schools and community responsibilities, and influenced New Labour (Chapter 18) — though Hopkins Burke argues New Labour "embraced and distorted" it with a strong, directive central state (p. 453).

**Radical egalitarian communitarianism.** This variant "focus[es] on inequality, deprivation and the market economy as causes of crime and promote[s] policies to eliminate poverty, which they define as a degree of deprivation that seriously impairs participation in society" (p. 455; glossary p. 507). Communities cannot exercise responsibility, and individuals cannot be expected to meet obligations, where structural deprivation excludes them from participation. It thus combines the communitarian emphasis on mutual obligation with the structural concerns of the victimized actor model and left realism.

## Software lens

Open-source and engineering communities are a natural laboratory for communitarian ideas. Licences and platform terms emphasize individual rights (fork it, use it, leave), but sustainable projects need responsibilities — maintainership, reviewing others' code, upholding conduct norms — and contributors' identities are shaped by the community ("the 'I' is constituted through the 'We'"). Etzioni-style communitarianism pushes responsibilities; radical egalitarian communitarianism asks whether everyone can actually participate — whether unpaid maintainers, contributors in low-income regions or people without free time are excluded so that "responsibility" becomes a burden only the comfortable can bear.
