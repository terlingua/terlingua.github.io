## Learning objectives

- Explain what left realism is and why it is not a strict integration of theories
- Explain why left realists said it was time to 'take crime seriously'
- Explain the distinction between concentrated and widespread effects of crime and the importance of incivilities
- Define the balance of intervention and describe the four corners and relationships of the square of crime

## The ideas

**Not integration but recognition.** "Left realism is not really an attempt to integrate and synthesize elements from different theories but is more a recognition of the validity of explanatory elements contained in each of the three models of crime and criminal behaviour and their practical value as part of a comprehensive strategy" (p. 361). "Essentially, all the left realists are really saying is that there is something to be said for most explanations of crime... The problem with most theorists... is that, by occupying entrenched positions on the causes of crime, they are not able to step back and look at the wider picture" (p. 349). The glossary: "a response to populist conservatism and right realism that proposes the need for a balance of intervention to address both the crime and the conditions that have caused it" (p. 505).

**Taking crime seriously.** Some left criminologists — notably Lea and Young (1984) — broke with left idealism and argued "a 'new realist' view on crime was necessary and that it was time to 'take crime seriously'" (p. 362). Crime is a real problem. "Corporate crime is important but its effects are generally widespread, while those of direct-contact crime are concentrated" (p. 362): "the real problem for those living in high-crime areas is posed by predatory offenders in their midst" (p. 346). People in such areas suffer offences "they may or may not report to the police", plus "a range of incivilities, such as vandalism, threats, vulgarity, sexual harassment, noise and swearing, all of which taken together further reduce quality of life and increase despair" (p. 362). Victimization surveys (e.g. the Islington Crime Survey) documented this. Left realists explain crime through **relative deprivation** (Merton revisited: deprivation relative to expectations), **subculture** (collective solutions) and **marginalization** (exclusion from economic and political participation).

**Balance of intervention.** "Crime requires a comprehensive solution where there must be a 'balance of intervention'. Both crime and the causes of crime must thus be tackled" (p. 362): "on the one hand, crime must be tackled and criminals must take responsibility for their actions and, on the other hand, the social conditions that encourage crime must also be tackled" (glossary, pp. 502–3).

**The square of crime** (Lea, 1992; Young, 1987) — "a reminder that crime is the outcome of a number of lines of force, and intervention to prevent it must take place at different levels" (p. 348). Four corners (p. 349):

1. **The state / criminal justice system** — front-line agents label individuals and groups, a major factor in recidivism.
2. **The victim** — may encourage offenders through inadequate defence or precipitate crime through lifestyle, and determines its impact.
3. **Society / the general public** — informal and formal social control.
4. **Offenders** — number, rate of offending, types of crime.

Crime is produced by the corners *and their relationships*: **state-society interplay** (police–public relations), **state-offender interplay** (criminal justice actions) and so on. Young: control requires intervention "at the level of the factors which give rise to the putative offender (such as structural unemployment), the informal system (such as lack of public mobilisation), the victim (such as inadequate target hardening), and the formal system (such as ineffective policing)" (p. 349). Lea later called it the "social relations of crime control" (p. 348).

## Software lens

The square of crime is a ready-made framework for security programmes: **offenders** (threat actors and what produces them — e.g. economic incentives for ransomware), **victims** (users and systems — target hardening, MFA), **the public/community** (users reporting phishing, open-source maintainers, coordinated disclosure communities) and **the state/formal system** (security team, law enforcement, platform enforcement — with the risk of mislabelling). Effective programmes work on all four corners *and* their relationships; e.g. a hostile relationship between the security team and engineers (state-society interplay) blocks the flow of information, just as poor police–public relations do. The concentrated-versus-widespread point maps to prioritization: a vendor's diffuse licence violation matters, but a credential-stuffing campaign hitting your users is concentrated, immediate harm.
