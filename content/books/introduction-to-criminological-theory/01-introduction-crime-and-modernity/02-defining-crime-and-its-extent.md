## Learning objectives

- Explain why "an act that contravenes the criminal law" is the simplest but a problematic definition of crime
- Distinguish *mala in se* from *mala prohibita*
- Explain what it means to call crime a social construction, with examples of language and reclassification
- Explain why criminology has focused on the lower classes and neglected white-collar and corporate crime
- Distinguish white-collar, corporate and organizational crime

## The ideas

**Moral and legal definitions.** Defining crime as "the doing of wrong" fails both ways: some immoral things (poverty, social deprivation) are not crimes, and some crimes (parking on a yellow line, some tax evasion) are not widely seen as immoral. The legal definition — "an act that contravenes the criminal law" (p. 5) — is simpler, but many people break the law without being regarded as 'criminals'.

**Mala in se and mala prohibita.** English law distinguishes acts "wrong in themselves" (murder, theft, serious assault), often treated as 'real' crimes, from acts *mala prohibita*, prohibited "not because they are morally wrong but for the protection of the public" (p. 5) — health, safety and pollution regulation enforced through criminal law because that is the most effective lever.

**Social construction.** Legal definitions "change over time and vary across culture" (p. 6). Cannabis in the UK moved from Class B to C in 2004 and back to B in 2009; stalking, racially motivated crime and knowingly passing on HIV were newly criminalized. Language does construction work too: Mars (1982) contrasts 'hard' words (*crime*, *theft*) with 'soft' ones (*fiddle*, *perk*); "creative accounting" sounds less criminal than "fraud"; deaths from safety failures are called "accidents" or "disasters". The book concludes: "The way that crime is defined is therefore a social construction and part of the political processes" (p. 6).

**The usual suspects.** Crime is associated with particular groups — young men, the unemployed — some of whom become 'folk devils'. Media and policy 'crack down' on burglary and street crime, not pollution or corporate fraud. Most criminological research, and so most theory, has studied the lower socio-economic groups, the 'dangerous class' that frightened respectable society once prison replaced execution and transportation.

**The neglect of white-collar and corporate crime.** Recognized since the early twentieth century, it remains under-researched and often atheoretical. Estimates cited: US white-collar economic losses about ten times those of 'ordinary' economic crime (Conklin, 1977); 100,000 deaths a year from occupational disease linked to wilful violation of safety laws (Swartz, 1975); 30,000 from defective products (Kramer, 1984). Croall adds that corporate crime is larger *and longer lasting* in effect.

**Definitions.** Sutherland (1947): "a crime committed by a person of respectability and high social status in the course of his occupation" (p. 7) — but this is narrow. White-collar crime also covers an employee committing crime *against* their organization or a self-employed person evading tax. **Corporate crime** involves "illegal acts carried out in the furtherance of the goals of an organization" (p. 7). Schraeger and Short's **organizational crime** adds *omission* (failure to act) and requires serious physical or economic impact. The Schwendingers go further, to harms that breach human rights even if not prohibited — a line Chapter 12's zemiology extends.

## Software lens

An insider who exfiltrates customer data for personal gain commits white-collar crime against the organization. A company that knowingly ships a product with disabled safety checks to hit a launch date, or that suppresses a breach disclosure to protect the share price, is closer to corporate or organizational crime — the act serves the organization's goals. "Incident", "outage" and "data exposure" are soft words; "negligence", "concealment" and "fraud" are hard ones. Which word appears in the postmortem is itself a construction decision. Many security and privacy rules (data retention limits, export controls) are *mala prohibita*: they are wrong because they are prohibited, not because the act itself is evil.
