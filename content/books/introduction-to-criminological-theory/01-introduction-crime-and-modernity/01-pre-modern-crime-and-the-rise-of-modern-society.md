## Learning objectives

- Explain how crime was understood before the modern age: natural law, sin and demonology
- Describe pre-modern criminal justice, including the 'bloody code' and discretionary mercy
- Name the four intellectual origins of modernity and its economic, political and cultural features
- Explain why almost every theory in the book is called a *modern* theory

## The ideas

Hopkins Burke opens with a claim that frames the whole book: the theories it covers are products of roughly the last two hundred years and of a way of life called **modernity**. To see what is distinctive about them, you first need the contrast case.

**Pre-modern explanation.** For more than a thousand years European thought explained crime spiritually. Thomas Aquinas taught that a God-given *natural law* inclines people toward good, so an offender was both a criminal and a sinner who damaged his own 'humanness'. Popular thought went further with **demonology**: offenders were possessed by demons and driven to act. The author points out that the *logic* of demonology — the offender is pushed by forces beyond their control — survives in secular form in the predestined actor model of Part Two.

**Pre-modern justice.** Justice was "chaotic, predominantly non-codified, irrational and irregular, and at the whim of individual judgement" (p. 3). In England from the seventeenth to the early eighteenth century the propertied class protected itself through the **'bloody code'**: by 1800 more than 250 offences, many of them minor property crimes, carried the death penalty. The code was terror delivered as public theatre. Yet it was rarely applied in full: landowning judges and magistrates handed out mercy based on character references, piety or whim. Severity plus arbitrary discretion was itself a tool of power.

**The rise of modern society.** Modernity grew from four sources: humanism and Protestantism (the 'protestant ethic' that success in *this* world follows hard work, and a challenge to divine right); the seventeenth-century scientific revolution (nature runs on discoverable laws); the eighteenth-century **Enlightenment** (society can be explained and governed by natural laws, built on individual rationality and free will); and the evident power of industrial society and science in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Its features cluster in three areas: **economic** (market production for profit, industrial technology, division of labour, wage labour), **political** (the centralized nation-state, bureaucracy, systematic surveillance, representative democracy) and **cultural** (tradition challenged in the name of reason, science and technical knowledge).

**A caution from Garland.** Pre-modern people did have secular stories about crime — bad company, drink, idleness, neglectful parents. What they lacked was "a developed sense of differential explanation" (p. 5): an account of why *some* people offend and others do not. Modern criminology is, at its core, a project of differential explanation.

## Software lens

Think of the pre-modern system as an undocumented legacy codebase with one admin who can override any rule. Draconian defaults (death for stealing turnips) combined with ad hoc exceptions granted by whoever holds root. Modernity is the move to written specifications, consistent enforcement, audit trails and evidence-based debugging. Demonology is the "cosmic rays flipped a bit" explanation for every bug: it names a cause outside the system and so stops investigation. Notice, though, that "the developer was overwhelmed by forces beyond their control" is still a popular postmortem story — the shape of the explanation outlived its content.
