## Learning objectives

- Define theory, hypothesis and the relationship between theory and empirical research
- Explain what theories do with observed patterns
- Name the three models of crime and criminal behaviour and the two further parts of the book
- Explain why theories are presented chronologically within each model and why some cross model boundaries

## The ideas

**Theory is explanation.** Students are often intimidated by the word, but "Theory nevertheless means nothing more than 'explanation'" (p. 8): how, and above all *why*, we do things. Everyone theorizes daily when they generalize about the world — though everyday theories are often illogical and shaped by selective observation: "often we see what we want to see".

**Theory and research.** "Theories are logical constructions that explain natural phenomena" (p. 8). They are not always directly observable but can be supported or refuted by evidence. The bridge is the **hypothesis**: a testable proposition logically derived from a theory, which must be capable of being accepted or rejected. Theories "provide a framework for us to interpret the meanings of observed patterns" and "help us to determine when these patterns are meaningful and when they are not".

**The map.** The book has five parts:

1. **The rational actor model** (Chapters 2–4): people have free will and choose crime as they choose anything else; crime can be deterred by making it costly.
2. **The predestined actor model** (Chapters 5–8): positivism and determinism; crime is caused by biological, psychological or social factors outside the person's control; the remedy is treatment of the person or the environment.
3. **The victimized actor model** (Chapters 9–12): offenders make choices, but in conditions shaped by an unjust, unequal society that criminalizes the activities of the poor and powerless while ignoring those of the powerful.
4. **Integrated theories** (Chapters 13–18): deliberate attempts to cross model boundaries.
5. **Crime in an age of moral uncertainty** (Chapters 19–24): postmodern, cultural, risk-society, communitarian and penal-society perspectives that do not fit one tradition.

Within each model, theories appear in order of emergence, and each later theory tries "to revive, develop and/or rectify identified weaknesses" in its predecessors (p. 9). Boundaries blur over time: biologists come to accept environmental factors, some psychologists embrace choice. Where a theory could sit in more than one model, the book flags the ambiguity.

## Software lens

A theory is like an architecture model of a running system: you cannot "see" the model, but it predicts what the metrics should do. A hypothesis is the falsifiable check derived from it ("if the cache is the bottleneck, p99 latency will drop when we double its size"). A theory that cannot produce a failing test is not doing work. "We see what we want to see" is confirmation bias — the reason we pre-register what a canary must show before we call a rollout healthy. The three models map loosely onto three postmortem stances: the engineer chose badly (rational actor), the engineer was set up to fail by tooling, training or temperament (predestined actor), and the rules and metrics themselves were written by and for someone else (victimized actor).
