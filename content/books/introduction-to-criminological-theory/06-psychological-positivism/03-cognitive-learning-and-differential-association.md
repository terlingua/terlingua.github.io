## Learning objectives

- Explain why cognitive learning theories sit uneasily within the predestined actor model
- Describe Tarde's laws of imitation and Sutherland's differential association theory
- Explain Burgess and Akers's social learning theory and Akers's four concepts
- Describe cognitive behavioural interventions and their rationale

## The ideas

**A critique from within.** Cognitive learning theories have "their foundations in a fundamental critique of the predestined actor model": by observing responses to stimuli, we can infer the internal cognitive processes that produce them (p. 129). They incorporate "notions of creative thinking and thus choice; in many ways more akin to the rational actor model" (glossary, p. 503).

**Tarde.** Gabriel Tarde proposed that people imitate others in proportion to closeness of contact, that inferiors imitate superiors, and that new fashions displace old. His simple model of learning influenced Sutherland.

**Sutherland's differential association.** Sutherland first used the term in *The Professional Thief* (1937), describing how thieves were "restricted in their physical and social contacts to association with like-minded others" (p. 123). In 1939 he developed it into a general theory: crime is "a learned activity much like any other". The mature theory (with Cressey) holds that criminal behaviour is learned in interaction within intimate personal groups; learning includes techniques and also the direction of **motives, drives, rationalizations and attitudes**; a person becomes delinquent because of "an excess of definitions favourable to violation of law over definitions unfavourable" — weighted by **frequency, duration, priority and intensity** of associations. The glossary: offending occurs "when individuals acquire sufficient inclinations towards law-breaking that outweigh their associations with non-criminal tendencies" (p. 504). Because it explains learning in any class, Sutherland used it for white-collar crime too.

**Social learning theory.** Burgess and Akers (1968) rewrote differential association "in the language of operant conditioning": criminal behaviour can be learned in non-social reinforcing situations and through social interaction where others reinforce it (p. 125). Akers (1985) refocused on four concepts: **differential association** (the most important source of social learning — patterns of interaction favourable or unfavourable to offending), **definitions** (one's own attitudes and meanings about acts), **differential reinforcement** (the balance of anticipated or actual rewards and punishments) and **imitation** (modelling observed behaviour).

**Cognitive behavioural methods.** Cognitive learning theories led to behaviour-modification strategies widely used with offenders (Hollin), including **self-instructional training**, **'thought stopping'**, **emotional control training** and **problem-solving training** (p. 126). The rationale: change internal psychological and cognitive processes and behaviour will change. These became the basis of accredited offending-behaviour programmes.

## Software lens

Differential association is a remarkably good model of how engineering cultures transmit practices — good and bad. Juniors learn not only *techniques* (how to bypass a failing test) but *definitions* ("everyone skips the security review when shipping on Friday"), weighted by frequency, duration, priority (what you learn first sticks) and intensity (whom you respect). An excess of definitions favourable to cutting corners produces corner-cutting regardless of the written policy. Akers's differential reinforcement adds incentives: if shipping fast is rewarded and the bypass is never punished, the definitions are reinforced. Cognitive behavioural techniques have counterparts in blameless postmortem facilitation and structured problem-solving under pressure.
