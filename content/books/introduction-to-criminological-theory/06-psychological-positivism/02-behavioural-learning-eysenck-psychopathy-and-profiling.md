## Learning objectives

- Explain classical and operant conditioning as the basis of behavioural learning theories
- Describe Eysenck's three personality dimensions and his account of conscience as a conditioned reflex
- Define psychopathy (antisocial personality disorder) and summarize the evidence about its causes
- Explain offender profiling and serial murder

## The ideas

**Conditioning.** Behavioural learning theories propose "that criminal behaviour is conditioned learned behaviour" (glossary, p. 503). They draw on **Pavlov** (classical conditioning: a neutral stimulus paired with an unconditioned one comes to elicit the response) and **Skinner** (operant conditioning: behaviour followed by reinforcement increases, by punishment decreases).

**Eysenck (1970, 1977)** built a general theory of crime on conditioning. **Conscience is a conditioned reflex**: children learn anxiety associated with punished acts, and that anxiety later inhibits them. People differ in how readily they condition, depending on personality. Three dimensions, each a continuum (pp. 117–18):

- **Extroversion (E)** — impulsiveness and sociability; high E: outgoing, optimistic, impulsive. Extroverts are under-aroused, condition poorly and seek stimulation.
- **Neuroticism (N)** — high N: anxious, moody, highly sensitive; low N: stable.
- **Psychoticism (P)** — cold, aggressive, insensitive; Feldman notes its similarity to psychopathy.

High E combined with high N conditions especially poorly and is most associated with offending. Hans and Sybil Eysenck (1970) tested 178 incarcerated young offenders; the 122 later reconvicted scored significantly higher on extroversion. Criticisms: inconsistent findings, especially on E; detention itself may make people neurotic; personality tests are culturally loaded and do not explain why particular crimes occur.

**Psychopathy.** "Antisocial personality disorder appears to be a relatively recent term that is interchangeable with that of psychopathy"; definitions emphasize "an incapacity for loyalty, selfishness, irresponsibility, impulsiveness, inability to feel guilt and failure to learn from experience" (p. 119). Causes are contested: some find psychopathic children more often have psychopathic or alcoholic fathers, but Cleckley (1976) found many psychopaths came from happy families, and Hare (1970) notes most people from disturbed backgrounds do not develop APD. Physiological findings: low electrodermal reactivity, 'gating out' unpleasant input (Hollin), and Eysenck found APD patients mostly extroverted — consistent with poor conditioning. Cleckley adds that psychopaths may be found in legitimate occupations — "business, medicine and psychiatry" — and only come to official attention when they engage in proscribed activities (p. 120).

**Offender profiling.** Personality typing "has been used, particularly in the USA, to help detect particular types of criminals and it is a method found to have been most useful in the detection of serial murders" (p. 121) — though not new: Dr Thomas Bond profiled Jack the Ripper in 1888. **Serial murder** is "a repetitive event where the murderer kills at a number of different times, frequently spanning a matter of months or years, and often at different locations" (glossary, p. 508).

## Software lens

Operant conditioning is the engine behind every gamified product and every badly designed incentive. On-call engineers who are praised for heroic firefighting and ignored for quiet prevention are being conditioned toward firefighting. Eysenck's under-aroused extrovert is the engineer who seeks novelty and finds routine maintenance aversive; the useful response is to channel that toward incident response or exploratory work rather than to moralize. Cleckley's point about psychopathic traits in respected professions is directly relevant to insider risk and leadership: the same traits (charm, low guilt, impulsiveness) can be rewarded in some corporate cultures until they cross into fraud. Profiling's narrow success (serial murder) is a caution against generalizing a technique from the domain where it was validated.
