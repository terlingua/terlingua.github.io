## Learning objectives

- Summarize the shared assumptions and weaknesses of psychological positivism
- State its policy implications, from psychoanalysis to cognitive behavioural programmes
- Explain the overlap with sociological explanations and with choice

## The ideas

**Shared assumptions.** All variants locate the cause of crime inside the individual's mind — personality, conditioning, unconscious conflict, learned cognition — and treat crime as something to be diagnosed and changed. Like biological positivism, they accept the legal definition of crime as given and presume offenders differ from non-offenders.

**Weaknesses.**

- *Testability*: psychodynamic concepts (unconscious guilt, latent delinquency) are difficult to falsify.
- *Samples*: many studies use institutionalized or clinic samples (Bowlby's 44 thieves, Eysenck's prisoners), so findings may reflect the institution or selection.
- *Reverse causation*: detention may produce the traits measured.
- *Inconsistent evidence*: personality findings, especially on extroversion, vary between studies; psychopathy's origins are contested.
- *Social blind spots*: individual explanations neglect class, opportunity and power — though learning theories (Sutherland, Akers) bridge to sociology and differ "only in emphasis from sociological perspectives" (p. 53).

**Policy implications** (p. 127 and summary pp. 128–9). If the cause is psychological, the response is treatment: psychoanalytic therapy and supportive environments (Aichhorn) for weak conscience; family support and continuity of care (Bowlby, Rutter); behaviour modification using reinforcement and punishment; token economies; and, from cognitive learning theory, "a range of behaviour modification treatment strategies introduced with the intention of changing behaviour" (summary point 10) — the cognitive behavioural programmes widely used in probation and prison. Offender profiling serves investigation. The more individualistic variants also lend themselves to identifying 'at risk' children for early intervention — with the same risks of labelling and intrusion noted for biological positivism.

## Software lens

The psychological positivist toolkit — assess the individual, diagnose, then train or treat — is how many organizations respond to repeated human error: mandatory training, coaching plans, personality-based hiring filters. The weaknesses transfer too: training data drawn only from people who already failed (selection), outcomes measured after the intervention changed them (reverse causation), and blindness to the environment that produced the error. Learning theories are the bridge: behaviour is learned in context, so changing team norms and reinforcement often works better than treating individuals in isolation.
