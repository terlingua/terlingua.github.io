## Learning objectives

- Explain Freud's id, ego and superego and his two models of criminal behaviour

- Describe Bowlby's maternal deprivation thesis, its evidence and Rutter's critique
- Summarize research on discipline, broken homes and fatherless families

## The ideas

Psychological positivism locates crime in the **mind** rather than the body: "people commit crime because of internal psychological factors over which they have little or no control; there is a criminal personality" (glossary, p. 507).

**Freud.** Psychoanalysis rests on the claim that sexuality is present from birth. Personality has three parts: the **id** (instinctive drives demanding satisfaction), the **ego** (reality-oriented mediator) and the **superego** (conscience, internalized parental and social standards). Behaviour reflects the conflict between the demands of the id and the inhibitions of the superego. Freud proposed **two models of criminal behaviour** (p. 112): first, some offences (arson, shoplifting, some sexual offences) reflect **mental disturbance or illness** arising in psychosexual development — a model that, the book notes, reduces parents' responsibility; second, offenders possess a **'weak conscience'**. Because morality is linked to guilt, a well-developed conscience inhibits offending; paradoxically, some people with excessive unconscious guilt may offend to *be punished*.

**Aichhorn (1925).** A child is born in an 'asocial state' with drives demanding satisfaction. If drives are suppressed rather than socialized, the child is in **'latent delinquency'**, which outside provocation can activate (p. 113). Many offenders had underdeveloped consciences resulting from the absence of intimate attachment to parents; the remedy was a happy, pleasurable environment to build identification with adults. The glossary: "the absence of an intimate attachment with parents could lead to later criminality" (p. 505).

**Bowlby (1952).** Offending occurs "when a child has not enjoyed a close and continuous relationship with its mother during its formative years" (p. 113). He compared 44 juvenile thieves at a child guidance clinic with a matched clinic control group, finding more early maternal separation among the thieves and an 'affectionless' character. Criticisms: small, clinic-based samples; no data on maternal deprivation among non-offenders in general. **Rutter (1981)** argued the *stability and quality* of care matter more than an absence of breaks; a small number of substitutes can provide good mothering without adverse effect.

**Discipline and family.** The Gluecks found offenders' fathers' discipline lax and inconsistent, with physical punishment; McCord et al. (1959) agreed consistency mattered more than strictness; Bandura and Walters (1959) found fathers of aggressive boys punished aggression at home but approved it outside. Broken home studies were inconclusive — the nature of the problem (break-up, supervision, low income) was unclear. **Maginnis (1997)** concluded children of single-parent families are more likely to have behavioural problems because they lack economic security and parental time, and "the most reliable indicator of violent crime in a community is the proportion of fatherless families" (p. 129).

## Software lens

Psychodynamic ideas are hard to test — a recurring criticism — but the attachment findings have a workplace echo. Rutter's correction is the important one: it is not the absence of interruptions that protects people but the stability and quality of the relationships they do have. New engineers rotated constantly between managers and teams with no stable mentor show more "unexplained" problems, and a small number of consistent substitutes can work fine. McCord's finding transfers too: consistent enforcement of norms matters more than how harsh the norms are; inconsistent "rules apply at home but not outside" signals (Bandura and Walters) teach the opposite of what is intended.
