## Learning objectives

- Explain Sellin's culture conflict and the difference between primary and secondary conflicts
- Explain Vold's group conflict theory

- State Quinney's propositions on the social reality of crime
- Summarize the criticisms of conflict theories

## The ideas

Conflict and radical theories "explain crime and criminal behaviour in terms of the unequal nature of the socio-political structure of society" (p. 225). The glossary describes conflict theories as a variant of the victimized actor model in which definitions of criminality "are made by those with control of authority in a pluralist but equal society" (p. 503) — pluralist conflict, not Marxist class war.

**Thorsten Sellin (1938)**: each culture establishes its own **conduct norms**, embedded through socialization. **Primary conflicts** occur *between* cultures — through border conflicts, territorial extension or, typically, migration. **Secondary conflicts** occur *within* a culture when subcultures with their own conduct norms develop; laws usually represent the dominant culture's norms, so members of subcultures may be criminalized for following their own (p. 208).

**George Vold (1958)** "emphasized the group nature of society and stressed the fact that groups compete with each other in order to secure what they identify as their interests" (p. 225). Groups seek state support to protect their interests; lawmaking, law-breaking and enforcement reflect group conflict, and those who lose the legislative struggle find their behaviour criminalized. Much crime is the behaviour of minority-power groups.

**Austin Turk**, building on Dahrendorf, "saw the social order as the outcome of powerful social groups who successfully control society in their own interests", providing "a normative justification for law, which is then enforced by controlling agencies such as the police" (p. 225). People with an unclear view of how their behaviour affects others, especially the powerful, are most likely to be caught — which explains why young people fall foul of the law (p. 209). Control is exercised by **coercion** (physical force) and by the **control of legal images and living time**: the powerful shape how law is perceived, and as generations grow up under a system it comes to seem natural, so resistance fades.

**Richard Quinney** "considered crime to be the product of legal definitions constructed through the exercise of political power" (p. 225). His six propositions (1970) on **the social reality of crime** (p. 210): crime is a definition of behaviour created by authorized agents in a politically organized society; criminal definitions describe behaviour conflicting with the interests of segments with power to shape public policy; they are applied by segments with power to enforce law; behaviour patterns are structured in relation to criminal definitions, and people in segments with less power are more likely to act in ways defined as criminal; conceptions of crime are constructed and diffused through communication; and the social reality of crime is constructed by the formulation and application of definitions, the development of related behaviour patterns and the construction of criminal conceptions.

**Criticisms** (pp. 210–11): conflict theory explains *lawmaking* better than *law-breaking*; it struggles with crimes condemned across all groups (murder, rape); it underplays consensus on core criminal law; it often treats groups as homogeneous; and its pluralism understates how far power is concentrated — the opening for radical, Marxist theories.

## Software lens

Sellin's secondary conflict is visible when an acquired company's engineering culture (its own conduct norms around deploys, reviews or open-sourcing) collides with the parent's policies: engineers acting normally by their own norms are suddenly "in violation". Vold and Turk describe internal politics: whichever group wins the policy argument (security, platform, product) gets its interests written into the rules, and the losing group's habits become violations. Turk's "control of legal images and living time" explains why a contested policy stops being contested after a few years — new hires never knew another way.
