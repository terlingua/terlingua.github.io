## Learning objectives

- Explain peacemaking criminology's critique of US criminal justice and its three traditions
- Describe Quinney's spiritual framing of the causes of crime and Fuller's peacemaking pyramid
- Explain restorative justice as peacemaking's central proposal
- State the criticisms and the policy implications of conflict and radical theories

## The ideas

**Peacemaking criminology** is "a more contemporary variant of the radical tradition" signalled by Pepinsky and Quinney's *Criminology as Peacemaking* (1991), drawing on three traditions: **religious and humanistic**, **feminist** and **critical** (p. 217). It argues "the whole of the US criminal justice system is based on the continuance of violence and oppression (as seen in the prison system); war (as seen in the 'war on crime' and the 'war on drugs'); and the failure to account for how the larger social system contributes to the problem of crime (as seen in the failure to reduce poverty in society)" (p. 226). Criminology should promote social equity and move "away from traditional criminal justice to restorative justice" (p. 218).

**Quinney's framing** of aetiology is spiritual, Buddhist-influenced: Western rational thought is limited; "Human existence is characterized by suffering; crime is suffering; and the sources of suffering are within each of us"; "Crime can be ended only with the ending of suffering, only when there is peace - through the love and compassion found in awareness" (1991: 3–4, quoted p. 218). A coherent unifying theory remains "one of the greatest challenges".

**Fuller's peacemaking pyramid** (2003) has six stages (pp. 218–20):

1. **Non-violence** — opposition to violence and capital punishment.
2. **Social justice** — opposing structural injustice such as racism and sexism.
3. **Inclusion** — involving victims, offenders and communities in responses.
4. **Correct means** — due process and fair procedures; outcomes cannot justify coercive means.
5. **Ascertainable criteria** — rules and processes clear to all participants.
6. **Categorical imperative** — treat others as you would have them treat you; act as you would want all to act.

**Responses to critics.** Akers and Gibbons ask how the grand changes would be achieved; Gibbons: the Pepinsky and Quinney volume "has little to say about how the grand-scale changes they propose might be achieved" (p. 221). Peacemakers point to structural reforms already normal in Europe — welfare, health care, family support — which "To the average European... are not radical but mainstream" (p. 222).

**Restorative justice** is "the single most important proposal": restructuring much of criminal justice so offenders, victims and communities address the harm, repair relationships and reintegrate the offender, rather than inflicting pain (p. 222). Peacemaking has been viewed as "'utopian', 'soft on crime', 'unrealistic' and 'just not feasible'" (p. 226).

**Policy implications of conflict and radical theories** (p. 224): reduce inequality and redistribute power; decriminalize behaviours of the powerless that harm no one; pursue the crimes of the powerful; restrain discriminatory enforcement; and, for peacemakers, non-violent restorative alternatives.

## Software lens

Fuller's pyramid reads like a design checklist for incident and conduct processes. Non-violence: no punitive humiliation. Social justice: check whether enforcement falls disproportionately on some groups. Inclusion: involve those affected — the customer, the on-call engineer, the team. Correct means: a fair process even when the outcome seems obvious. Ascertainable criteria: written, understandable rules. Categorical imperative: would we accept this process if applied to us? Restorative practice is how good blameless postmortems already work: focus on harm and repair, involve affected parties, reintegrate rather than exclude. The "soft on crime" criticism has its counterpart too — blameless culture is often mocked as consequence-free; the answer is that accountability through repair is still accountability.
