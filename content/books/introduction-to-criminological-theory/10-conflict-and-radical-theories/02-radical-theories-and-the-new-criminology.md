- State the criticisms of radical theories and the later CCCS reworking

## The ideas

**Radical criminology** is "usually informed by some version of Marxist socio-economic theory" and proposes "that there are deeply inherent inequalities in society that provide those with economic and political power with the opportunity to criminalize the activities of the poor and powerless" (glossary, p. 507). The difference from conflict theory: inequality is structural and rooted in capitalism, not one of many group contests.

**Precursors.** Marx and Engels saw the lumpenproletariat as unreliable for revolution. **Wilhelm Bonger (1916)**: capitalism breeds social irresponsibility and egoism; crime rises with unemployment in recessions; offenders act from self-interest rather than social interest (p. 212). **Rusche and Kirchheimer (1939)**: forms of punishment correspond to the labour market (developed in Chapter 23).

**US radicals.** **Chambliss (1969)** argued "criminal justice bureaucracies tend to deal with members of the lower social classes more harshly than with other people because the latter have little to offer in return for leniency, while they are in no position to fight the system" (p. 226). Pressure to plead guilty leads the powerless to surrender trial "in nine cases out of ten"; much legal effort processes those least likely to be deterred (addicts, murderers) while those most deterrable (white-collar offenders) are rarely pursued. **Quinney's** later Marxist work saw criminal law as an instrument of the ruling class. **Spitzer (1975)**: capitalism's surplus populations are managed as **'social junk'** (skid-row alcoholics, not threatening) or **'social dynamite'** (threatening, proto-revolutionary) (p. 214); controlling them deepens fiscal crisis.

**UK: The New Criminology.** Radical young criminologists at the **New Deviancy Conference** (York, late 1960s) — Rock, Downes, Laurie Taylor, Stan Cohen, Ian Taylor, Jock Young — questioned orthodox criminology's role in legitimating inequality. They wanted to "restore some dignity to the deviant person" — neither the "'poor wee things'" of positivism nor the pathological creatures of right-wing criminology, but "knowing people responding rationally, albeit sometimes rebelliously, to their circumstances" (p. 214). Influences: the Chicago School, Becker and labelling, phenomenology, anti-psychiatry (Laing). Taylor, Walton and Young's *The New Criminology* (1973) — the "seminal UK text" — attempted "to link the concerns of labelling theory with Marxism" (p. 225), calling for a **fully social theory of deviance** covering the wider origins of the act, its immediate origins, the act itself, the immediate and wider origins of social reaction, and the outcome of the reaction on further deviance. The glossary: "a theoretical synthesis of Marxism and labelling perspectives" (p. 506).

**Criticisms.** *The New Criminology* offered "a generalized prescription for a crime-free, socialist 'good society'" that now looks **utopian**, and its generality offered little substantive theory (p. 215). Radicals romanticized offenders, neglected the intra-class victimization of the poor, and offered few practical policies — criticisms that later turned some (Young, Lea) into left realists (Chapter 18), while others continued as critical criminologists (Chapter 12). The CCCS's *Policing the Crisis* (1978) reworked the utopian elements using Gramsci's concept of **hegemony** (p. 216).

## Software lens

Chambliss's observation that bureaucracies are harsher on those who "have little to offer in return for leniency" and cannot fight back has an uncomfortable parallel in enforcement at scale: platform policies are often applied strictly to small accounts and leniently to large customers who can escalate, threaten churn or hire lawyers. His point about deterrability is also instructive: enforcement effort concentrates on the least deterrable (impulsive individual abusers) while the most deterrable (organized, calculating, well-resourced violators) face little. The New Criminology's insistence on treating deviants as "knowing people responding rationally" to their circumstances is a good default when investigating why engineers bypass controls.
