## Learning objectives

- Explain what went wrong when the French tried to apply pure Classical principles
- Describe the neo-Classical revisions and who introduced them
- Explain why the neo-Classical compromise opened the door to positivism and to expert witnesses
- List the central attributes of the Classical and neo-Classical Schools and the criticism each attracts

## The ideas

**From theory to code.** Classical philosophy shaped the *Declaration of the Rights of Man* (1789) and the **French Penal Code of 1791**. Implementation exposed the flaw: the Classical theorists "had deliberately and completely ignored differences between individuals" (p. 40). First offenders and recidivists were treated identically; children, the 'feeble-minded' and the insane were treated "as if they were fully rational and competent". Defendants who could not understand the proceedings undermined the code's legitimacy, so it was revised in **1810 and 1819** to allow judges some latitude.

**The neo-Classical compromise.** Neo-Classicists such as **Rossi, Garraud and Joly** revised the doctrine of free will. Ordinary sane adults remain fully responsible, but children (and sometimes the elderly) are less capable of free choice, and the insane and 'feeble-minded' even less. This is "the beginnings of the recognition that various innate predisposing factors may actually determine human behaviour" (p. 40) — the theoretical seed of the predestined actor model.

**Experts enter the courtroom.** Doctors, psychiatrists and later social workers were admitted to assess biological, psychological and social differences and so the degree of responsibility. Sentences became **individualized**, depending on mitigating circumstances, and punishment "increasingly came to be expressed in terms of punishment appropriate to rehabilitation" (p. 41). Yet Taylor, Walton and Young note "no radical departure from the free-will model": choice remained a characteristic of the individual, with recognition that "certain structures are more conducive to free choice than others". Most modern criminal justice systems rest on this "somewhat awkward theoretical compromise" between free will and determinism.

**Central attributes** of the Classical/neo-Classical Schools (p. 41) and their exposure to criticism:

1. A focus on the criminal law and a **legal definition of crime** — exposed to the criticism that legal definitions are social constructions varying over time and place.
2. **Punishment should fit the crime, not the criminal** — exposed to the criticism that it ignores individual differences in culpability and rehabilitation prospects.
3. **Free will** allied to hedonistic utilitarianism — exposed to the evidence that rationality is limited and unequally distributed.

## Software lens

The 1791 Code is a strict validation rule shipped to production without edge-case testing: it handled the median user and failed badly for the inputs nobody modelled — children, the mentally ill. The 1810/1819 revisions are the hotfix that adds an override path, plus the requirement for specialist sign-off (the expert witness). Note what the compromise did to the architecture: once you allow overrides based on individual characteristics, you need a whole subsystem to assess those characteristics, and that subsystem (positivist criminology, psychiatry, probation) eventually grows into a rival paradigm. Every "just add an exception flag" eventually becomes its own service.
