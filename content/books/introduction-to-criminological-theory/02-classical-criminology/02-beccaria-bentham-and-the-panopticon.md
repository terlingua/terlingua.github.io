## The ideas

**Cesare Beccaria (1738–94)** wrote *Dei Delitti e Delle Pene* (*On Crimes and Punishments*), translated into 22 languages. A strong social contract theorist, he grounded criminal behaviour in **free will and hedonism**: "all human behaviour is essentially purposive and based on the pleasure-pain principle" (p. 36). So fixed sanctions must be written into law, not left to judicial discretion; the law applies equally to all; the court's only job is to determine guilt; no mitigation. The book summarizes thirteen propositions (p. 36), including:

- each person sacrifices part of their liberty to the state to escape chaos;
- the 'despotic spirit' — the tendency to offend — is in everyone;
- punishments are set by the legislature, not courts;
- seriousness is judged by **harm to society**, not the offender's intentions;
- punishment must be **proportional**, on a tariff;
- punishment must be **prompt** and **certain** to be effective;
- laws must be **well publicized**;
- punishment exists for deterrence, so **capital punishment is unnecessary**;
- **prevention is better than punishment**;
- what is not expressly prohibited is permitted.

**Jeremy Bentham**, Beccaria's leading disciple, influenced by Locke's denial of innate ideas and original sin, saw offenders not as monsters but as "'forward children', 'persons of unsound mind', who lacked the self-discipline to control their passions" (p. 37) — products of faulty upbringing. Punishment must outweigh the pleasure of crime "but the law must not be as harsh and severe as to reduce the greatest happiness", and law should control harmful acts, not regulate morality. He agreed capital punishment was barbaric but, unlike Beccaria, allowed that torture might occasionally have utility — the book contrasts torture to extract a confession (useless for finding the right offender) with torture to extract urgent information (a different, utilitarian rationale).

**Qualified free will.** Neither man held a pure free-will view. Beirne argues Beccaria's "is a determined will, rather than a free will" (p. 37), and Bentham wrote that "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure". Beirne concludes Beccaria held agency "simultaneously involving 'free' rational calculation and 'determined' action" (p. 38). Early *practice*, however, presumed full rationality and responsibility.

**The Panopticon** (spelled 'Panoptican' in the book), published 1791: a circular tiered honeycomb of cells around a central inspection tower, so constant visibility made chains unnecessary. Prisoners would work sixteen hours a day for the owner's profit. Bentham called it "a 'mill for grinding rogues honest'" (p. 38), to stand near the city centre as a reminder of the 'fruits of crime', and proposed the same **inspection principle** for schools, asylums, workhouses, factories and hospitals. Ignatieff (1978) argues these institutions formed one interlocking disciplinary structure whose pains were "calibrated in an ascending scale, school-workhouse-asylum-prison" and which closely resembled the factory. The strict design was never built in England; Millbank (1812) was a failed variant.

## Software lens

Beccaria reads like a spec for a fair rules engine: rules defined centrally (legislature, not each judge), published to everyone, applied identically, triggered promptly and reliably, with severity proportional to measured *impact* rather than guessed intent. "Everything not forbidden is permitted" is an allowlist-versus-denylist choice made in favour of liberty. The Panopticon is the original observability-as-control design: if subjects believe they may be watched at any time, you need fewer hard restraints. Engineers who design workplace monitoring, keystroke logging or "productivity" dashboards are reusing Bentham's inspection principle — and Ignatieff's warning about where that leads.
