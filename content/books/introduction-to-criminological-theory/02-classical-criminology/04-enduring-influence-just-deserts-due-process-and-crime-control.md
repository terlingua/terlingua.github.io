## Learning objectives

- Explain how the Classical School survived the rise of positivism
- State the four principles of the just deserts approach
- Contrast Packer's due process and crime control models
- Explain the policy implications of Classicism and the problem of a "mathematics of deterrence"

## The ideas

The rational actor model "went out of fashion as an explanatory model of criminal behaviour at the end of the nineteenth century but continued to inform criminal justice systems throughout the world" (p. 45). Its enduring legacies are the sentencing tariff, just deserts and due process.

**Just deserts** (Von Hirsch, 1976) has four principles (p. 42): only a person found guilty by a court can be punished; anyone found guilty must be punished; punishment must not be *more* than commensurate with the gravity of the offence and culpability; and must not be *less*. It "eschews individual discretion and rehabilitation as legitimate aims of the justice system. Justice must be both done and seen to be done" (p. 42).

**Packer's two models.** Packer (1968) argues the whole criminal justice system rests on "a balance between the competing value systems of due process and crime control" (p. 42).

- **Due process**: the system's purpose is to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt in a public trial before punishment. It assumes an idealized rule of law and the **presumption of innocence**, requires rules on police powers and evidence, distrusts informal discretion, and accepts that some guilty people go free because wrongful conviction and arbitrary state power are worse evils. Its weakness: high acquittal rates can look like failure and may not deter.
- **Crime control**: prioritizes efficiency and results — catching, convicting, punishing. There is almost a 'presumption of guilt', legal safeguards are seen as obstacles, the occasional sacrificed innocent is acceptable if minimized, and victims' and society's interests come first because swift processing is believed to deter.

When the rational actor model revived with the new right (Chapter 3), "the purist Classical tradition of 'due process' promoted in particular by Beccaria would be very much superseded by the interests of the proponents of the crime control model" (p. 43).

**Policy implications.** If people choose crime, the policy question is how to use the state to influence them not to. But the theory "is lacking in sophistication" and was operationalized assuming "a mathematics of deterrence" — a proportional calculation done first by policy-makers and then by would-be offenders (p. 44). We are not all the same, nor do we agree on what a rational decision to offend is. And certainty of punishment requires effective detection: "if the 'certainty of punishment' is to be achieved, there must be a major investment in policing".

## Software lens

Due process versus crime control is the false-positive versus false-negative trade-off. An intrusion-detection or fraud system tuned for crime control blocks fast and accepts collateral damage to legitimate users; one tuned for due process requires strong evidence, an appeal path and audit logs, and accepts that some attackers slip through. Neither is "correct"; every system sits at some point on the curve, and the choice is a value judgement. Just deserts is the policy that every confirmed violation gets *exactly* its tariff — no more for the unpopular, no less for the VIP. The "mathematics of deterrence" problem is familiar too: a penalty schedule designed by the policy team assumes every engineer reads it and computes the same expected cost.
