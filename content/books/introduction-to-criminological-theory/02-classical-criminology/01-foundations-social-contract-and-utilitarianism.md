## The ideas

The Part One introduction opens with James Q. Wilson's observation that "the average citizen" thinks crime rises when it becomes more profitable and falls when penalties are "swifter, more certain, or more severe" (p. 29). That commonsense view is the rational actor model, and it has two intellectual roots.

**Social contract theory**: legitimate government exists only by the voluntary agreement of free people exercising free will.

- **Hobbes (1588–1679)** had a bleak view: in a 'state of nature' there is a "war of all against all" and life is "nasty, brutish and short" (p. 30). People should freely submit to an absolute power — the *Leviathan* — which enforces the contracts they make. Compliance can be enforced by fear of punishment, *but only if* entry into the contract was freely willed. Here the book locates "the origins of the very idea of modern criminal justice systems".
- **Locke (1632–1704)** saw natural law protecting rights to life, liberty and property. Money allowed accumulation beyond use and generated inequality and conflict, so people agree (unanimously) to form a community, (by majority) to set up institutions, and (as property owners) to consent to taxes. Unlike Hobbes, rights cannot be surrendered to arbitrary power; government exists to give natural rights "precision, clarity and impartial enforceability" (p. 31).
- **Rousseau (1712–1778)** thought people began as free, equal 'noble savages'; society made them competitive and selfish. His solution is the **general will**: citizens acting as a whole, freely adopting laws that apply equally to all. Sovereignty belongs to the citizen body, not kings.

From these the book draws four assumptions (p. 32): humans once lived in a state of innocence or nature; leaving it required *reason* exercised by responsible individuals; the *will* is a real, generally free faculty that controls behaviour; and society has a *right to punish*, transferred to the state as a code of criminal law. Humans are therefore "rational actors" who freely contract, and laws properly approved by citizens may legitimately compel compliance.

**Utilitarianism** judges acts and policies by their tendency to promote happiness. **Bentham (1748–1832)**: happiness is pleasure, unhappiness pain; the goal is "the greatest happiness of the greatest number". Pleasures and pains are weighed by intensity, duration and proximity (and extent, the number affected), in a person-neutral calculation of the net balance (pp. 32–3). **Mill (1806–1873)** accepted hedonism but distinguished *qualities* of pleasure (intellectual above sensual) and made social consequences, not pure self-interest, the criterion — useful refinements that are hard to measure.

Bentham's two additions to social contract theory are the ones that matter for criminology: the principal control over free will is **fear, especially fear of pain**, and **punishment is the main way to create that fear** (p. 33).

## Software lens

A social contract is a terms-of-service you actually agreed to: enforcement is legitimate *because* you consented. Hobbes is the "one admin with root, because otherwise chaos" model; Locke is "admins exist to protect user rights and cannot confiscate data without consent"; Rousseau is governance by the whole community (RFCs, open voting) where rules bind everyone equally. Bentham's calculus is a cost function: intensity, duration, proximity and extent are weights. The design question his heirs still argue about — whether people really evaluate the function — returns in every chapter of Part One.
