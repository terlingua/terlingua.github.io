## The ideas

All deviant subculture explanations "share a common perception that certain social groups have values and attitudes that enable or encourage delinquency" (p. 164). Mostly positivist, they explain why (predominantly young) people offend in company "for whom this is seen as the normal thing to do" (glossary, p. 504). Antecedents include Durkheim, Merton, Sutherland and the Chicago School gang studies (Thrasher: gangs grow from street playgroups in disorganized slums).

**Albert Cohen (1955).** The working-class boy is judged at school by a "middle-class measuring rod" — alien norms he cannot meet — and experiences "a deficit of respect and status frustration" (p. 148). Interacting with others in the same position, a collective solution emerges: a subculture with inverted values (**reaction formation**), where delinquency — often non-utilitarian, malicious, negativistic — earns status.

**Walter Miller (1958)** rejected the idea of reaction against middle-class values: lower-class culture itself generates delinquency. Its **focal concerns** — "toughness, smartness, excitement, fate and autonomy" (p. 148) — lead those who live by them to break the law almost automatically; 'one-sex peer units' (street gangs) provide belonging and status.

**Cloward and Ohlin (1960)** combined Merton's anomie (the 'push') with Sutherland's differential association and Shaw's cultural transmission (the 'pull'): illegitimate opportunities, like legitimate ones, are unequally distributed — an **illegitimate opportunity structure**. Three subcultures (p. 149): **criminal** (where organized adult crime offers apprenticeships), **conflict** (no criminal opportunities either, so frustration is worked off in assault, vandalism and gang fights) and **retreatist** ('double failures' who retreat into drugs and alcohol).

**Wolfgang and Ferracuti (1967)** — the **subculture of violence**: "an expectation that the receipt of a trivial insult should be met with violence; failure to respond in this way is greeted with social censure from the peer group" (glossary, p. 509).

**David Matza** provides "an influential and crucial link with later non-determinist explanations" (p. 164). Delinquents are not compelled; they **drift** "between delinquent and conventional behaviour", sometimes choosing delinquency and often not (p. 153). Delinquency is "a status and delinquents are role players" (glossary, p. 504). With Sykes, he identified **techniques of neutralization** that release young people from conventional controls (p. 154):

- **denial of responsibility** ("I didn't mean it")
- **denial of injury** ("I didn't really harm him")
- **denial of the victim** ("he deserved it")
- **condemnation of the condemners** ("they're all crooks anyway")
- **appeal to higher loyalties** ("I did it for my mates")

These are excuses, not explanations; underneath lies commitment to **subterranean values** — like Miller's focal concerns but existing in mainstream culture too, chiefly the need for stimulation and excitement.

## Software lens

Neutralization techniques are the vocabulary of every rationalized policy violation in tech: "I didn't mean to leak it, the tool defaulted public" (responsibility), "no real user data was exposed" (injury), "they're scraping us anyway" (victim), "security just blocks everything" (condemners), "I did it to unblock the team" (higher loyalties). Recognizing them in postmortems and code-review threads is a practical skill. Cloward and Ohlin's insight that *illegitimate* opportunity is also structured explains why insider abuse clusters where access, tooling and know-how exist. Matza's drift is a healthier model of most rule-breaking engineers than the "bad actor" frame: they are not committed deviants, they drift when neutralizations are available.
