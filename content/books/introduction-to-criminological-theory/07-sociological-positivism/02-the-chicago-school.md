## Learning objectives

- Explain the ecological approach of Park and Burgess and concentric zone theory
- Describe Shaw and McKay's findings and the idea of cultural transmission
- Describe the Chicago Area Project
- State the main criticisms of the Chicago School

## The ideas

The Chicago School — sociologists at the University of Chicago in the 1920s and 1930s — "developed the ecological explanation of crime that proposes that people engage in criminal activities because of determining factors in their immediate environment" (glossary, p. 503). Its central message: "the poor are not simply born into a life of crime but are driven by the conditions of their social environment. By changing their surroundings, it is possible to transform these people into law-abiding citizens" (p. 164).

**Human ecology.** Robert Park borrowed from plant ecology: cities are like natural environments where groups compete for space, invade and succeed one another. **Ernest Burgess** proposed that cities grow "radially in a series of concentric zones or rings" (p. 138), five zones distributed by competition: the central business district ('loop'); the **zone in transition**; working-class homes; middle-class residential areas; and outer commuter suburbs. The zone in transition — "rows of deteriorating tenements and often built in the shadow of ageing factories" — was constantly disrupted as the business district expanded, and received waves of immigrants too poor to live elsewhere. These patterns "weakened family and communal ties and resulted in 'social disorganization'".

**Shaw and McKay** (1931) mapped juvenile court records and confirmed "that offending behaviour flourished in the zone in transition and was inversely related to the affluence of the area and corresponding distance from the central business district" (p. 138). Crucially, crime stayed highest in the same slum neighbourhoods over decades *regardless of which ethnic group lived there*: as groups prospered and moved out, their crime rates fell, and newcomers' rose. Crime was a property of places, not peoples. Disorganized neighbourhoods "help produce and sustain criminal traditions that compete with conventional values and can be 'transmitted down through successive generations'" (p. 164) — **cultural transmission**.

**Social disorganization** has roots in Durkheim's idea that "imperfect social regulation leads to a variety of different social problems, including crime"; the Chicago School called "for efforts to reorganize communities to emphasize non-criminal activities" (glossary, p. 508).

**The Chicago Area Project (CAP)**, established by Clifford Shaw in the 1930s, gave local residents in deprived areas autonomy to organize neighbourhood committees against crime: recreation programmes, campaigns to improve the physical environment, and detached workers who mediated for young people with schools and courts (p. 139). Evaluations were mixed but it pioneered community-based prevention.

**Criticisms.** The 'ecological fallacy' of inferring individual behaviour from area data; reliance on official statistics that may reflect where police patrol; treating slum life as 'disorganized' when it may be differently organized (as subcultural theorists later argued); and neglect of the political and economic decisions (land use, investment) that create zones in the first place.

## Software lens

Shaw and McKay's central finding — high-crime areas stayed high-crime as populations changed — is exactly what you see with "cursed" services and codebases: the defect rate stays high as successive teams rotate through, because the problem lives in the architecture, the on-call load and the inherited norms, not in the people. Cultural transmission explains why: workarounds and shortcuts are taught to each new cohort. The Chicago Area Project's lesson is to give the people living in the "zone in transition" the autonomy and resources to reorganize it themselves, rather than parachuting in outside controls. Beware the ecological fallacy when reading team-level dashboards: a team's high incident rate says nothing about any individual engineer on it.
