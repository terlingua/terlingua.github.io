## Learning objectives

- Explain Merton's distinction between cultural goals and institutionalized means
- Describe the five modes of adaptation with examples
- Contrast Merton's anomie with Durkheim's
- Explain criticisms and later developments, including Messner and Rosenfeld's institutional anomie theory

## The ideas

**Goals and means.** Merton (1938) focused on the individual's position in the social structure. Every society has **cultural goals** (in the USA, above all material success — the 'American Dream') and **institutionalized means** of reaching them (education, hard work, legitimate employment). "The ideal situation is where there is a balance between goals and means so that those who conform feel that they are justly rewarded" (p. 164). Strain arises when a society urges everyone toward the same goal while "access to the means to achieve that goal is limited by the position of a person in the social structure" (p. 141).

**Five modes of adaptation** (pp. 141–3):

| Adaptation | Goals | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conformity** | accept | accept | the law-abiding majority |
| **Innovation** | accept | reject | theft, fraud, drug dealing — success by illegitimate means; also much white-collar crime |
| **Ritualism** | reject (scale down) | accept | the bureaucrat following rules for their own sake |
| **Retreatism** | reject | reject | 'dropouts', addicts — "in the society but not of it"; the least common |
| **Rebellion** | reject and replace | reject and replace | revolutionaries seeking new goals and means |

**Merton versus Durkheim.** Durkheim's anomie is normlessness during rapid change when regulation of aspirations breaks down. Merton's is structural and chronic: a mismatch between culturally induced aspirations and structurally available means. The glossary: Merton "proposes that individuals use alternative means - including criminal activities - to gain access to socially created needs that they are unable to obtain through legitimate behaviour" (p. 502).

**Criticisms.** Merton assumes a single shared goal system; he explains property crime better than expressive or violent crime; he treats adaptation as individual, ignoring groups (which subcultural theory addresses); he relies on official statistics showing lower-class crime; and he cannot readily explain why most strained people conform. Yet innovation fits white-collar crime well — executives under pressure for ever more success — and his concept was revived in left realism's **relative deprivation** (Chapter 18).

**Institutional anomie theory.** Messner and Rosenfeld (1994) argue the American Dream is a cultural ethos committing everyone to material success "in a mass society dominated by huge multinational corporations" (p. 145); economic institutions dominate family, education and polity, weakening their ability to restrain, so anomie and crime are high even among the advantaged.

## Software lens

Merton's model is a strong lens for gaming of metrics. If an engineering org makes "ship X features per quarter" the overriding goal while starving teams of time, tooling and headcount (the means), expect **innovation**: shipping by illegitimate means — skipping tests, disabling checks, inflating story points. **Ritualism** is the team that has given up on outcomes and follows the process to the letter ("the ticket says done"). **Retreatism** is quiet quitting. **Rebellion** is the team that forks the project or pushes to replace the goals entirely. The remedy is to rebalance goals and means, not to punish the innovators alone. Messner and Rosenfeld's point applies when one institution (revenue) dominates all others (craft, mentoring, ethics review).
