## The ideas

Sociological positivism holds that "people commit crime because of determining factors in their environment over which they have little or no control" (glossary, p. 508).

**Solidarity.** In *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893) Durkheim contrasted **mechanical solidarity** — simple societies held together by likeness, conformity and a strong, punitive collective conscience — with **organic solidarity** — industrial societies held together by interdependence among specialized groups (p. 133). No society is purely one or the other; pockets of intense mechanical solidarity persist inside organic societies.

**Not a conservative lament.** The book warns against confusing Durkheim with nineteenth-century conservatives and Tönnies, who wanted to restore lost moral certainties. "For Durkheim, the division of labour is a progressive phenomenon" (p. 134): the collective conscience shifts from the individual's obligation to society to society's obligation to the individual. Moral individualism is "not unregulated self-interest but the imposition of a set of reciprocal obligations". Against Adam Smith and the utilitarians, the division of labour's real significance is *moral*: it restrains self-interest. Unbridled egoism as the basis of civilized order is "quite absurd"; laissez-faire society, with arbitrary inequalities and class war, was "a dangerous condition". His goal was to eradicate the **'forced division of labour'**.

**Crime is normal.** A society without crime is impossible and would be abnormal: controls tight enough to eliminate crime would stifle "innovation and social progress" (p. 134). Crime marks moral boundaries and today's deviant may be tomorrow's reformer.

**Three types of deviant** (pp. 134–5): the **biological deviant** (physiological or psychological malfunction, present even in a normal division of labour); the **functional rebel** — a normal person reacting to a pathological, forced division of labour, expressing the true 'spontaneous' collective conscience; and the **skewed deviant**, socialized into a disorganized society — the usual focus of criminology.

**Two arguments for rising crime** (p. 135): industrial societies encourage **unbridled egoism**, contrary to solidarity; and rapid modernization makes **inefficient regulation** likely because new controls have not yet replaced old ones — a state of normlessness or **anomie**. Human needs are unlimited unless society regulates them; in a depression people must lower aspirations, and in a sudden boom "there is now no limit on aspirations".

**Over-determinism and Boudon.** Durkheim is criticized for methodological collectivism: individuals seem to have no choice, and his work is dismissed as conservative. Boudon's methodological individualist reading recognizes choice and coalitions that drive social change, constrained by structure. Hopkins Burke and Pollock apply this to hate crime: mechanical solidarities persist at **macro** (national identity, fundamentalist belief), **mezzo** (organized hate groups) and **micro** (gangs, football firms) levels (p. 136).

## Software lens

Durkheim's solidarity types map onto organizations. A five-person startup runs on mechanical solidarity: shared culture, everyone does everything, deviation is noticed and sanctioned informally. A large engineering org runs on organic solidarity: specialized teams interdependent through APIs, contracts and SLAs; cohesion comes from reciprocal obligations, not sameness. Anomie appears during rapid reorganizations and hypergrowth, when old norms no longer fit and new ones have not formed — the period when incidents, shortcuts and ethically dubious behaviour spike. The "crime is normal" insight has a DevOps reading too: a system with zero rule violations is either over-constrained or not measuring; some deviance (shadow IT, unofficial tools) signals needs the official system fails to meet — the functional rebel.
