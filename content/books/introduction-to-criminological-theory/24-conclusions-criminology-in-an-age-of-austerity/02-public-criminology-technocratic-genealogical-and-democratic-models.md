## Learning objectives

- Explain the public criminology debate and the disjunction between criminological knowledge and public opinion
- Describe the technocratic model and its weaknesses
- Describe the genealogical model and its weaknesses
- Explain the democratic model, its recognition that research is not value-neutral, and its links to moral communitarianism

## The ideas

**The debate.** Fichtelberg and Kupchik (2011) "observe a recent debate among criminological scholars who have engaged with the idea of 'public criminology'" (p. 498), focused on "the growing disjunction between criminological knowledge" and public opinion and policy (p. 487). Better engagement would help policy: Currie (2007) notes policy-makers sometimes agree with criminologists "but are reluctant to act on criminological research when it contradicts public sensibilities" (p. 488).

**The technocratic model.** The criminologist is "the expert whose knowledge should be used to formulate criminal justice policy", advising policy-makers to build policies "based not on ideology or opinion, but instead rooted in scientific research" (p. 489). Garland and Sparks: modern criminology's politics were "'essentially technicist and state-centred, typically offering top-down expert solutions'" (2000: 195). The analogy is an idealized natural science. Problems: natural science itself is politicized in the public sphere (smoking, climate, stem cells) and shaped by political and economic pressures; when publics reject expert advice, technocrats dismiss them as "'irrational' or 'ideological'"; and "it is fundamentally anti-democratic. The technocratic model reduces politics to technical, problem-solving issues, leaving out deeper discussions about the values inherent in the chosen approach" (p. 490) — e.g. writing about capital punishment as if empirical evidence alone could settle a moral question. Criminological knowledge lacks "the efficacy, generalizability or certainty proposed by the technocratic model" (p. 491).

**The genealogical model** "swings to the extreme opposite" (p. 491). It places criminal justice "within broader political and social discourse" and explores consequences — Foucault's *Discipline and Punish*, Garland's *The Culture of Control*, Simon's *Governing through Crime*. Foucault's genealogy "examines the scientific, political and legal discourses that shape the modern world, putting aside the validity of the theories themselves". It focuses on how criminal justice is constructed rather than on using science for wise policy, and on local resistances. Left realists (Young; Currie) object that "the reality of crime is overlooked when analysts remain focused on justice issues", leaving policy to "other, often less progressive" forces (p. 492).

**Speaking and listening.** "The technocratic model involves criminologists who speak to but do not listen to the public, and the genealogical model involves criminologists who speak to other criminologists" (p. 493).

**The democratic model** "involves an exchange of ideas between the criminologist and the public, in which public concern helps direct criminological research and criminological research influences public opinion and policy" (p. 498). It follows from left realism, which encouraged criminologists to take public concerns seriously (p. 493). "A democratic model recognizes that no criminological research is value-neutral. 'Crime' itself is a normative concept, as are 'justice', 'victim' and virtually all other terms that the criminologist uses" (pp. 494, 498).

**Closing thoughts.** "Democratic criminology is very much in accord with the root democracy notions that underpin the radical moral communitarian programme and are in congruence with the role of the progressive criminologist from that perspective in helping to challenge policies that help to reinforce the disciplinary-control-matrix" (p. 498).

## Software lens

The three models describe how engineering and security teams relate to users and the wider public. **Technocratic**: "we're the experts; users who reject our security UX are irrational" — speaking without listening, and disguising value choices (privacy versus fraud prevention) as technical ones. **Genealogical**: critical analyses of how platforms govern users, valuable but often written for other critics and disconnected from building anything. **Democratic**: user concerns shape research and design priorities, research findings are shared back openly, and teams admit that terms like "abuse", "harm" and "trust" are normative choices, not neutral metrics. That last point is perhaps the single most transferable lesson of the whole book.
