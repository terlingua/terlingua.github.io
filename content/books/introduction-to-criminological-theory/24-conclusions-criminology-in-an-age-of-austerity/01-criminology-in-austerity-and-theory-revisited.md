## Learning objectives

- Explain how the socio-economic context changed between the first edition (2001) and the fourth (2014)
- Describe the 2008 crisis, austerity politics and the 'Big Society' and their critics
- Explain why context matters for academic and applied criminology
- Restate the book's overall map of theories in one integrated view

## The ideas

**A changed context.** "The first edition of An Introduction to Criminological Theory was published in July 2001 in a very different socio-economic climate from today's. Those were good times to be a researcher in the areas of criminology and criminal justice" (p. 497). "The 2008 credit crunch was one element of a global financial crisis that led to a major - indeed, unprecedented in recent times - economic recession, with many business collapses, rising unemployment and, in many countries (such as the UK), a new austerity politics aimed at reducing high and unsustainable levels of national debt". For ordinary working people the consequences were "pay freezes, reduced hours, redundancies and an uncertain (but far from optimistic) future" (p. 498).

**The Big Society.** The UK coalition announced cuts to welfare and public pensions and a vision of a bottom-up 'big society' "in which the state withdraws from many of its current (expensive) obligations, to be replaced by strong citizens' groups (cheap) and reinvigorated communities" — an extension of the 'age of responsibility' promoted by Thatcherism and New Labour communitarianism (p. 485). Critics see "an ideological smokescreen, covering the increasing pace of privatization and enormous spending cuts", and note that without a legal right to welfare "voluntary efforts are unlikely to be sufficient to replace reliable state provision" (Charlesworth, 2010). The author's verdict: "there appears to be little - or, indeed, no - light at the end of the tunnel. Indeed, we might need to find the tunnel."

**Austerity ahead.** The Institute for Fiscal Studies and the Institute for Government warned austerity "could still be in place when the 2020 election takes place" (p. 498). "This will be the future socio-economic context in which academic and professional (applied) criminology will operate, certainly in the UK".

**Theory revisited.** The book's map, restated: the **rational actor** model (Classical, right realism, deterrence, rational choice, routine activities) — choose and deter; the **predestined actor** model (biological, psychological, sociological positivism) — find and treat causes; the **victimized actor** model (labelling, conflict, radical, feminist, critical) — question who defines and who gets criminalized; **integrated** theories (biosocial, environmental, control, SAT, desistance, left realism) — cross boundaries; and **age of moral uncertainty** perspectives (postmodern, cultural, risk, communitarian, penal society). Box's recession thesis (Chapter 15), Wacquant's government of insecurity (Chapter 23) and radical moral communitarianism (Chapter 22) all predict that austerity will matter for crime and its control.

## Software lens

The austerity argument maps onto tech downturns: layoffs, hiring freezes and cost-cutting change the context in which security, reliability and ethics work happens. Box predicts weaker bonds and more strain; Wacquant predicts more monitoring of the precarious; the Big Society critique predicts that responsibilities offloaded to volunteers and "communities" (unpaid open-source maintainers, internal guilds) will not replace funded capability. The book's closing map is also a handy diagnostic: when you hear an explanation for a failure, ask which model it belongs to — choice, cause, construction, integration or uncertainty — and what that model would miss.
