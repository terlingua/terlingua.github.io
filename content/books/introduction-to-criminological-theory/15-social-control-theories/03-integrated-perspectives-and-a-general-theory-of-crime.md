## Learning objectives

- Describe Elliott et al.'s integration of strain, learning and control
- Explain Box's radical reformulation of control theory, including the effects of recession
- Explain Braithwaite's reintegrative versus stigmatizing shaming and the role of communitarian societies
- Explain Gottfredson and Hirschi's self-control theory, the versatility construct and its criticisms

## The ideas

**Elliott et al. (1979)** synthesized anomie (strain), social learning and social control into a single model: weak bonds from early socialization, strain that further attenuates bonds, and — crucially — exposure and commitment to delinquent peer groups that provide learning and reinforcement (pp. 299–300).

**Stephen Box (1981, 1987)** sought to explain why self-report studies (like Hirschi's) find weak class–delinquency links while official statistics show strong ones. His answer combined control and labelling: the official system selectively processes the poor, and that processing — "particularly when it is perceived as discriminatory" — gives further impetus to crime (p. 301). In later work Box showed how **economic recession** "could lead to breakdown in social bonds and an increase in criminal activity" (p. 309): reduced legitimate opportunities and greater strain, weaker stakes in conformity, and more punitive control of the marginalized. He located this "radical reformulation" within the victimized actor model (p. 302).

**John Braithwaite (1989)** "builds on and integrates elements of control, labelling, strain and subcultural theory and argues that the key to crime control is a cultural commitment to shaming in ways that are described as 'reintegrative'" (p. 310). **Reintegrative shaming** condemns the act while respecting the person and is followed by ceremonies of forgiveness and reacceptance; **stigmatizing shaming** (disintegrative) labels the offender as an outcast, pushing them toward criminal subcultures. Reintegrative shaming works best in **communitarian** societies with dense interdependencies rather than individualistic ones (pp. 301–2). The glossary: "the key to crime control is a cultural commitment to shaming in ways that are positive rather than negative" (p. 508). It became central to left realism (Chapter 18). Neither Box nor Braithwaite abandoned control theory; both rescued it from its emphasis on the individual and family.

**A general theory of crime.** Gottfredson and Hirschi (1990) "combine rational actor model notions of crime with a predestined actor model theory of criminality" (p. 310). Crimes are mostly "trivial and mundane affairs that result in little gain and require little in the way of effort, planning, preparation or skill", and their **versatility construct** "points to how crime is essentially interchangeable" — offenders do not specialize, undermining the idea of the criminal career (p. 302). Criminality is **low self-control**, which also explains 'analogous' acts — promiscuity, alcohol use, smoking. Low self-control is established in early childhood through **ineffective parenting** (failure to monitor, recognize and punish misbehaviour) and stays stable through life; opportunity converts it into crime. Criticisms: tautology (low self-control is inferred from the behaviour it explains); neglect of structure; difficulty explaining planned white-collar and organized crime; and doubtful stability over the life course (Chapter 17).

## Software lens

Braithwaite's distinction is the theoretical backbone of blameless postmortems: condemn the action and its impact clearly, but reaffirm the person's membership of the team — and make reintegration visible (they lead the remediation). Stigmatizing shaming — public naming, permanent marks on a record — produces disengaged outsiders. It works better in teams with dense mutual dependence (communitarian) than in loosely coupled, individualistic cultures. Box's recession thesis is a warning for layoffs and hiring freezes: weaker stakes and more surveillance of those who remain can raise insider risk. Gottfredson and Hirschi's versatility point suggests abuse is opportunistic and generalist: the account that spams is also the one that scrapes and phishes, so cross-signal detection beats offence-specific silos.
