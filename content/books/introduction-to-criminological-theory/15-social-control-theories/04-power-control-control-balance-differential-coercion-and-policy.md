## Learning objectives

- Explain Hagan's power control theory and its account of gender differences in delinquency
- Explain Tittle's control balance theory, control surplus and control deficit
- Explain Colvin's differential coercion theory
- State the policy implications of social control theories

## The ideas

**Power control theory** (Hagan et al., 1985–90) "combines social class and control theories of criminal behaviour in order to explain the effects of familial control on gender differences in crime" (p. 310). It assumes mothers are the primary agents of socialization. In **patriarchal households** (father in a position of authority at work, mother at home or in a subordinate job), daughters are controlled more closely and taught risk avoidance, while sons are freer to take risks — so boys are much more delinquent than girls. In **egalitarian households** (parents with similar workplace power), daughters receive more freedom and sons more control, so gender differences in delinquency shrink (pp. 304–5).

**Control balance theory** (Tittle, 1995–2000) "defines deviancy as any activity that the majority find unacceptable and/or disapprove of and occurs when a person has either a surplus or deficit of control in relation to others" (p. 310). A **control surplus** exists when people exert more control than is exerted over them; a **control deficit** when they are controlled more than they control. Any imbalance is likely to produce deviance: deficits produce resentment, envy and loss of stake (and **repressive** deviance — predation, defiance, submission), while surpluses produce **autonomous** deviance — exploitation, plunder, decadence — by those who want to extend their control (p. 305). Braithwaite (1997) proposes redistributing control imbalances toward a more egalitarian society, with necessary control exercised "in ways that respect those who are subjected to the control" (p. 306).

**Differential coercion theory** (Colvin, 2000) "seeks to extend our existing understanding of the coercion-crime relationship" (p. 310). Coercion — force, threats, intimidation, removal of support — has multiple sources: families, schools, peer relations, neighbourhoods and the economy. Its *consistency* matters: **erratic coercion** produces anger and low self-control and predicts chronic offending, while **consistent coercion** produces calculated compliance or calculated crime. Coercion fosters "social-psychological deficits" (anger, low self-control, weak bonds, coercive ideation) (p. 306). Related work: Athens (1997) on coercive interpersonal relations; Hagan and McCarthy (1998) on coercion among homeless street youth.

**Policy implications** (p. 308). Strengthen bonds: support families and effective, consistent parenting; early childhood programmes; school engagement and meaningful activities that build commitment and involvement; reintegrative rather than stigmatizing responses; reduce structural strains that weaken bonds (Box); redistribute control imbalances (Tittle, Braithwaite); and reduce erratic coercion in families, schools and workplaces (Colvin).

## Software lens

Control balance theory explains deviance at both ends of an organization. Engineers with a severe **control deficit** — micromanaged, surveilled, no say over their work — are prone to resentment, sabotage, malicious compliance or quiet exit (repressive deviance). Executives and admins with a large **control surplus** — unaccountable power — are prone to exploitation and abuse of privilege (autonomous deviance). Least-privilege and checks on admins address the surplus; autonomy and voice address the deficit. Colvin's erratic coercion is the manager whose sanctions are unpredictable: it produces anger and impulsive responses, whereas consistent, transparent rules produce calculated compliance. Hagan's work is a reminder that differential monitoring of groups (who gets close supervision) shapes risk-taking — and innovation.
