## Learning objectives

- State the unifying assumption of all control theories and how it reverses the usual question
- Trace control theory's roots in Durkheim, Hobbes and the predestined and rational actor models
- Describe Reiss's personal and social control, Toby's 'stakes in conformity' and Nye's family-based control
- Explain Reckless's containment theory: push and pull factors, outer and inner containment

## The ideas

**The reversed question.** Most theories ask why people offend. Control theories ask why people *conform*. "The unifying factor in the different versions of control theory is the assumption that crime and deviance is only to be expected when social and personal controls are inadequate" (p. 309). They "have a long and distinguished pedigree with strong foundations in both the rational actor and predestined actor models. Later variants have incorporated elements from the victimized actor model". Hobbes's view that people without restraint would pursue self-interest by force or fraud, and Durkheim's argument that unlimited aspirations must be regulated by society, are the roots (pp. 293–4).

**Reiss (1951)** distinguished **personal control** — individuals internalize the norms of non-deviant primary groups so that they regulate themselves — from **social control** — the ability of social groups and institutions to make norms effective (p. 295). Delinquency results from failure of either; his study of juvenile probationers found personal control weakness predicted recidivism.

**Toby (1957)** introduced **'stakes in conformity'**: young people with much to lose — good prospects at school, future careers — are less likely to risk them by offending; those with little stake are more easily recruited into gangs (p. 296). The concept recurs throughout later control theory.

**Nye (1958)** produced "a much more systematic version" focused on the family as the main source of control (p. 296): **direct control** (restrictions and punishments imposed by parents), **indirect control** (affectional identification with parents, so the child avoids disappointing them), **internal control** (conscience) and the availability of **legitimate means** to satisfy needs. His was the first major presentation of control research and largely consistent with later survey findings.

**Reckless's containment theory** (1961, 1967) asked why, despite 'push' factors (restlessness, aggression, deprivation) and 'pull' factors (delinquent peers, subcultures, opportunities), most people stay law-abiding (p. 296). Insulating 'containments':

- **Outer containment** — reasonable limits and expectations; meaningful roles and activities; complementary factors such as a sense of belonging, effective supervision and discipline, especially in the family.
- **Inner containment**, which Reckless thought more important because it works whatever the environment: a strong, favourable **self-concept**; **goal orientation** (direction in life); **frustration tolerance**; and **norm retention** (commitment to and internalization of norms) (p. 297).

Matza's drift (Chapter 7) also sits close to control theory: when controls are loosened by neutralizations, young people are free to drift.

## Software lens

Control theory's reversed question is the more useful one for platform integrity and insider risk: not "why do some people abuse the system?" but "what keeps most people from doing so, and what happens when it weakens?" Stakes in conformity are reputation, tenure, equity and career; indirect control is not wanting to let your team down; internal control is professional ethics; direct control is access policy and review. Reckless's inner containment maps onto resilience under pressure — a clear sense of professional identity, goals and tolerance for frustration — and outer containment onto clear expectations, meaningful work and belonging. When reorganizations strip away belonging and meaningful roles, outer containment weakens for everyone.
