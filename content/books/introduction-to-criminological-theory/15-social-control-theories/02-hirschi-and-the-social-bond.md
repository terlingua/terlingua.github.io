## The ideas

**Core proposition.** Travis Hirschi (1969) "made the most influential contribution" to later control theory: all control theories "share the assumption that individuals become involved in criminality when their bond to society is weak or broken" (p. 309) — in his words, "'delinquent acts result when an individual's bond to society is weak or broken'" (Hirschi, 1969: 16, p. 298). People are naturally capable of deviance; what needs explaining is why they refrain. Unlike earlier control theorists who stressed internal psychology, Hirschi used his concepts "in a much more sociological sense".

**Four elements of the social bond** (p. 298):

- **Attachment** — emotional ties and sensitivity to the opinions of others (parents, teachers, peers). The more we care what they think, the more their expectations constrain us.
- **Commitment** — investment in conventional lines of action (education, career, reputation); the rational element — offending risks what one has built (Toby's stakes in conformity).
- **Involvement** — time spent in conventional activities; "idle hands" — a busy person has less time for delinquency.
- **Belief** — acceptance of the moral validity of society's rules; the weaker the belief that rules should be obeyed, the more likely violation.

The elements are interrelated: attachment supports belief, commitment encourages involvement.

**Findings.** In his self-report study of Californian adolescents, Hirschi "found that the closer a relationship a child enjoys with its parents, the more it is attached to and identifies with them, the less likelihood there is of involvement in delinquent behaviour" (p. 309). Attachment to school and teachers had similar effects; surprisingly, attachment to *delinquent* peers did not protect, and social class was only weakly related to self-reported delinquency.

**Criticisms** (p. 299): the theory does not explain *why* bonds are weak in the first place, or what motivates offending when bonds are weak; it fails "to consider the underlying structural and historical context" (Elliott et al., 1979; Box, 1981); it explains primary deviance among adolescents but "habitual secondary deviance appears to be outside its conceptual boundaries" (Box); it struggles with serious adult and white-collar crime by highly bonded people; and causal direction is unclear (delinquency may weaken bonds). These gaps motivated integrated theories.

## Software lens

Hirschi's four bonds are a practical framework for engineering engagement and integrity. **Attachment**: engineers who care about their teammates' and lead's opinion are less likely to cut corners that would embarrass them. **Commitment**: vested equity, a promotion track and a public reputation in open source raise the cost of misconduct. **Involvement**: meaningful, absorbing work leaves less room for side schemes; idle, disengaged staff are a risk factor. **Belief**: engineers who think the security policy is legitimate follow it; those who see it as theatre route around it. Note the white-collar caveat: highly bonded senior people commit some of the biggest frauds — bonds can bind people to organizations whose goals are themselves the problem.
