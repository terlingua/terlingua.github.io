## Learning objectives

- Explain why constitutive criminology is the main postmodern rethinking of criminology
- Define crime as 'the power to deny others' and explain how control fuels crime
- Explain the two theoretical foundations: Lacan and chaos theory
- Explain replacement discourses and the policy problem of investing in structures of oppression
- State the weaknesses of constitutive criminology

## The ideas

**The only developed attempt.** "The only well-developed attempt to rethink the central issues and themes of criminology in terms of postmodernism is the constitutive criminology originally developed by Stuart Henry and Dragan Milovanovic" (p. 385). Crime, criminals, victims and control are *co-produced* — constituted — through discourse and social interaction; agents and structures make each other.

**Crime as the power to deny.** "Henry and Milovanovic (1996) define crime as the power to deny others and they argue that the conventional crime control strategies, in the form of fast-expanding criminal justice institutions - the police and prisons - or as political rhetoric rehearsed in the media, fuel the engine of crime" (p. 385). Harm occurs when someone is denied their humanity or their ability to make a difference: harms of **reduction** (offended parties lose some quality — property, standing, dignity) and of **repression** (they are prevented from attaining a position or expressing themselves). The definition reaches beyond law to harms by corporations, states and institutions.

**Two theoretical foundations** (p. 376):

- **Jacques Lacan**, the post-Freudian psychoanalyst: subjects are constituted in language; discourse positions people and structures their desires. Dominant discourses (law, criminology, media) constitute people as 'criminals' and 'victims'.
- **Chaos theory**, describing dynamic, non-linear systems: small inputs can produce large effects and outcomes are unpredictable. Modernist justice values "rationality, uniformity and consistency of treatment before the law", whereas a chaos-informed postmodern equivalent "allows room for creativity" and variation, so different local justice systems can co-exist (Young's example of Native American and other community-based justice alongside state law).

**Replacement discourses.** Constitutive criminologists seek "'replacement discourses' that fuel positive social constructions with the intention not to 'replace one truth with another' but instead invoke 'a multiplicity of resistances' 'to the ubiquity of power'" (glossary, p. 503; Henry and Milovanovic, 1996: ix–xiii). Changing how crime is talked about — in media, policy and criminology — changes what is constituted.

**Policy.** "The fundamental policy implication confronting postmodern - and in particular constitutive - criminology is how is it possible as a society to stop investing in structures of oppression" (p. 384): de-investing from the expansion of policing and prisons and from crime-centred political rhetoric; supporting restorative, community and peacemaking alternatives; producing replacement discourses.

**Weaknesses.** Constitutive criminology is abstract and hard to operationalize or test; its language is opaque; its relativism makes it hard to justify any moral judgement; and, like postmodernism generally, it may underestimate the persistence of power. Critics ask how its local, plural justice avoids reproducing inequality.

## Software lens

"Crime as the power to deny others" is a useful definition for harms in sociotechnical systems: denial of access (account lockouts without appeal), of voice (shadow bans), of standing (algorithmic downranking) and of dignity (abusive content). Constitutive criminology's claim that control strategies "fuel the engine of crime" has a security analogue in arms races: heavy-handed anti-abuse controls create incentives for more sophisticated evasion and a market for bypass tools. Chaos theory is native territory for SREs: complex systems behave non-linearly, small changes cause outsized incidents, and uniform global rules may perform worse than locally adapted ones. Replacement discourses are the reason language in incident reviews matters: "root cause: human error" constitutes a culprit; "contributing factors" constitutes a system.
