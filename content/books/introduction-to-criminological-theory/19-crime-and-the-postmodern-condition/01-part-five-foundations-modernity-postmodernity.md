## The ideas

**Modern theories.** All the theories so far are products of **modernity**: secular, industrialized, rationalized, rule-bound societies in which science is the dominant form of knowledge, with a faith in reason that "will ultimately form a harmonious system" (Berlin, p. 367). The three models each propose control: deter (rational actor), treat the individual or transform the environment (predestined actor), or stop criminalizing the dispossessed (victimized actor) (p. 366). Integrated theories in Part Four aimed at "a bigger, better, all-encompassing theory" — "clearly in line with modernist social science thinking".

**Three sources of the postmodern** (pp. 367–8):

1. **Daniel Bell's** *The End of Ideology* (1960) and *The Coming of Post-Industrial Society* (1973): ideological exhaustion after totalitarianism and war, and post-industrial ('post-Fordist') economies where services replace manufacturing and consumers outperform producers — old class analyses lost explanatory power.
2. **Poststructuralism** (France, late 1960s–1970s): sceptical of attaching fixed meanings to words; Foucault argued knowledge and language "cannot be regarded as anything other than subjective and relative", so "no intellectual tradition can have privileged authority over another".
3. An **aesthetic** movement rejecting the austere 'international style' of modernist architecture — 1960s tower blocks and multi-storey car parks.

**Three distinguishing characteristics** (p. 368): "an aversion to 'metadiscourses' - or grand self-legitimating theories" that can lead to intellectual sterility and political oppression; "an awareness of the indeterminacy of knowledge and the impossibility of absolute truth"; and "an enthusiasm for eclecticism and variety". The glossary: postmodernism is "a challenge to rationality, reason and science as the dominant forms of social explanation" (p. 507).

**Economic and political postmodernity.** Post-Fordism: flexible work, weakened unions, peripheral low-paid (often female, part-time) labour, services over manufacturing, enterprise culture, subcontracting — "deindustrialization and the disorganization of capitalism" (p. 368). Politically, Foucault's power is not the state's prerogative but pervasive, with the state only one location of control and resistance; criminology should move beyond "'state-law-crime-criminals'" to a wider chain.

**Sceptical and affirmative postmodernism** (Rosenau, 1992). **Sceptics**: the postmodern age is "one of fragmentation, disintegration, malaise, meaninglessness... even absence of moral parameters and societal chaos" — "the postmodernism of despair"; "no social or political project is worthy of commitment... all that is left is play" (p. 374). **Affirmatives** agree with the critique of modernity but "have a more hopeful, optimistic view", are "more indigenous to Anglo-North American culture", and are open to positive political action, spirituality and local, non-dogmatic projects.

**The relativism problem.** "If there is no such thing as the 'truth of the human condition', it is clearly difficult to formulate an argument in support of basic human rights, or to locate legitimate foundations for law" (p. 385). Relativism "denies the possibility of truth and hence of justice in anything other than a purely subjective form, which inevitably consigns us to the prospect of conflict". Postmodernism may celebrate diversity and give voice to the oppressed, but "power relations and political decisions are fundamentally important and may restrict this ideal".

## Software lens

The shift from modern to postmodern thought parallels the shift in software architecture from grand, centrally planned systems (one canonical data model, one enterprise service bus, one "source of truth") to plural, loosely coupled systems that tolerate inconsistency and local variation (microservices, eventual consistency, multiple bounded contexts). Aversion to metadiscourses is distrust of the "one framework to rule them all". The relativism problem has a counterpart too: if every team's local truth is equally valid, you lose the shared foundations (security baselines, accessibility standards, privacy rights) that need to bind everyone. Affirmative postmodernism is the pragmatic stance: accept pluralism but commit to local, revisable, value-driven projects.
