## Learning objectives

- Explain how anarchist criminology differs from reasoned modernist critiques of law
- Describe its roots in Bakunin and Kropotkin and its contemporary US forms (Pepinsky, Tifft, Ferrell)
- Explain the critique of state law as inherently inhuman and the criminology of crime and resistance
- State the policy implications of anarchist and postmodern criminology

## The ideas

**'Unreasonable' critique.** "Unlike most modernist intellectual orientations, anarchism and anarchist criminology do not seek to incorporate reasoned or reasonable critiques of law and legal authority but, in contrast, argue that progressive social change requires the 'unreasonable' and the 'unthinkable'" (p. 385). Anarchist criminologists "launch aggressive and 'unreasonable' critiques against law and legal authority because they argue that these institutions undermine human community and diversity".

**Roots.** Bakunin and Kropotkin attacked state authority and legal control. Kropotkin criticized law's tendency "to crystallize that which should be modified and developed on a day-to-day basis" and demanded abolition of prisons and the law itself; Bakunin called for the destruction of the state and its replacement by "the spontaneous and continuous action of the masses" (p. 380). Ferrell: such critiques emerged "out of head-on confrontations between state legal authorities and anarchists attempting to construct alternative societal arrangements" — e.g. the Industrial Workers of the World's free-speech fights, breaking laws in numbers great enough to overload courts and jails.

**Contemporary anarchist criminology.** "Ferrell (1998) observes that anarchist criminology has actually flourished during the previous thirty years in the USA" (p. 385). Pepinsky (1978) advocated "'communist anarchism as an alternative to the rule of criminal law'" and later developed peacemaking criminology, opposed to the violence inherent in state law (p. 381). **Tifft (1979)** argued "for replacing state/legal 'justice' with a fluid, face-to-face form of justice grounded in emerging human needs". DiCristina critiqued criminology's epistemological claims. Many critical criminologists agree state law is "so thoroughly lubricated by economic privilege, intertwined with patriarchal arrangements and protected by racist procedures as to constitute a mailed fist" brought down on the marginalized.

**Against the law.** "Anarchist criminology thus produces a profoundly radical critique of state law as a system of inherent inhumanity, and its sense of standing 'against the law' leads logically to a criminology of crime and resistance" (p. 385; glossary p. 502). Some crime — workplace sabotage under authoritarian, alienating work, graffiti, squatting — is read as resistance to domination (p. 383). It embraces the postmodern "lack of commitment to master plans or grand narratives - including its own" and "fluid communities of uncertainty and critique" (p. 384).

**Policy implications** (p. 384): stop investing in structures of oppression; decentralize justice into face-to-face, needs-based community forms; reduce reliance on state law, police and prisons; celebrate diversity and resistance; and, consistent with postmodern scepticism, avoid prescribing master plans. Critics see romanticization of offending and an inability to protect the weak from predation — the same criticisms left realists made of left idealism.

## Software lens

Anarchist criminology has a clear echo in hacker and open-source culture: suspicion of centralized authority, preference for decentralized, consensus-based governance, "rough consensus and running code" over formal rules, and celebration of rule-breaking as resistance (jailbreaking, circumventing DRM). Tifft's fluid, face-to-face justice resembles how small open-source communities handle conflict through maintainers and direct conversation rather than formal codes. The critique is also familiar: without any formal structure, communities can fail to protect vulnerable members from harassment — the "tyranny of structurelessness" — which is why many projects adopted codes of conduct.
