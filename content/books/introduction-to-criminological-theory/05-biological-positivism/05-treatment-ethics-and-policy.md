## Learning objectives

- Describe the three forms of individualized biological treatment and their problems
- Explain the link between biological positivism, Social Darwinism and eugenics
- Explain why the Holocaust poses a question for criminology about modernity
- State the policy implications of biological positivism and the risk of medicalization

## The ideas

**Treatment.** If crime results from a biological condition, the logic is to treat the condition. The book considers three individualized treatments (p. 105):

- **Surgical intervention**, often prefrontal leucotomy (severing frontal lobes from thalamus), which reduces anxiety; and castration of sex offenders — with cautionary cases such as a castrated Californian rapist who turned to child molesting and murder.
- **Chemotherapy** — drugs for treatment and control, e.g. antabuse for alcoholism and hormonal drugs to reduce sexual drive.
- **Electrocontrol** — electrical stimulation or monitoring of the brain.

**Positivist assumptions.** Biological positivism casts the researcher as a "detached objective neutral observer" discovering natural laws; once found, treatment can reduce offending, even pre-emptively for those 'at risk'. "In short, criminal behaviour is perceived to be a sickness - an inherently problematic analysis - that has led to treatments that are intrusive, in some cases unethical, and on occasion with horrendous wider implications" (p. 107).

**Social Darwinism and eugenics.** The same Darwinian source fed **Social Darwinism**: the claim that rational judgement, morality and business success were unequally distributed by race and class. Prominent figures argued that Africans, Indians, black Americans, paupers, criminals "and even women" had smaller brains and less capacity for reason (p. 107). Selective breeding was proposed in Britain and the USA; **eugenics** aimed to 'improve' human genetic stock. Goring recommended that people with criminal characteristics should not reproduce. Modern echoes: the 1994 Maudsley centre, a closed-door 1995 conference on isolating a 'criminal gene', and *The Bell Curve*. Muncie: such theories appeal because they offer "scientific evidence that clearly differentiates us from 'them'", an out-group "we feel legitimately entitled to target, outlaw and, in the final instance, eradicate" (p. 108) — characteristic of totalitarian regimes.

**The Holocaust question.** Morrison (1995): the Holocaust was "the crime of the twentieth century" yet had not appeared in criminology textbooks; the essential question is whether it "is at odds with modernity or simply the logical consequence of a project" of which biological positivism was a component (p. 108). (Compare Bauman's *Modernity and the Holocaust*.)

**Policy implications** (p. 109). Crime reflects biological inferiority shown by physical or genetic markers. Policies have included isolation, sterilization, execution, brain surgery, chemical castration, improved diets and programmes against alcohol and drugs. "The policy implication... is to treat the defect and protect society from the untreatable": drugs, psychosurgery, plastic surgery, genetic counselling and eugenics; experts as decision-makers, individualized diagnosis, risk prediction, indeterminate sentences and lifelong incarceration of the incurable. The danger: medicalizing criminal justice, with potential government misuse "as a form of social control with discrimination against different social groups based on the presence of biological risk indicators". Chapter 13 revisits these issues with biosocial theory.

## Software lens

"Treat the defect and protect society from the untreatable" is a pattern every builder of risk-scoring systems should recognize. Predictive models that flag people as high-risk from fixed characteristics, combined with decisions made by "experts" (or models) rather than through due process, and indeterminate restrictions ("account limited pending review" with no end date), recreate the positivist toolkit. The book's warning about discrimination "based on the presence of biological risk indicators" applies directly to proxies like postcode or device type that correlate with protected characteristics. "Us versus them" science is attractive precisely because it licenses action against an out-group.
