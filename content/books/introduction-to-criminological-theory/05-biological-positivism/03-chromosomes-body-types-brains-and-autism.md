## Learning objectives

- Explain the Klinefelter and XYY 'extra maleness' hypotheses and why they failed
- Describe body-type theories from Kretschmer and Hooton to Sheldon, the Gluecks and Cortes and Gatti
- Summarize what the book says about brain injury and organic psychoses
- Explain the features of autistic spectrum disorders that may predispose to offending, and the caution about publicity

## The ideas

**Sex chromosomes.** Normal complements are XX (female) and XY (male). Klinefelter et al. (1942) described sterile, feminized males, later found to have an extra X (XXY); Court Brown (1962) found high criminality among Klinefelter males in psychiatric institutions. Studies of **XYY** men tested the hypothesis of 'extra maleness' and so more aggression: Casey (1965) and Neilson (1968) found XYY men in secure hospitals tended to be very tall, of low intelligence, with EEG abnormalities and histories of theft and assault (p. 90). Criticisms (pp. 90–1): samples from special hospitals revealed more *psychiatric disorder* than criminality; there is no "fixed and identifiable XYY syndrome", so it cannot predict crime; and offending by some XXY males may reflect adolescent anxiety about apparent lack of masculinity. Advances in genetics have revived claims that aspects of criminality have genetic components.

**Body types.** Kretschmer linked asthenic and athletic builds to schizophrenia and pyknic builds to manic depression. Hooton (1939) measured 17,000 people and declared criminals organically inferior. **Sheldon (1949)** classified physique as **endomorph** (soft, fat), **mesomorph** (muscular, athletic) and **ectomorph** (skinny, fragile); offenders tended to mesomorphy, and because their parents were offenders he concluded the causes were inherited (p. 92). The **Gluecks (1950)** supported him. Critics argue social selection: delinquent activities draw the athletic. **Cortes and Gatti (1972)** reply that this accuses biology of more determinism than it claims: physical factors are part of social selection. Later work linked physique to bullying.

**Brain injury and psychoses.** There is "little evidence that brain injuries actually lead to criminal behaviour"; reported cases are rare and "the original personality and social background of the person are of greater significance" (p. 93). Some organic psychoses (epidemic encephalitis in the 1920s, linked to destructiveness and arson; others such as epilepsy and tumours) have been associated with behaviour change.

**Autistic spectrum disorders.** Bleuler coined 'autism'; Kanner (1943) separated it from childhood schizophrenia; Asperger (1944) described more able children ('autistic psychopathy'). Autism and Asperger's are two of five pervasive developmental disorders, defined behaviourally because no biological markers are known; autistic traits are "widely distributed in the normal population" (p. 97). Risk factors for offending common among autistic children include peer rejection, social isolation, poor social functioning and impulsivity. Features that *can* predispose (Berney, 2004): **narrow obsessions** with unawareness of effects on others (the man who entered houses to watch washing machines); **misinterpreting social rules** and social naivety, making some "easy prey" and unwitting accomplices of criminals; and **distress at changes to routine**, which can provoke aggressive outbursts. Yet Howlin: "there is little evidence of any significant association between autism and criminal offending", despite "occasional and sometimes lurid publicity" (p. 97).

## Software lens

The XYY story is a textbook spurious finding: a striking hypothesis, a skewed sample (secure hospitals), a vivid label, and no reliable syndrome to predict from. It is also a reminder that being tall and in a special hospital tells you more about who gets institutionalized than about genes. The autism discussion is directly relevant to engineering teams, which include many autistic and neurodivergent people. The book's balanced point — some features (rule literalism, distress at abrupt change, social naivety exploited by others) carry specific risks, while overall association with offending is weak and publicity distorts — supports practical measures: explicit written norms, advance notice of change, and awareness that social engineers target trusting colleagues.
