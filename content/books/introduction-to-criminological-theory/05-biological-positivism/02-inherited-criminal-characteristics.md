## The ideas

**Criminal families.** Dugdale (1877) traced the Juke family and found most members were criminals or paupers; Goddard (1914) traced 480 members of the Kallikak family. Both noted social causes but emphasized heredity. Shared environment, not just genes, runs in families, so these studies cannot separate the two.

**Goring (1913)** compared 3,000 repeat prisoners with a control group: prisoners were physically smaller and mentally inferior; criminality correlated between parents and children and between brothers; children separated from imprisoned parents early were as likely or more likely to offend. Goring concluded inheritance, not environment, was primary. Weaknesses (p. 87): poor measurement of environment; assuming parental 'contagion' transmits only techniques, not *values*; and studying only males even though 102 brothers but only 6 sisters were imprisoned — if criminality were inherited, females should be similarly affected unless it is sex-linked. Goring recommended that people with criminal characteristics should not reproduce (p. 107).

**Twin studies.** Identical (monozygotic) twins share all genes; fraternal (dizygotic) twins share about half, like ordinary siblings. Greater similarity among identical twins is read as genetic. **Criminal concordance** is the percentage of pairs where, if one has a record, so does the other. **Lange (1930)**: 77 per cent for 13 identical pairs versus 12 per cent for 17 fraternal pairs (8 per cent for ordinary brothers). **Christiansen (1968)**, 6,000 Danish twin pairs: 35.8 per cent identical versus 12.3 per cent fraternal — much lower than Lange, and Christiansen did *not* claim heredity was dominant (pp. 87–8). Problem: identical twins are treated more alike and spend more time together, so shared environment is confounded; a predisposition may be inherited while the form of offending is shaped by other factors.

**Adoption studies.** Adoptees separate genetic from rearing influence. **Hutchings and Mednick (1977)**, Copenhagen male adoptees: 48 per cent of those with criminal records had criminal birth fathers versus 31.1 per cent of those without; highest risk when *both* birth and adoptive fathers were criminal. In a matched comparison, 49 per cent of criminal adoptees had criminal birth fathers versus 28 per cent of controls. Mednick et al. (1984) replicated across all Danish non-familial adoptions 1924–47 with a slightly weaker correlation (p. 89). Confound: agencies place children in environments similar to their origins. And what the inherited "crime-inducing characteristics" are "is not really considered".

**IQ.** From the late 1960s some claimed intelligence is genetic and explains crime differences between ethnic groups (Shockley; Jensen); Gordon (1986) called IQ the best predictor of offending; Hirschi and Hindelang (1977) found IQ at least as good a predictor as other social variables — but IQ is strongly related to class and ethnicity, and tests reflect culture and schooling (p. 89). *The Bell Curve* (1994) extended the argument; the book links such work to the eugenic tradition (pp. 107–8).

## Software lens

Twin and adoption designs are natural experiments trying to separate two confounded variables — like trying to tell whether a performance regression comes from the code change or the infrastructure it was deployed on, when both changed together. Family studies are the equivalent of noticing that the same team's services fail often and concluding the team is "bad" without asking whether they inherited the worst legacy systems. Christiansen's lower concordance after using full registers is a lesson in selection bias: Lange's small, prison-sampled set inflated the effect, just as a benchmark run on hand-picked cases inflates speed-ups.
