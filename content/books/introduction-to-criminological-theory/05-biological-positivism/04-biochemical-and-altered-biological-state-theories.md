## Learning objectives

- Distinguish biochemical theories from altered biological state theories
- Summarize the evidence on testosterone, blood sugar and adrenaline/arousal
- Explain Eysenck's and Baldwin's arousal arguments
- Describe the links proposed between allergies, diet, alcohol and illegal drugs and crime

## The ideas

**Two categories.** Biochemical theories involve substances or processes "already present in the body"; altered biological state theories "link behavioural changes in an individual with the introduction of an external chemical agent" (pp. 98, 100).

**Sexual hormones.** Male animals of most species are more aggressive than females, linked to **testosterone**, which has been associated with murder and rape. But in humans the relationship is "more complex": "in most men testosterone levels do not significantly affect levels of aggression" (pp. 98–9). (Hormonal theories of women's offending are covered in Chapter 8.)

**Blood sugar.** **Hypoglycaemia** (low blood sugar, sometimes linked to diabetes) "may result in irritable, aggressive reactions" and has been linked to sexual offences, assaults and motiveless murder; Virkkunen tied it to truancy, low verbal IQ, tattooing, stealing from home and alcohol abuse (pp. 99–100). In one case a jury accepted that a hypoglycaemic man had been reduced to "an automaton".

**Adrenaline and arousal.** Adrenaline injections changed psychopaths' behaviour but not other prisoners'; Hare (1982) found criminals show fewer stress signs when threatened with pain; Mednick et al. (1982) found some violent criminals need stronger stimuli to be aroused and recover more slowly (p. 100). **Eysenck**: a person with low stress levels is easily bored and "craves exciting experiences", so stressful situations are enjoyable. **Baldwin (1990)** links the age–crime curve to arousal: children habituate and seek bigger thrills, while the stimulus from crime and the physical capacity for it decline with age — which raises the question whether adrenaline production is biologically or socially dictated.

**Allergies and diet.** Allergic reactions to pollen, inhalants, drugs and food have been linked with irritability and aggression and so, in some circumstances, assault; diet and food additives have been similarly studied (p. 101).

**Alcohol.** Alcohol "has probably much closer links with crime and criminal behaviour than most other drugs - with the contemporary exception of crack cocaine and possibly heroin", partly because it "is legal, readily available and in extremely common usage" (p. 101). Saunders (1984): alcohol a significant factor in about 1,000 arrests a day; Flanzer (1981): 80 per cent of US family violence involved alcohol.

**Illegal drugs.** The relationship varies by type and quantity: "cannabis and opiates such as heroin tend to reduce aggressive hostile tendencies, while cocaine and its derivative crack are more closely associated with violence" (p. 104). Much drug-related crime is acquisitive — funding a habit (see Chapter 4's stolen goods markets) — rather than pharmacological. Some regard drug misuse as intrinsically wrong and in need of punishment regardless of its effects.

## Software lens

Arousal theory is a familiar pattern in security: thrill-seeking, boredom-driven behaviour explains a lot of early-career hacking, griefing and defacement, and the age–crime decline matches many reformed teenage hackers. Baldwin's open question — is the craving biological or socially shaped? — maps onto whether you redirect it (CTFs, bug bounties, red-team roles) or treat it as a defect. The drugs section models a useful distinction for incident analysis: a substance (or a tool) can cause harm through its direct effects (pharmacological, like crack and violence) or through the economy around it (acquisitive crime to fund a habit; ransomware economics). Different mechanisms need different controls.
