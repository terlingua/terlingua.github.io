## The ideas

**Why a new model?** France published the first national crime statistics in 1827. They were "astonishingly regular", with stable differences between places (p. 80). Rational actor proponents expected random variation; regularity suggested other factors. Crime and recidivism were also *rising* despite prompt, proportionate punishment under the new code.

**Context and foundations.** The predestined actor model emerged amid rapid industrialization, urban crowding and class conflict. It "rejected the rational actor model emphasis on free will and replaced it with the doctrine of determinism" (pp. 80–1): crime is caused by factors inside or outside the person beyond their control — "in some way, it is the destiny of the individual to become a criminal". Three intellectual foundations:

1. **Evolution and science** — Darwin made humans animals subject to natural laws; scientists looked for causal forces, first in the offender's biology.
2. **Social evolutionism** — Herbert Spencer: human beings develop through interaction with the social world they inhabit, so environment shapes development.
3. **Positivism** — Comte: "the methods of the natural sciences provide the only means of obtaining knowledge of human nature and society" (p. 82), built from empirical data, with laws formulated and tested. The radical step was applying this to *social* laws, promising order engineered by enlightened scientists.

Ferri's epigraph captures the programme: every crime results from anthropological, telluric (physical environment) and social causes.

**Lombroso (the Italian School).** Lombroso, Ferri and Garofalo argued criminology should focus on "the scientific study of criminals and criminal behaviour" (p. 83). Lombroso held criminals are **atavistic** — "throwbacks to earlier forms of evolutionary life" marked by unusual ears, sloping foreheads, long arms, receding chins, twisted noses. Later editions added climate, poverty, immigration and urbanization (by the fifth edition social factors made up 80 per cent of his explanation, p. 265). His four types (p. 84): **born criminals** (atavistic), **insane criminals**, **occasional criminals or criminaloids** (opportunistic, but with innate predisposing traits) and **criminals of passion** (anger, love, honour). He reduced female crime to prostitution and abortion instigated by men. Methods were primitive and data limited. Yet three contributions endure: moving from armchair theorizing to scientific study of the criminal; showing the value of clinical and historical records; and recognizing the need for **multi-factor** explanations.

**Ferri (1856–1929)** explained crime through the interaction of physical factors (race, geography, temperature), individual factors (age, sex, psychology) and social factors (population, religion, culture). **Garofalo (1852–1934)** proposed **natural crimes** — acts no society could refuse to punish — which violate two universal sentiments: **pity** (revulsion at voluntarily inflicting suffering) and **probity** (respect for others' property). The Italian School's ideas were later congenial to Mussolini's regime as scientific legitimation of racial purity and authoritarianism.

## Software lens

The 1827 statistics episode is what happens when you finally instrument production: the "random user error" story collapses when error rates turn out to be stable per region and per release. Regularity implies systemic causes. Lombroso is the cautionary tale of feature engineering gone wrong: he chose visible surface features, found correlations in a biased sample (prisoners), and treated them as causes. His three good contributions — measure instead of philosophize, use records, model multiple factors — are still the basis of good incident analysis.
