## Learning objectives

- Explain Tannenbaum's 'dramatization of evil'
- Distinguish Lemert's primary and secondary deviance and explain what determines the transition
- Define the self-fulfilling prophecy and master status
- Explain how labelling can make conformity difficult and deviance a career

## The ideas

**Tannenbaum (1938)**, often regarded as founder of the perspective, noted that of the many young males who break the law, only some are apprehended. His **'dramatization of evil'** describes how a community first defines the *acts* of a young person as evil and then gradually comes to define the *person* as evil; the young person, tagged and segregated, comes to see themselves as the community does. "The person becomes the thing he is described as being" (Tannenbaum, 1938: 20; summarized p. 198).

**Lemert (1951).** **Primary deviance** is the initial rule-breaking that may arise from many causes — biological, psychological, social — and has little effect on the person's self-concept; they rationalize it and "do not consider themselves to be deviant" (p. 198). If acts meet **societal reaction**, and "with each act of primary deviance the offender becomes progressively more stigmatized through 'name calling, labelling or stereotyping'", a crisis may occur. One resolution is to accept the deviant status and reorganize life around it: **secondary deviance**, deviance that is a response to the problems created by the societal reaction. "Primary deviance may or may not develop into secondary deviance. It is the number of criminal transgressions and the intensity and hostility of societal reaction that determines the outcome" (p. 198). An undetected offender is less likely to persist than one officially sanctioned.

**Self-fulfilling prophecy.** Becker, Erikson and Kitsuse used Merton's concept: "a false definition of a situation, evoking a new behaviour that makes the original false assumption come true" (glossary, p. 508). Labelling someone as criminal provokes hostile reactions that close conventional opportunities — jobs, friends, schooling — so they turn to deviant others and deviant identity.

**Master status.** A person's other statuses — parent, spouse, worker — are "hidden under the criminal label until that becomes their 'master status' or controlling public identification" (p. 199). Labelling theorists claim "the false definition of offenders as uncompromisingly criminal fulfils this very prophecy by evoking hostile and negative societal reactions that render conformity difficult" — producing a **deviant career**.

**The shift in focus.** These ideas move criminology from asking "why did they do it?" to "why were they caught, and what did the reaction do to them?" — and they reinforce Chapter 4's warning (Martin and Webster) that conviction can leave people with little to lose.

## Software lens

Secondary deviance has a direct analogue in how teams treat engineers after incidents. An engineer who causes one outage (primary deviance, causes various and usually systemic) and is then publicly blamed, nicknamed and quietly removed from interesting work may disengage, stop taking risks, or leave — confirming the original judgement. Blameless postmortems are, in effect, an anti-labelling technology: they respond to the act without converting it into a master status. The same logic applies to users: permanently flagging an account after one violation, so every later action is treated as suspicious, can push legitimate users toward the evasive behaviour the flag predicted.
