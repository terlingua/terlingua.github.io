## Learning objectives

- State the core labelling claim and Becker's definition of deviance
- Explain moral entrepreneurs and the two outcomes of a successful moral crusade
- Use Platt and Tierney as examples of social construction
- Explain Erikson's boundary maintenance and Quinney's segmental view of law

## The ideas

**Core claim.** "Labelling theorists argue that no behaviour is inherently deviant or criminal, but only comes to be considered so when others confer this label upon the act" (p. 205). The glossary: crime "is a product of the social reaction to an activity: if the action is ignored or not discovered the person does not become a criminal" (p. 505).

**Becker (1963).** "Social groups create deviance by making the rules whose infraction constitutes deviance, and by applying those rules to particular people and labelling them as outsiders. From this point of view... the deviant is one to whom the label has been successfully applied; deviant behaviour is behaviour that people so label" (p. 194). Rules "are made by people with power and enforced upon people without power" — by the old for the young, men for women, white people for minorities, the middle class for the working class. Most people internalize rules without questioning how their behaviour is decided for them.

**Moral entrepreneurs.** Some rules are cynically designed to keep the powerless in place; others reflect "a sincere - albeit irrational and mistaken - belief" by high-status people that a new rule will benefit its subjects. Becker called rule creators **moral entrepreneurs**. A successful **moral crusade** has two outcomes (p. 205): "(i) there is the creation of a new group of 'outsiders' who infringe the new rule; and (ii) there is a social control agency charged with enforcing the rule". (Becker's own example was the US Marihuana Tax Act of 1937 and the Federal Bureau of Narcotics.)

**Platt (1969)** showed that modern approaches to 'juvenile delinquency' were "the outcome of a nineteenth-century moral crusade undertaken by largely upper-class women" — the 'child savers' who invented the juvenile court and new categories of youthful misbehaviour. **Tierney (1982)** argued 'wife battering' became a social issue worthy of criminal justice intervention only after the mid-1970s, "mainly because of the increasing strength of the women's movement" (p. 206). Note that construction cuts both ways: it can create new controls over the powerless (Platt) or bring previously ignored harms by the powerful into view (Tierney).

**Erikson (1962)**: all social systems place boundaries on permissible behaviour, and "deviancy is simply that which is defined as crossing these parameters" (p. 206). Deviance performs **boundary maintenance**: punishing it clarifies and reinforces the group's norms — a Durkheimian echo.

**Quinney** argued "society is segmentally organized and the criminal law tends to reflect the values of politically powerful sections of society" (p. 206), linking labelling to conflict theory (Chapter 10).

**Recipients.** Labels are not distributed at random: only some violators are caught. Kitsuse (1962) argued "it is not the behaviour per se" but the interactional process of definition that matters; police background expectations lead them to concentrate on certain 'types' (Cicourel, 1968); Piliavin and Briar found demeanour shaped police decisions; class and race bias are well documented (p. 197).

## Software lens

Every content-moderation, acceptable-use or code-of-conduct policy is made by someone. Becker's questions are design questions: who made this rule, for whom, and who enforces it? A new policy creates a new class of violators and usually a new enforcement function (a trust-and-safety team, a compliance bot). Moral entrepreneurs in tech are often sincere — the engineer who pushes a new mandatory linter "for everyone's good" — and the new rule may be good or bad. Tierney's example shows construction can surface real harms (harassment policies took hold only once communities organized), while Platt's shows it can expand control over the least powerful (junior contributors, new accounts). Cicourel's "background expectations" are the reviewer heuristics that decide whose PRs get extra scrutiny.
