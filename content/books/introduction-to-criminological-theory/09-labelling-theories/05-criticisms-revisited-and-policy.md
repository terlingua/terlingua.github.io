## Learning objectives

- State the main criticisms of labelling theories
- Explain informal labelling (Matsueda; Heimer and Matsueda) and other revisions
- State the policy implications for early-career and juvenile offending

## The ideas

**Criticisms** (pp. 201–2):

- **Vagueness.** Plummer (1979): so loosely defined it harbours diverse positions and internal contradictions; critics (Taylor et al., 1973) call it a vague perspective lacking consistent concepts and failing "to make precise distinctions between mere description and causal statements". Schur (1971) replies that its strength lies in analysing neglected aspects of social reality and complementing other approaches.
- **Defining deviance.** Gibbs (1966): if an act is deviant only when a reaction follows, how can labelling theorists talk of 'secret deviants' and 'primary deviants'? This implies deviance can be identified independently of reaction.
- **Causes of primary deviance.** It ignores why people offend in the first place.
- **Determinism of the label.** It treats the labelled as passive; many resist or reject labels.
- **Real harms.** Serious crimes like murder are condemned everywhere; it is easier to apply to 'victimless' or minor deviance.
- **Power.** Radical critics: it describes labelling by front-line agents but does not explain the structure of power behind who makes rules — the gap filled by conflict and radical theory (Chapter 10). Left realists add that it dismisses real victimization.

**Labelling revisited.** "In more recent years, the notions and concepts of labelling theories have been modified and developed with much attention devoted to the issue of informal labelling carried out by parents, peers and teachers" (p. 206), which "has a greater effect on subsequent criminal behaviour than official labelling" (p. 203). Matsueda (1992) and Heimer and Matsueda (1994) argue informal labels are not just an outcome of contact with the criminal justice system but depend on the individual's position in social structure and on how they think significant others see them (**reflected appraisals**). Braithwaite's **reintegrative shaming** (Chapter 15) integrates labelling with control theory: stigmatizing shame produces outsiders; reintegrative shame condemns the act while affirming the person.

**Policy.** The most significant implications concern "the criminal justice response to early career offending, in particular by children and juveniles" (p. 205). Trying juveniles in adult courts "may simply exacerbate the problem of recidivism" (Myers, 2003). Strong, automatic labelling that results in social rejection "is largely unsupported as an effective method of crime control". Adolescents forming identities and beginning to build stakes in conformity are especially affected by obstacles such as difficulty finding work, ineligibility for student loans and exclusion from conventional networks. Policy "should be remedial and foster reintegration following shame (Braithwaite, 1989)" — through academic mentoring, career advising or alternative activities — and structured to weaken deviant self-concepts and strengthen stakes in conformity. Earlier labelling-inspired policies included diversion from formal processing, decriminalization of minor offences and Schur's 'radical non-intervention' ("leave the kids alone wherever possible").

## Software lens

Informal labelling is more powerful than official labelling — in teams, how peers and leads talk about someone matters more than what HR records. Reflected appraisals (how you think others see you) drive engagement. The policy recommendations transfer cleanly to engineering management and platform governance: avoid automatic, permanent labels for first or minor violations (account bans, performance flags); keep an exit path; pair any sanction for juniors with mentoring and a route back; and remember that removing someone's stake in conformity (career prospects, reputation) removes what keeps them conforming.
