## Learning objectives

- State the victimized actor model's core claim and its two theoretical foundations
- Explain symbolic interactionism and the 'procedural self' (Mead)
- Explain phenomenology and ethnomethodology and why the latter questions criminal statistics
- Distinguish consensus from conflict views of society and summarize Weber, Simmel, Marx and Dahrendorf

## The ideas

**The model.** The predestined actor model was criticized for "its acceptance of the conventional morality and criminal laws as self-evident truths" (p. 186). The **victimized actor model** proposes "that the criminal is in some way the victim of an unjust and unequal society and it is the behaviour and activities of the poor and disadvantaged that are targeted and criminalized, while the actions of the rich and powerful are simply ignored or not even defined as criminal".

**Foundation 1: symbolic interactionism** (G.H. Mead) "primarily analyses the way individuals conceptualize themselves and others around them with whom they interact" (glossary, p. 509). Central is the **'procedural self'**: identity "is continuously constructed and reconstructed in interaction with 'significant others'". Meanings do not reside in objects but "emerge out of the social processes of interpretation" (p. 186). Hence "deviance is not a property inherent in certain forms of behaviour but one that is conferred on certain forms of behaviour by an audience" — shifting attention from the deviant to the reaction of police, courts, psychiatrists, social workers and teachers.

**Phenomenology** (Schutz): sociology should not try to establish the 'reality' of social phenomena; "phenomena are only 'real' if they are defined as such by individuals who then act on the basis of those definitions" (p. 187). Stigmatic responses affect how deviants see themselves; agencies create deviant meanings. **Ethnomethodology** studies "how individuals experience and make sense of social interaction"; all expressions of reality are **'indexical'** — tied to the context of use. Its major significance for criminology is "its profound questioning of the utility of criminal statistics", treated as "social constructions produced... by interpretative work and social organization".

**Foundation 2: conflict.** The orthodox view (Parsons) assumed **consensus** on goals and norms. A rival tradition sees society as conflict-ridden:

- **Weber**: conflict over scarce resources in the market, between class, party and status groups, and over ideas; resistance to the "iron cage" of bureaucratic rationalization; struggle for legislative power — "his central contribution to conflict criminology" (p. 187).
- **Simmel**: *homo homini lupus*; the self is fragmented across groups; stable social types — the stranger, the enemy, the criminal, the pauper; strangers and immigrants are scapegoats.
- **Marx**: human beings are *homo faber*, unfinished and creative; conflict stems from economic class relations; the state and criminal law reflect economic realities, used by the powerful to protect their interests; some treat criminals as proto-revolutionaries (the **primitive rebellion thesis**). The quotation from *Capital* describes expropriated peasants "turned into beggars, robbers, and vagabonds, partly from inclination, but in most cases from the stress of circumstances" (p. 190).
- **Dahrendorf**: pluralist conflict over the control of **authority**; conflict can be a positive motor of change; inequality arises from power and authority relations inherent in any society, not only capitalism.

US hostility to Marxism (immigrant screening, McCarthyism) made Dahrendorf's non-Marxist conflict theory attractive to Vold, Turk and Quinney. The 1960s brought anti-materialism, anti-authoritarianism, critiques of conditional welfare and new rights movements, which fed radical criminology (pp. 190–2).

## Software lens

Ethnomethodology's challenge to crime statistics is the challenge every SRE should apply to dashboards: an "incident count" is produced by people deciding what to file, how to classify it and whether to bother. Change the paging policy and the count changes without the underlying reliability changing. Symbolic interactionism explains why an engineer's identity on a team ("the one who broke prod") is built in interaction and can harden. Dahrendorf's point that conflict over *authority* exists in every organization, regardless of ownership model, is a useful corrective to the idea that a flat or open-source structure eliminates power.
