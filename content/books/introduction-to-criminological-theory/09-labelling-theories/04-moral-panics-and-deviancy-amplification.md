## The ideas

**Amplification.** Labelling also works at the group level. Wilkins's (1964) **deviancy amplification feedback** or spiral: "the less tolerance there is to an initial act of group deviance, the more acts will be defined as deviant" (glossary, p. 504). Reaction increases deviance, which increases reaction.

**Moral panic** is "a frenzy of popular societal indignation usually whipped up about a particular activity that is seen to threaten the very fabric of civilization; once labelled as such, those engaged in the activity become ostracized and targeted as 'folk devils'" (glossary, p. 506). Jock Young (1971) first used the term for recreational drug users in north London; **Stanley Cohen (1973)** developed it in *Folk Devils and Moral Panics* on the 1964 **mods and rockers** disturbances (p. 200). Media exaggeration produced public concern; the police increased surveillance; more arrests followed, which "appeared to confirm the validity of the original media reaction"; press emphasis on stylistic antagonisms encouraged polarization and young people adopted the identities offered. Traditional norms — employment, family — formed the context. These studies "marked a significant break" with positivist searches for causes.

**Hall et al. (1978)**, *Policing the Crisis*, used moral panic about 'mugging' in a Marxist framework: the panic about black street robbery served to legitimize a more authoritarian state during a crisis of hegemony; moral panic is limited "if employed without reference to" the wider political and economic context (p. 200).

**Goode and Ben-Yehuda (1994)** identify five characteristics (p. 201): (i) a **disproportionate** reaction; (ii) **concern** about the threat; (iii) **hostility** to the objects of the panic; (iv) widespread **consensus** that the threat is real; (v) **volatility** in scale and intensity. They "challenge the assumption of earlier theorists that moral panics are in some way engineered at the behest of dominant elites" (p. 206): panics may be grassroots, interest-group or elite-driven.

**Criticisms.** Left realists insist crime and fear of crime should be taken seriously, not dismissed as panic; Waddington (1986) argued muggings *were* increasing and asked what a proportionate response would have been. Watney (1987) questioned applying the concept to HIV/AIDS reactions. McRobbie and Thornton (1995) argue the idea needs rethinking where media institutionally need 'good stories' and sensationalism is used "'ironically'" for commercial purposes (p. 201).

## Software lens

The tech world produces moral panics regularly: "hackers", teenagers on new platforms, a newly named malware family, AI-generated content. Watch for the five features — disproportion, concern, hostility, consensus, volatility — and for amplification: a security vendor's alarming report prompts new monitoring, which finds more incidents, which confirms the report. That does not mean threats are unreal (Waddington's point): the analytical task is to ask what a *proportionate* response would be. Amplification also happens inside companies: after one insider incident, heavy-handed monitoring of a whole department can alienate staff and create the disaffection that produces more incidents.
