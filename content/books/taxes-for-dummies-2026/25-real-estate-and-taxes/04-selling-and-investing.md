## Learning objectives

- Report a home sale correctly
- Handle converting a home to a rental and divorce transfers
- Compute rental depreciation and understand the passive loss limits
- Use a 1031 exchange and know its key rules

## The ideas

**Reporting a sale** (p. 539): report on Form 8949/Schedule D if you have taxable gain **or** receive a Form 1099-S (showing the exclusion), even when the gain is fully excluded. The closing agent can skip the 1099-S for sales up to $250,000 ($500,000 married) when the seller certifies the gain is excludable.

**Don't hold a home just to avoid a loss** (pp. 539–540). If you convert it to a rental, depreciation uses the **lower** of adjusted basis or value at conversion, and the home sale exclusion is lost if you don't sell within three years of moving out.

**Divorce** (p. 541): transfers between spouses incident to divorce aren't taxable (section 1041), with carryover basis; each ex-spouse may exclude up to $250,000 on a sale if they qualify.

**Rental real estate** (pp. 542–543): deduct operating costs, interest, taxes and depreciation (27½ years residential, 39 nonresidential, building only): $200,000 building ÷ 27.5 ≈ $7,273 a year. Active participants can deduct up to $25,000 of losses with MAGI under $100,000, phased out by $150,000 (same for single and joint — a marriage penalty). Real estate professionals are exempt.

**1031 exchanges** (pp. 543–544): defer gain by exchanging business or investment **real property** for like-kind real property (since 2018, real property only). Use a qualified intermediary (never touch the cash), identify replacements within **45 days** and close within **180 days**; cash or debt relief received ("boot") is taxable; the deferred gain carries over in basis and can disappear with a step-up at death.

**REITs** offer real estate exposure without landlord duties; their dividends are mostly ordinary income (eligible for the 20% QBI deduction).
