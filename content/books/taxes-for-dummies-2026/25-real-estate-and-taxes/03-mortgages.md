## Learning objectives

- Weigh faster mortgage payoff against retirement contributions
- Evaluate down payments and refinancing on an after-tax basis
- Know when home equity interest is (not) deductible — a major correction

## The ideas

**15 vs 30 years and down payment** (pp. 535–538): paying down a mortgage is a risk-free return equal to its rate (after any tax benefit). Funding deductible retirement accounts usually comes first (immediate deduction; retirement assets also don't count for financial aid). Don't keep extra cash in savings earning less than the mortgage costs. "Take the biggest mortgage for the deduction" is silly — you spend a dollar of interest to save a fraction of it in tax (p. 537).

**Refinancing break-even** (p. 538): adjust payment savings for lost interest deductions — $100 a month at a 24% bracket is about $76 after tax, so $2,000 of costs takes about 26 months to recover (if you itemize; if you take the standard deduction, the full $100 is saved). Points on a refinance are amortised over the loan.

**Home equity and cash-out refinancing** (pp. 538–539): *Correction:* the book says borrowing against your home to start a business, buy other real estate or pay off credit cards gives "a tax deduction as a bonus". Since 2018 (made permanent by OBBBA), interest on home-secured debt is deductible as **mortgage interest only if the proceeds buy, build or substantially improve the home securing it**. Cash-out proceeds used to pay credit cards or buy a car produce nondeductible interest. (Interest traced to a business or rental may be deductible on Schedule C or E under the interest-tracing rules, and investment use may give investment interest.)

**New car loan interest** (p. 539): up to $10,000 a year for 2025–2028 on loans for new, U.S.-assembled personal vehicles, phased out above $100,000 ($200,000 joint) MAGI.
