## Learning objectives

- Determine whether a casualty loss is deductible and compute it
- Handle insurance, gains from involuntary conversions and the prior-year election
- Identify the remaining "other itemized deductions"

## The ideas

**Which losses?** For 2018–2025, personal casualty and theft losses are deductible only if attributable to a **federally declared disaster** (p. 236). OBBBA made this permanent and, from 2026, adds state-declared disasters. A casualty must be "sudden, unexpected and unusual" (fire, storm, flood, earthquake) — not moths, termites, dropped china or a dog's chewing (p. 237).

**Computation** (p. 237): for each item, the loss is the *lesser* of the decline in fair market value or your basis, minus insurance (including insurance you could have claimed but didn't). Then subtract $100 per casualty and 10% of AGI from the total. Example: AGI $100,000, uninsured loss $11,000 → $11,000 − $100 − $10,000 = **$900**. "Qualified disaster losses" designated by Congress use a $500 floor with no 10% reduction and can be added to the standard deduction. Real estate is treated as one item. Repair costs can measure the loss if they only restore the property (p. 238).

**Timing**: a federally declared disaster loss can be claimed in the prior year (amended return) for a faster refund (pp. 236, 239). Large losses can create a net operating loss.

**Gains** (pp. 236–237): if insurance exceeds basis, you have a gain, which can be deferred by buying replacement property costing at least the proceeds within two years (four years for a main home in a federally declared disaster area).

**Other itemized deductions** (line 16, pp. 239–240) — the ones never subject to the 2% floor: gambling losses up to winnings; federal estate tax on income in respect of a decedent; impairment-related work expenses; unrecovered annuity investment on a final return; claim-of-right repayments over $3,000. (The book also lists repayments of $3,000 or less; those were 2%-floor items and are no longer deductible.)
