## Learning objectives

- Explain why filing season is too late for most tax planning
- Weigh the benefits and risks of preparing your own return
- Use IRS publications for what they are good at
- Recognise the limits of tax software ("garbage in, garbage out")
- Know what you can do on irs.gov, how to pay cheaply, and what changed for the 2026 filing season

## The ideas

**Planning happens before December 31.** By the time you file, the book says, “it's too late to take advantage of many tax-reduction strategies for that tax year” (p. 19). A 401(k) deferral, a charitable gift, harvesting a capital loss or timing a bonus must happen within the year. A few things can still be done between January 1 and April 15 for the prior year — notably IRA and HSA contributions — but most cannot.

**Doing your own return** works best when your situation is stable: last year's return is your template. Benefits: lowest cost, and you learn the system, which improves future decisions. Caveat: laws change every year even if your life doesn't (p. 20).

**IRS publications** — Publication 17 (individuals) and Publication 334 (small business) — are thorough and free, but they state rules rather than strategies. Use them “to confirm facts that you already think you know or to check the little details” (p. 21). If you call the IRS, note the date, the employee's name and ID number, your question and the answer: IRS staff sometimes give wrong answers, and your notes protect you in an audit.

**Software** recalculates everything when one number changes and prompts for deductions. Drawbacks (pp. 22–23): garbage in, garbage out; thin explanations can create false confidence; and the computer “has a far lower IQ than you have”. Major products include TurboTax, H&R Block and TaxSlayer.

**irs.gov** offers forms for current and past years, an online account with transcripts, refund status, and payment. Paying from a bank account (Direct Pay or EFTPS) is free; card payments go through private processors that charge a fee (the book cites about $2.15 for a debit card and 1.75% for a credit card, p. 24). Beware of scams: pay only through official sites.

**Free File** is a partnership in which private companies offer free federal filing to taxpayers under an AGI limit, if you start from the IRS website (p. 25). Start from irs.gov, because going to a company's site directly may lead to paid versions. IRS **Direct File**, the government's own free filing tool piloted in 2024 and 2025, was not offered for the 2026 filing season, and the IRS said no future launch date had been set (this happened after the book went to print).
