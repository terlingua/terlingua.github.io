## Learning objectives

- Decide whether you need a preparer
- Distinguish unenrolled preparers, enrolled agents, CPAs and tax attorneys by training, rights and cost
- Find and vet a preparer

## The ideas

**Do you need one?** Probably not if your return is stable; consider it when your life changed (house, business, retirement). Even with a preparer you must organise your records first; the more you organise, the lower the fee. Don't wait until April: you'll choose badly and pay a rush premium (p. 26).

**Unenrolled preparers** have the least training and many work part-time (H&R Block, Jackson Hewitt, local shops). Basic returns can cost $200 and up. There is no national licensing (p. 26). (Background: anyone paid to prepare returns must have an IRS preparer tax identification number, PTIN; the IRS's attempt to require competency testing for all preparers was struck down in *Loving v. IRS* (D.C. Cir. 2014); the voluntary Annual Filing Season Program grants limited representation rights.)

**Enrolled agents (EAs)** are licensed by the IRS after an exam (or IRS experience), must do continuing education (72 hours every three years, an average of 24 a year), and have unlimited rights to represent taxpayers before the IRS. They work only in tax, and their fees usually fall between preparers and CPAs (p. 27).

**CPAs** are licensed by states after extensive education and an exam; continuing education requirements (the book says at least 40 hours a year) vary by state. Not all CPAs specialise in individual tax. They are most valuable for complex or unusual situations (partnership K-1s, a business, a first-time event); paying a CPA every year for a simple return is wasteful (p. 28).

**Tax attorneys** charge $300–$400 an hour and up (some over $1,000) and often don't prepare returns at all. They are for litigation, complex transactions and legal problems such as criminal exposure, where attorney-client privilege matters (p. 28).

**Who is best?** The credential matters less than the individual. The book calls it a myth that only a CPA can do your taxes (p. 29). Find candidates through friends, coworkers and professional associations, be wary of referral loops between advisers, and interview two or three (Chapter 28 of this companion lists the book's interview questions).
