## Learning objectives

- Apply the 3-year, 6-year and unlimited assessment periods
- Know the 10-year collection statute (with a correction on its start)
- Appeal an audit with or without a formal written protest
- Respond to a Statutory Notice of Deficiency in time

## The ideas

**Assessment statute** (p. 412): **3 years** from the later of the due date or the filing date (an early return counts as filed on the due date; an extension doesn't extend the deadline unless you file later). A 2023 return filed by April 15, 2024 can be assessed until April 15, 2027. **6 years** if you omitted more than 25% of gross income (or more than $5,000 of income from foreign financial assets). **Unlimited** for fraud or no return. You may be asked to sign an extension (Form 872); refusing typically triggers a deficiency notice.

**Collection statute** (p. 413): **10 years from the date of assessment** (the Collection Statute Expiration Date, CSED). *Correction:* the book says it starts on the later of filing or April 15; it actually starts from each assessment, so additional tax assessed after an audit has its own 10 years. It's suspended while an offer in compromise, CDP hearing, bankruptcy or an absence from the U.S. of six months or more is pending. The IRS can extend it by agreement only in connection with an installment agreement (or release of levy).

**Appeals** (p. 413): after an audit you get a **30-day letter** (Form 4549 and an explanation). You can agree, provide more information, or request Appeals. A formal written protest is needed if the amount exceeds $25,000; otherwise a small case request suffices. Appeals officers can weigh litigation hazards; most cases settle. States exchange data with the IRS; you generally must report federal changes to your state.

**Statutory Notice of Deficiency** (pp. 411, 414): the **90-day letter**, sent by certified mail. You have **90 days from the date of mailing** (150 if addressed outside the U.S.) to petition the U.S. Tax Court without paying first. The book says 90 days "of receipt"; the clock runs from mailing, and the notice states the last day. Disputes of $50,000 or less per year can use the informal small tax case procedure. If you don't petition, the IRS assesses and collects; you can then only pay and sue for a refund.
