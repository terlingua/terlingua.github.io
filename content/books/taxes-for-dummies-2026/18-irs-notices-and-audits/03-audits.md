## Learning objectives

- Distinguish correspondence, office, field and research (random) audits
- Know your rights: representation, suspension, burden of proof, Form 4822
- Prepare and conduct an audit effectively
- Use the repetitive audit policy

## The ideas

**Why you're audited** (p. 405): document mismatches, data errors, statistical outliers (DIF scores), related returns, or random selection. Roughly 85% of audits produce more tax.

**Types** (pp. 405–409):

- **Correspondence**: by mail, on specific items (deductions, credits, EITC, IRA payments). Send copies, never originals.
- **Office**: at an IRS office on listed items; postponements are usually allowed twice.
- **Field**: at your business; broader scope (bank deposits analysis, related years).
- **Research/random** (National Research Program): line-by-line checks to measure compliance.

**Rights** (p. 405): you needn't attend — an EA, CPA or attorney can represent you (unenrolled preparers have only limited rights, for returns they prepared, through the Annual Filing Season Program). You can suspend an interview to consult a representative. The burden of proof starts with you; it shifts to the IRS in court if you have credible evidence and met record-keeping requirements. A financial status (lifestyle) audit, including Form 4822, is allowed only if there's a reasonable indication of unreported income, and you aren't required to complete Form 4822.

**Conduct** (pp. 410–411): organise documents by issue; be courteous; answer only what's asked; state disagreements once and appeal rather than argue.

**Repetitive audits** (p. 409): the IRS policy is not to audit the same issue if it was examined in either of the two preceding years with no or a small change.
