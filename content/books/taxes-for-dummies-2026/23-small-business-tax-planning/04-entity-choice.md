## Learning objectives

- Compare liability protection with insurance
- Compare sole proprietorships, LLCs, S corporations and C corporations for tax
- Understand S corporation reasonable salary and eligibility

## The ideas

**Liability** (pp. 506–507): incorporating (or forming an LLC) separates business liabilities from personal assets, but lenders often require personal guarantees, you're always liable for your own professional negligence, and formalities must be respected. Liability insurance pays claims; incorporation doesn't.

**Tax treatment** (pp. 507–509):

- **Sole proprietorship / single-member LLC**: disregarded; Schedule C. (The book's note that some states require two members for an LLC is outdated; all states allow single-member LLCs, which can elect corporate or S status.)
- **Partnership / multi-member LLC**: Form 1065, K-1s; flexible allocations.
- **S corporation**: pass-through; up to 100 shareholders (family members can count as one), one class of stock, eligible shareholders only (U.S. citizens and residents, certain trusts and estates). Election on Form 2553, generally within 2 months and 15 days of the start of the tax year. Owners who work must take a **reasonable salary**; remaining profit is distributed (the book calls it a "dividend") without SE or FICA tax — the main tax attraction, offset by payroll costs and state taxes.
- **C corporation**: 21% flat corporate rate, then dividends taxed again (double taxation); losses stay inside the corporation; best for retaining earnings, raising outside capital, or qualified small business stock (section 1202, expanded by OBBBA).

The book's default advice: keep it simple — start as a sole proprietor and add an LLC or S election when the benefits clearly exceed the costs (p. 509).
