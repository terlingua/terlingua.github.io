## Learning objectives

- Keep an audit trail and separate business finances
- Apply the hobby loss presumption correctly (with a correction)
- Know employer filing duties and the new 1099 threshold timing
- Use cash-method timing within the constructive receipt rules

## The ideas

**Records** (pp. 494–495): Schedule C filers, especially cash businesses, are audited at higher rates. Deposit all receipts into one business account; pay expenses by card or check for a trail; never treat "paid from the business account" as proof of a business purpose.

**Hobby vs business** (pp. 495–496): *Correction:* the book says an activity "is considered a hobby if it shows a loss for at least three of the past five tax years". The actual rule is a **presumption in your favour**: if an activity shows a profit in **3 of 5** years (2 of 7 for horses), it's presumed to be for profit. Failing that doesn't make it a hobby automatically; the IRS weighs factors (businesslike manner, expertise, time, expectation of appreciation, history, financial status, personal pleasure). Since 2018 hobby expenses aren't deductible at all, while hobby income is taxable (Chapter 6).

**Payroll and information returns** (pp. 496–497): withhold and deposit income tax and FICA; Form 941 quarterly (or 944 annually if your annual liability is $1,000 or less — about $5,000 of wages); Form 940 for FUTA; W-2s and state filings. *Correction:* the book says the 1099-NEC/MISC threshold rises to $2,000 "starting in 2027" (pp. 497, 503); under OBBBA it applies to **payments made after December 31, 2025** (that is, 2026 payments reported in early 2027), as the book says on p. 338. Payroll services are worth it: unpaid trust fund taxes can make owners personally liable (the 100% trust fund recovery penalty).

**Cash-method timing** (pp. 497–498): recognize income when received and expenses when paid, so you can shift some between years. You can't hold a check received in December until January (constructive receipt). *Correction:* the book says C corporations generally can't use the cash method; C corporations with average annual gross receipts of $31 million or less (2025, indexed) can.
