## Learning objectives

- Classify workers and know the consequences
- Know which benefits are deductible for owners versus employees
- Hire children correctly (with a correction on the FICA age)
- Apply the business interest and NOL limits (with corrections)

## The ideas

**Contractor vs employee** (pp. 503–504): control over how, when and where work is done, investment, opportunity for profit or loss, relationship permanence and integration into the business point to employee status. Misclassification brings back payroll taxes and penalties. Form SS-8 asks the IRS to decide. Contractors pay both halves of SE tax but deduct expenses and can fund SEP or solo 401(k) plans.

**Benefits** (pp. 504, 508): a C corporation can deduct health, disability, group term life (up to $50,000 tax-free per employee), dependent care ($5,000; **$7,500 from 2026**) and cafeteria plans for owner-employees. Unincorporated owners and more-than-2% S corporation shareholders can't get most of these tax-free; they deduct self-employed health insurance (for months not eligible for an employer plan) and retirement contributions on Form 1040.

**Hiring family** (pp. 504–505): real work at reasonable pay shifts income to lower brackets and funds a child's Roth IRA. *Correction:* the book says children "under the age of 19" working for a parent pay no Social Security tax; the exemption is for a child **under 18** employed by a parent's sole proprietorship or a partnership owned only by the parents (FUTA exemption under 21). It doesn't apply if the business is a corporation (including an S corporation). Hiring a family member over the wage base may increase total family FICA (p. 505).

**Business interest** (p. 505): limited to 30% of adjusted taxable income for businesses above the small-business gross receipts test. *Corrections:* the threshold is $31 million average gross receipts for 2025 (indexed; $25 million was the 2018 figure the book uses), and OBBBA restored the EBITDA-based measure (adding back depreciation and amortization) for 2025 onward, whereas the book describes an EBIT measure. **NOLs**: indefinite carryforward, 80% of taxable income (Chapter 20).
