## Learning objectives

- Compute the tax-free part of a pension using the Simplified Method
- Handle a rollover and the 20% withholding trap
- Explain net unrealized appreciation for employer stock
- Apply the plan-specific early-distribution exceptions (age 55, QDRO)

## The ideas

**Pensions and annuities** (lines 5a/5b). Fully taxable unless you have after-tax cost (contributions that were already taxed). Then a fixed part of each payment is a tax-free return of cost (pp. 93–95).

**Simplified Method** (qualified plans, starting after November 18, 1996): tax-free amount per payment = your after-tax cost ÷ a number of payments based on age at the starting date (one life: 360 payments if 55 or under, 310 for 56–60, 260 for 61–65, 210 for 66–70, 160 over 70; two lives use combined age, from 410 down to 210). The book's example: $31,000 of cost, combined age 125 → 310 payments → $100 a month tax-free (p. 95). Once cost is recovered, payments are fully taxable; if death comes first, the unrecovered cost is deductible on the final return. The **General Rule** (actuarial tables, Publication 939) applies to nonqualified annuities.

**Rollovers** (p. 97). A distribution paid to you from an employer plan has 20% withheld. To roll over the full amount within 60 days, you must make up the withheld 20% from other funds; whatever isn't rolled over is taxable (and penalised if under 59½). A **direct rollover** (trustee to trustee) has no withholding and no 60-day risk. The IRS can waive the 60-day deadline for causes beyond your control. Hardship distributions can't be rolled over.

**Net unrealized appreciation (NUA)** (p. 97). If a lump-sum distribution includes employer stock and you move the shares to a taxable account, you pay ordinary tax only on the plan's cost basis; the appreciation is taxed as long-term capital gain when you sell.

**Early-distribution exceptions specific to employer plans** (p. 100): separation from service in or after the year you turn 55 (50 for public safety employees); QDRO payments to an ex-spouse; plus death, disability, medical expenses and SEPP. The first-home and education exceptions apply only to IRAs.

**Still working.** You can delay RMDs from your current employer's plan until you retire, unless you own 5% or more; this doesn't apply to IRAs (p. 100).

**Old relief for those born before 1936** — ten-year averaging and capital gain treatment on Form 4972 — still exists but affects almost no one now (p. 98).
