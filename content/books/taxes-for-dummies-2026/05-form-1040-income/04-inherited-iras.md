## Learning objectives

- Classify beneficiaries as eligible designated, designated or non-designated
- Apply the ten-year rule and when annual distributions are also required
- Explain a surviving spouse's options

## The ideas

**Beneficiary categories** (SECURE Act, deaths after 2019; pp. 89–92):

- **Eligible designated beneficiaries (EDBs)**: surviving spouse; the owner's minor child (under the 2024 final regulations, until age 21, then the ten-year rule); disabled or chronically ill individuals; anyone not more than ten years younger than the owner. EDBs may stretch distributions over life expectancy.
- **Designated beneficiaries**: other individuals named (adult children, grandchildren, friends). They must empty the account by December 31 of the tenth year after the year of death.
- **Non-designated beneficiaries**: estates, charities, most trusts. If the owner died before his required beginning date, the five-year rule applies; if after, distributions over the owner's remaining life expectancy.

**Annual distributions within the ten years.** If the owner died *on or after* his required beginning date (had started RMDs), a designated beneficiary must take annual RMDs in years 1–9 and empty the account in year 10. If the owner died before that date (and for all inherited Roth IRAs), there are no annual RMDs: only the year-10 deadline. The IRS waived penalties for missed annual amounts for 2021–2024; they are enforced from 2025. (The book describes annual RMDs as applying generally, p. 91; they apply only in the first case.)

**Surviving spouses** may treat the IRA as their own (roll it over), or keep it as an inherited IRA, which lets a spouse under 59½ take penalty-free withdrawals. From 2024 a spouse may also elect to be treated as the deceased spouse for RMD timing (p. 90).

**No 10% penalty** for beneficiaries of any age (p. 89).

**Losses inside IRAs.** The book says you can deduct a loss on a fully liquidated Roth or nondeductible IRA as a miscellaneous itemized deduction (p. 92). That deduction has not been available since 2018 (miscellaneous itemized deductions were suspended and OBBBA made the repeal permanent), so such losses are not deductible.
