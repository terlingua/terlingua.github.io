## Learning objectives

- Recognise the most expensive behavioural tax mistakes
- Use income shifting and deduction bunching when you control timing
- Evaluate financial advice for conflicts of interest

## The ideas

Taxes are often your largest or second-largest expense (p. 461), but they shouldn't dictate decisions on their own: what matters is the after-tax result toward your goals (p. 462).

**Mistakes** (pp. 462–466): getting advice *after* acting; underwithholding (the self-employed catch-up trap) or chronic overwithholding (an interest-free loan to the Treasury); skipping deductions for fear of audits (Judge Learned Hand: “Anyone may arrange his affairs so that his taxes shall be as low as possible”, p. 464); not using retirement accounts; ignoring taxes on investment sales and taxable distributions; political investing; and ignoring how savings outside retirement accounts reduce college financial aid.

**Timing** (pp. 466–467): if you expect a higher bracket next year, take income (a bonus) now; if lower, defer it (a cash-basis business can bill in late December so it's paid in January). **Bunch** itemizable expenses into alternate years so you itemize in one and take the standard deduction in the other; with today's large standard deduction this is a key technique (a donor-advised fund helps). Credit card charges count when charged, not when you pay the card (p. 467).

**Advice** (pp. 467–468): use tax advisors before major decisions and when situations change; beware commission-paid "planners" and asset-based fees that bias against paying off debt or investing elsewhere, and advice to sell appreciated holdings without weighing the tax.
