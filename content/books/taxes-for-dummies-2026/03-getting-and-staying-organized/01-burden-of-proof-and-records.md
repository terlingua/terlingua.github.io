## Learning objectives

- Explain why the burden of proof makes recordkeeping essential
- List the records needed for common deductions and situations
- Set up a simple recordkeeping system

## The ideas

**Burden of proof.** “The tax laws place the burden of proof primarily on the taxpayer” (p. 32). If you are audited and can't support a deduction, you pay the tax, interest and often penalties. Since 1998 (Code section 7491) the burden can shift to the IRS in court on a factual issue, but only if you present credible evidence, kept the required records and cooperated — so it is no excuse for poor records.

**What "complete and accurate" requires** (pp. 32–34):

- *Itemized deductions*: medical receipts (including travel to care); Form 1098 for mortgage interest; property-tax bills and proof of payment; state income tax payments; charitable receipts.
- *Charity*: for any single contribution of $250 or more you need a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the charity stating the amount and whether you got anything in return. For smaller cash gifts you need a bank record or receipt. (The book says “in excess of $250”; the statutory threshold is $250 or more.)
- *Dependents* other than a qualifying child: proof you provided more than half of total support — the cost, not the time, is what counts.
- *Car used for business*: either the standard mileage rate (70 cents a mile for 2025) or actual expenses; either way, a mileage log with business and total miles.
- *Home*: purchase price and costs, plus every improvement, kept until you sell (and beyond, until the sale year is closed); utility and repair records if you rent part or have a home office.
- *Business expenses*: the IRS watches closely; keep a receipt for every expense.

**Systems.** Software (Quicken, GoodBudget; QuickBooks, Xero or FreshBooks for a business) only tabulates what you enter, and you still need the underlying statements, because the IRS may verify computer records against them (p. 34). Low-tech works: an accordion file, a folder per year, or folders by schedule (A, B, D...) (p. 35). Keep receipts even in a year you take the standard deduction: next year you might itemize.
