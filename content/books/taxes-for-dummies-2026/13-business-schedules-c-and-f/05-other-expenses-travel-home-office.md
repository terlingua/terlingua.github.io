- id: repair-vs-improvement
  prompt: Which expense is a currently deductible repair rather than a capital improvement?
  explanation: A repair “keeps your equipment or property in good operating condition”; anything that betters, restores or adapts property, or prolongs its life, is an improvement to be depreciated (p. 287). ## Learning objectives

- Place common expenses on the right lines and distinguish repairs from improvements
- Apply the travel, tax-home and 50% meal rules; know entertainment is not deductible
- Compute the simplified home office deduction (corrected rate)
- Handle start-up costs, the at-risk rules, NOLs and the hobby presumption

## The ideas

**Other expense lines** (pp. 284–288): employee benefits (not your own health insurance, which is an adjustment); business insurance; mortgage and other business interest (including interest on a home equity loan used in the business); legal and professional fees (1099 rules apply); office expense; contributions to *employees'* retirement plans (your own go on Schedule 1); rent; repairs (deductible) versus improvements (capitalized); supplies; taxes and licenses (including employer payroll taxes, not your own SE tax).

**Leased cars** (pp. 286–287): lease payments are deductible by business use, reduced by an "inclusion amount" for expensive cars (IRS Publication 463 tables).

**Travel** (pp. 288–291): deductible when away from your *tax home* (the area of your main place of business) overnight or long enough to need rest. A temporary assignment expected to last over a year moves your tax home. Domestic trips: transportation fully deductible if the trip is primarily business; personal days' lodging and meals aren't. Foreign trips: allocate transportation unless the trip lasts a week or less or business takes over 75% of the time. Weekends between business days count as business days.

**Meals** are 50% deductible (80% for workers subject to Department of Transportation hours-of-service rules); per diem meal and incidental rates may replace receipts (for October 2024–September 2025: $86 high-cost / $74 other locations; the self-employed can use per diem for meals but not lodging). **Entertainment** (shows, golf, sporting events) has not been deductible at all since 2018 — the book's references to “meals and entertainment” at 50% (p. 290) apply only to meals.

**Home office** (Form 8829; p. 292): regular and exclusive use as your principal place of business (or to meet clients, or a separate structure). Actual method: business percentage of mortgage interest, taxes, insurance, utilities, repairs, plus depreciation; limited to business income, with carryforward. **Simplified method: $5 per square foot up to 300 square feet ($1,500 maximum)**, no depreciation, no carryforward. *Correction:* the book says $6 (p. 292).

**Start-up and organisational costs** (pp. 293–294): deduct up to $5,000 of each in the first year, reduced dollar for dollar once the category exceeds $50,000; amortize the rest over 180 months. Investigatory costs of a business you never start aren't deductible as start-up costs.

**At-risk rules** (p. 293): losses are limited to what you could actually lose (cash invested and debt you're personally liable for).

**Hobby presumption** (p. 294): a profit in three of five years (two of seven for horse activities) creates a presumption that the activity is a business; failing it isn't fatal, but continuing losses invite scrutiny.
