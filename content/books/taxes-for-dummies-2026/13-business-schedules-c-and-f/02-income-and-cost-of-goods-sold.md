## Learning objectives

- Report gross receipts completely, including income without a 1099
- Compute cost of goods sold from inventory
- Compare FIFO and LIFO
- Decide where business interest income goes

## The ideas

**Gross receipts** (line 1; pp. 265–266): all business income, whether or not reported on 1099-NEC, 1099-MISC or 1099-K. Itemising your 1099s (in software or a statement) helps match IRS records. Line 2: returns and allowances.

**Form 1099-K** (payment cards and third-party networks): OBBBA restored the third-party network threshold to more than $20,000 *and* more than 200 transactions, retroactively, so the planned $600 threshold never took effect. Card payments are reported regardless of amount. Personal reimbursements (splitting dinner) aren't income even if reported; business income is taxable even if not reported.

**Cost of goods sold** (Part III; p. 267): beginning inventory + purchases + production labor and supplies − ending inventory. You deduct what you *sold*, not what you bought. Ending inventory carries to next year's beginning inventory.

**FIFO vs LIFO** (p. 267): first-in, first-out assigns the oldest costs to sales; last-in, first-out assigns the newest. With rising costs, LIFO gives a higher cost of goods sold and lower taxable income. Inventory valuation (cost, or lower of cost or market) can't be changed without IRS consent.

**Other income** (line 6; p. 268): fuel tax credits, scrap sales, interest on receivables, fees for ad space on your building. Interest on a business bank account can go on Schedule B instead, where it isn't subject to self-employment tax.
