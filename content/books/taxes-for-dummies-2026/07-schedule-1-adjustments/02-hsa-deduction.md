## Learning objectives

- State HSA eligibility rules and the 2025 contribution limits
- Explain the HSA's triple tax advantage and how it differs from an FSA
- Know the deadline and the Medicare cutoff

## The ideas

**Eligibility** (p. 132). You must be covered by a high-deductible health plan (HDHP) — for 2025, a minimum deductible of $1,650 self-only / $3,300 family and an out-of-pocket maximum of $8,300 / $16,600 — and have no other disqualifying health coverage (dental, vision, accident, disability and long-term care coverage are allowed). You can't be someone else's dependent, and you can't contribute once enrolled in Medicare.

**Limits for 2025**: $4,300 self-only, $8,550 family, plus $1,000 catch-up at 55 or older (p. 132). For 2026: $4,400 and $8,750. Employer contributions (W-2 box 12, code W) count toward the limit. Contributions for 2025 can be made until April 15, 2026.

**Triple advantage**: deductible (or pre-tax through payroll, which also avoids FICA), tax-free growth, and tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical expenses. Unlike an FSA, there's no use-it-or-lose-it: the balance stays yours. Premiums generally aren't qualified expenses (exceptions: COBRA, coverage while on unemployment, long-term care, and Medicare premiums after 65).

**Reporting**: Form 8889; the deduction for your own (non-payroll) contributions goes on Schedule 1 line 13. An Archer MSA can be rolled into an HSA (p. 133).

**From 2026** (OBBBA): bronze and catastrophic marketplace plans count as HDHPs, direct primary care arrangements up to a monthly limit are allowed, and pre-deductible telehealth coverage is permanently allowed.
