## Learning objectives

- Apply the definition of alimony for pre-2019 instruments
- Identify payments that look like alimony but aren't
- Understand child-support contingencies and recapture

## The ideas

For divorce or separation instruments executed before 2019, alimony is deductible on Schedule 1 line 19a, with the recipient's SSN (missing SSN: $50 penalty and possible disallowance) and the instrument's date (p. 138). Post-2018 instruments: no deduction, no income.

**It's alimony only if** (p. 138): it's required by a divorce or separation instrument; paid in cash (checks count); the spouses don't file jointly; they aren't members of the same household (with an exception for payments within one month before departure, and for payments under a separation agreement before a decree); the obligation ends at the recipient's death; and it isn't designated as child support.

**Not alimony** (p. 139): property settlements (a house transferred), community income, payments to keep up the payer's own property, voluntary payments not required by the instrument.

**Special cases** (pp. 139–140): payments to third parties at the ex-spouse's written request (medical bills, rent) can qualify; premiums on a policy the ex owns; half of mortgage payments on a jointly owned home where the ex lives. **Child-support contingencies**: if a payment drops when a child reaches 21, the reduction amount is child support. Example: $2,000 a month reduced to $1,250 at 21 → $750 is child support, $1,250 alimony. **Recapture**: front-loaded payments that fall by more than $15,000 in the first three years trigger recapture of the excess into the payer's income.

**Property transfers between spouses** incident to divorce are generally tax-free with carryover basis (section 1041).
