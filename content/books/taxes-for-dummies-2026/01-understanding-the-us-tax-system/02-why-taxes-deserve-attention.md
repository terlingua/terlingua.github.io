## Learning objectives

- Explain why the tax system is a set of incentives you can use
- Describe what the procrastination and bad-advice case studies teach
- Explain why a refund is not a reward

## The ideas

**Taxes everywhere.** You pay tax when you earn (income and payroll tax), when you spend (sales and excise taxes) and when you save (tax on interest, dividends and gains). Individual income taxes plus payroll taxes are the federal government's main revenue sources; the book puts them at about 85% of federal revenue (p. 8). The modern income tax dates from 1913 and the 16th Amendment, with rates from 1% to 7%; Lincoln's Civil War income tax of 1861 came first (p. 8).

**Incentives.** “The tax system is built around incentives to encourage desirable behavior and activity” (p. 9). Home ownership, retirement saving, education and charity all get breaks. If you don't know they exist, you don't use them. The book's three quick examples: shelter earnings in retirement accounts such as 401(k) and SEP-IRA plans; spend less and pay less sales tax; and invest to suit your tax bracket, for example with tax-free bonds (p. 9).

**The cost of procrastination.** Sheila and Peter ran a profitable incorporated business, filed no extension and paid nothing by April 15 (p. 10). They paid a failure-to-file penalty (5% of the tax due per month, up to 25%), interest, and — biggest of all — a larger tax bill caused by not planning: they had set their own salaries so low that the deductible retirement contributions based on those salaries were tiny. They also lost decades of tax-deferred compounding on the money that could have gone into retirement accounts.

**The cost of poor advice.** George took advice from a commission-paid broker who sold him cash-value life insurance as a tax shelter and ignored his employer's tax-deductible retirement plan (p. 11). The result was “thousands of dollars more in taxes annually” plus commissions. The lesson: advice from someone paid by the product is not neutral.

**Refunds.** “All a refund indicates is that you overpaid your taxes during the previous year” (p. 11). A refund is an interest-free loan you gave the government. The return is a settling-up between what you paid during the year (withholding and estimated payments) and what you owed.
