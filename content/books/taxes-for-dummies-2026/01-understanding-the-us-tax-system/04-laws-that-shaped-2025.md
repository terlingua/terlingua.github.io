## Learning objectives

- Summarise what the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act changed and why its expiry mattered
- List the main One Big Beautiful Bill Act changes that affect individuals, and correct the book's simplifications
- Summarise the retirement changes in the SECURE Acts of 2019 and 2022

## The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (2017)

Effective for 2018. It cut most bracket rates (15% → 12%, 25% → 22%, 28% → 24%; top rate 39.6% → 37%), nearly doubled the standard deduction while eliminating personal exemptions (so more people stopped itemizing), doubled the child tax credit to $2,000, capped the state and local tax (SALT) deduction at $10,000, limited mortgage interest to debt of $750,000, and cut the corporate rate (pp. 16–17). Most individual provisions were scheduled to expire after 2025.

## The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (2025)

OBBBA made the TCJA brackets, standard deduction, zero personal exemption and $750,000 mortgage limit permanent, and added the following (pp. 17; IRS releases). The precise rules matter, and the book's summary is loose in places:

- **Tips and overtime.** New deductions for 2025–2028: up to $25,000 of qualified tips, and up to $12,500 ($25,000 joint) of qualified overtime premium pay. Both phase out above modified AGI of $150,000 ($300,000 joint), require a valid SSN, and are taken on Schedule 1-A whether or not you itemize. Payroll taxes still apply.
- **Seniors.** A separate deduction of $6,000 per person aged 65 or older, 2025–2028, reduced by 6% of modified AGI above $75,000 ($150,000 joint). The book says OBBBA “increases the standard deduction for lower- and moderate-income seniors by $6,000” (p. 17); strictly it is a *separate* deduction, available to itemizers too, on top of the existing extra standard deduction for age.
- **SALT.** Cap raised to $40,000 for 2025 ($20,000 married filing separately), rising 1% a year through 2029, then back to $10,000 in 2030. Above modified AGI of $500,000 (2025), the cap is reduced by 30% of the excess, but never below $10,000. The book says it “phases out”; it actually phases *down* to the $10,000 floor.
- **Child tax credit.** $2,200 per child for 2025, permanent and indexed for inflation (the book says 2025 through 2028; the IRS treats the $2,200 as permanent and it rises to $2,300 for 2027). The child needs an SSN, as before, and now the taxpayer (or, on a joint return, at least one spouse) must have one too.
- **Estate tax.** Basic exclusion of $15 million from 2026, indexed, with no scheduled sunset.
- **Itemized deductions for the 37% bracket.** From 2026, the tax value of itemized deductions is capped at 35 cents per dollar (often called the 2/37 rule). This is not the old Pease phase-out the book's wording suggests.
- **Car loan interest.** Up to $10,000 a year (2025–2028) of interest on a loan taken out after 2024 for a new, personal-use vehicle whose final assembly was in the United States; phases out above modified AGI of $100,000 ($200,000 joint).
- **Business.** 100% bonus depreciation for property acquired after January 19, 2025, made permanent; the 20% qualified business income deduction made permanent.

## The SECURE Acts (2019 and 2022)

Retirement changes (pp. 17–18): small-employer plan start-up credits; part-time workers' eligibility for 401(k) plans; penalty-free withdrawals of up to $5,000 for a birth or adoption; 529 money for student loans (up to $10,000 lifetime) and apprenticeships; employer matches on student loan payments; mandatory auto-enrollment for new 401(k) and 403(b) plans from 2025; higher catch-up limits; RMDs starting at 72, then 73 from 2023, then 75 for people born in 1960 or later; IRA contributions past age 70½; and the ten-year rule for most inherited accounts. The book says ages 60–63 may contribute “up to $10,000 more per year” (p. 18); the statute uses the greater of $10,000 or 150% of the regular catch-up, which made the 2025 figure **$11,250** (and $5,250 for SIMPLE plans).
