- id: double-counted
  prompt: Your Schedule C included $5,000 of interest that a client paid on a late invoice, but the payer issued a 1099-INT. The IRS added $5,000 to Schedule B without removing it from Schedule C. What do you write?
  explanation: “By adjusting Schedule B ... without adjusting my Schedule C, you are requiring me to pay tax on the same item of income twice” (p. 422). Matching notices compare forms to lines, so reporting income on a different line than the 1099 type invites them. Reporting it where the 1099 implies (and adjusting elsewhere) avoids notices.
  options:
  - text: Ignore it
  - text: Ask for a 1099 to be cancelled
  - text: Pay the tax and amend Schedule C
  - text: Explain that the income was already reported on Schedule C (enclosing it), that adding it to Schedule B counts it twice, and ask for the adjustment and any penalty and interest to be reversed

## Learning objectives

- Recognise the typical IRS errors and their causes
- Write a one-page response that gets results
- Prove timely filing and payment (with a caution on the mailbox rule)
- Handle erroneous refunds and the interest rule

## The ideas

**Typical IRS errors** (pp. 416–417): payments posted to the wrong year, type of tax or spouse's SSN; weekend/holiday due dates or valid extensions ignored; someone else's income under your SSN; tax-exempt income (IRA, municipal bonds) treated as taxable; income moved between schedules and counted twice; lost returns or checks; partial corrections (tax fixed, penalties not); wrong 1099s.

**Writing to the IRS** (pp. 417–418): keep it to a page; include your name, SSN, tax year, the notice and its control number, the error, the action you want, and **copies** (never originals) of proof; use the notice's return envelope or the IRS document upload tool or secure messaging where the notice offers it; give a phone number. Ask for **penalties and interest to be removed** along with the tax. If 30 days pass with no response, or you get a third notice, escalate to the Taxpayer Advocate.

**Proving timely filing** (pp. 420–423): e-file acknowledgments; for paper, **certified or registered mail** or a designated private delivery service receipt. Under section 7502, the postmark date is the filing date. *Caution:* the book cites cases presuming delivery from proof of ordinary mailing (p. 422); Treasury regulations since 2011 treat registered/certified mail (or an approved private delivery service) as the only proof of delivery apart from actual receipt, and courts such as the Ninth Circuit (Baldwin, 2019) have upheld that, so don't rely on testimony about ordinary mail. Duplicate returns: mark "DUPLICATE — Original filed [date]."

**Erroneous refunds** (p. 424): the IRS can't charge interest on an erroneous refund of $50,000 or less that you didn't cause, until it demands repayment (section 6404(e)(2)). Deposit but don't spend, or return it with a letter by certified mail.

**Late payment penalty** (p. 421): 0.5% a month (0.25% while an installment agreement is in effect for a timely-filed return), max 25%.
