## Learning objectives

- Track a refund and replace a lost or expired check (noting the shift to electronic refunds)
- Know when the IRS owes interest on a refund
- Allocate joint refunds and use injured spouse relief
- Meet the refund statute of limitations; use protective claims and the financial disability rule

## The ideas

**Tracking** (p. 428): Where's My Refund (irs.gov/refunds or the IRS2Go app), with your SSN, filing status and exact refund amount — about 24 hours after e-filing, four weeks after mailing. Refunds can be offset for back taxes, child support, federal debts (student loans) and some state taxes (p. 431).

**Paper checks are being phased out**: under Executive Order 14247, the Treasury stopped issuing paper refund checks for most individuals from September 30, 2025, so give direct deposit details (the book still discusses lost and uncashed checks: Form 3911; Treasury checks expire after 12 months, p. 429).

**Interest on refunds** (p. 429): if the IRS issues the refund more than 45 days after the later of the due date or the filing date, it pays interest (taxable income). Overpayments applied to next year's estimate earn no interest and can't later be switched to a refund.

**Joint refunds** (pp. 429–431): the refund belongs to the spouse whose payments and income produced it (Rev. Rul. 80-7 allocation; community property states differ). A spouse whose share of a joint refund is taken for the *other* spouse's past-due debts can file **Form 8379, Injured Spouse Allocation** (different from innocent spouse relief, Chapter 20). Joint estimated payments can be divided as the couple agrees if they file separately.

**Deceased taxpayers** (p. 430): Form 1310 is needed to claim a refund unless you're a surviving spouse filing jointly (or a court-appointed representative attaching the appointment).

**Refund statute of limitations** (p. 430): a claim must be filed within **3 years from when the return was filed** (an early return counts as filed on the due date) **or 2 years from when the tax was paid**, whichever is later, and the refund is limited to tax paid within the corresponding look-back period (3 years plus any extension; or 2 years). Withholding and estimated payments are treated as paid on the original due date. **Financial disability** (unable to manage financial affairs due to an impairment lasting 12 months or more, with no one authorised to act) suspends the period. **Protective claims** (p. 431): if your right to a refund depends on future events (litigation, a pending ruling), file a protective claim — usually a Form 1040-X identifying the contingency — before the deadline expires.
