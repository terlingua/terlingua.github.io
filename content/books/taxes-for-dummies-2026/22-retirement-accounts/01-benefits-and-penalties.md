## Learning objectives

- Quantify the upfront deduction and the value of tax-deferred compounding
- Know the 10% early withdrawal penalty and its main exceptions
- Avoid the age-55 and SEPP traps

## The ideas

**Benefits** (pp. 473–474): deductible contributions save your marginal rate now (25–30% federal plus state for many); growth compounds untaxed; employer matches are free money; low earners can add the saver's credit (2025 tiers: single up to $23,750 for 50%, $25,500 for 20%, $39,500 for 10%; joint $47,500 / $51,000 / $79,000; HOH $35,625 / $38,250 / $59,250). Deferral can win even if your retirement rate is higher, because more money compounds longer (p. 474).

**10% early withdrawal penalty** before 59½ (25% from a SIMPLE IRA in the first two years) (p. 472). Main exceptions: death; disability; unreimbursed medical costs over 7.5% of AGI; first-time home purchase ($10,000 lifetime, IRAs only); higher education (IRAs only); health insurance premiums while unemployed (IRAs); birth or adoption ($5,000 per child); and, from SECURE 2.0, one emergency personal expense distribution of up to $1,000 a year, domestic abuse victims, terminal illness and qualified disasters. Job loss alone is *not* an exception.

**Retiring early** (p. 473): two different routes. (1) **Separation from service in or after the year you turn 55** (50 for public safety employees) lets you withdraw penalty-free from *that employer's* 401(k)/403(b) — not from IRAs. (2) **Substantially equal periodic payments (SEPP/72(t))** from any account, computed by an IRS-approved method, continued for **five years or until 59½, whichever is later**; modifying them early triggers retroactive penalties. (The book merges these.)

**Loans** from employer plans (up to the lesser of $50,000 or half the vested balance) avoid tax if repaid; leaving the job can accelerate repayment (until the tax-return due date for the year of the offset).
