## Learning objectives

- Apply the head of household tests, including the cost-of-maintaining-a-home calculation
- Apply the "considered unmarried" rule for separated spouses
- Apply the qualifying surviving spouse rules and timeline

## The ideas

**Head of household** gives wider brackets and a larger standard deduction ($23,625 for 2025) than single. You must (p. 52):

1. Be unmarried or "considered unmarried" on December 31 (a married person living apart from the spouse for the last six months of the year, filing separately, who maintains a home for a dependent child more than half the year);
2. Pay more than half the cost of keeping up a home; and
3. Have a qualifying person: a qualifying child or dependent relative who lived with you more than half the year, or a dependent parent, who need not live with you — the parent's own home, an apartment or a nursing home counts.

A person who is your dependent only because of a multiple support agreement, or only because they lived with you all year as a non-relative, doesn't qualify you.

**Cost of keeping up a home** counts property taxes, mortgage interest, rent, utilities, repairs, property insurance, food eaten at home and other household expenses — but not clothing, education, medical care, vacations, life insurance or transportation, and not the mortgage principal (pp. 53–55).

**Living apart** means physically separate residences; emotional estrangement under one roof doesn't count (p. 52).

**Qualifying surviving spouse** (QSS; “qualifying widow(er)” in the book) lets you use the joint brackets and standard deduction for the **two years after** the year of your spouse's death if you have a dependent child (including stepchild or adopted child, but not a foster child) who lived with you all year, you paid more than half the cost of the home, you haven't remarried, and you could have filed jointly in the year of death (p. 55). Timeline for a death in 2023: 2023 joint; 2024 and 2025 QSS; 2026 onward HOH if eligible, otherwise single.
