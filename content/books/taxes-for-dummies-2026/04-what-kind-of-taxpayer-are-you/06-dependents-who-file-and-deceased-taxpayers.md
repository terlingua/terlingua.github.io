## Learning objectives

- Determine whether a dependent child must file
- Explain the dependent's limited standard deduction and the kiddie tax trigger
- Prepare for a decedent's final Form 1040 and the estate's Form 1041

## The ideas

**A dependent must file** for 2025 if (p. 60): unearned income exceeds $1,350; earned income exceeds $15,750; or gross income exceeds the larger of $1,350 or earned income (up to $15,300) plus $450. A dependent with withheld tax but no filing requirement should file to get it back — or claim exemption from withholding on Form W-4 if eligible (p. 61).

The numbers come from the **dependent's standard deduction**: the greater of $1,350 or earned income plus $450, but not more than the regular standard deduction.

**Kiddie tax.** Unearned income of a child under 19 (or a full-time student under 24 whose earned income doesn't cover half his support) above $2,700 (2025) is taxed at the parent's rate (p. 61). Chapter 17 explains it.

**Deceased taxpayers** (pp. 63–64). Death creates a new taxpayer, the estate, which files Form 1041 if its gross income is $600 or more. The decedent's final Form 1040 covers January 1 to the date of death, with a full standard deduction. A surviving spouse who hasn't remarried may file jointly. Income received after death goes on the estate's (or beneficiary's) return. Medical expenses paid by the estate within a year of death can be treated as paid by the decedent. Accrued savings-bond interest can be reported on the final return. Write "DECEASED", the name and date of death at the top; file Form 1310 to claim a refund unless you are the surviving spouse filing jointly (or a court-appointed representative attaching the appointment).
