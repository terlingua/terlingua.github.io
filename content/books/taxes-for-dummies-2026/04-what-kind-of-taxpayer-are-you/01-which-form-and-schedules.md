## Learning objectives

- Choose between Form 1040, 1040-SR and 1040-NR
- Explain what each numbered schedule adds to the return
- Decide between the standard deduction and itemizing

## The ideas

**One family of forms.** Forms 1040-A and 1040-EZ are gone. Since 2019 everyone uses Form 1040 (or the large-print 1040-SR if born before January 2, 1961 for 2025), with the same line numbers (p. 43). Form 1040-NR is for nonresident aliens with US-source income — the book's example is a Canadian owning a Florida rental (p. 45). US citizens and green-card holders living abroad still file Form 1040.

**Three schedules hold what doesn't fit on the 1040** (pp. 44–45):

- **Schedule 1**: Part I *additional income* (state refunds, alimony received under pre-2019 agreements, business income, rental and pass-through income, unemployment, gambling, cancelled debt and more); Part II *adjustments to income* (educator expenses, HSA, half of self-employment tax, self-employed health insurance and retirement plans, IRA deduction, student loan interest, alimony paid...).
- **Schedule 2**: *additional taxes* — AMT, excess premium tax credit repayment, self-employment tax, the 10% tax on early retirement distributions, household employment tax, Additional Medicare Tax, net investment income tax.
- **Schedule 3**: *credits and payments* — foreign tax credit, child and dependent care credit, education credits, saver's credit, energy credits; and payments such as extension payments and excess Social Security withheld. (The child tax credit is actually computed on Schedule 8812 and goes straight to Form 1040, not through Schedule 3, though the book lists it here.)
- New for 2025: **Schedule 1-A** carries the OBBBA deductions for tips, overtime, car loan interest and seniors to Form 1040 line 13b.

**Standard or itemized?** You take whichever is larger. For 2025 the standard deduction is $15,750 single and married filing separately, $31,500 married filing jointly (and qualifying surviving spouse) and $23,625 head of household (p. 46). Large mortgage interest, state and local taxes (now capped at $40,000), big medical bills or large charitable gifts can make itemizing worthwhile. Miscellaneous deductions such as investment advisory fees and unreimbursed employee expenses are gone; OBBBA made that permanent.
