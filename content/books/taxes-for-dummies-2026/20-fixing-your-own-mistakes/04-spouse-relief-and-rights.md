## Learning objectives

- Distinguish innocent spouse, separation of liability and equitable relief
- Know the deadlines and procedure for Form 8857
- Distinguish injured spouse relief
- Know the key taxpayer rights and the practitioner privilege

## The ideas

Joint filers are **jointly and severally liable** for the whole tax (p. 450). Form 8857 offers three kinds of relief (pp. 451–453):

- **Innocent spouse** (section 6015(b)): an understatement due to your spouse's erroneous items that you didn't know and had no reason to know about, where holding you liable is unfair.
- **Separation of liability** (6015(c)): if divorced, widowed, legally separated or not living in the same household for 12 months before filing; allocates the deficiency to the spouse whose items caused it. The IRS must show you had *actual knowledge* of an item to deny relief for it.
- **Equitable relief** (6015(f)): a catch-all that also covers **underpayments** (tax shown but not paid), considering abuse, economic hardship, knowledge and benefit.

**Timing**: (b) and (c) must be requested within **two years** of the IRS's first collection activity against you (a levy notice, for example); equitable relief can be requested any time while the IRS can still collect (or within the refund period for refunds). The book's "two years" rule applies only to the first two. The IRS notifies your ex-spouse; collection is suspended while pending; denials can be taken to Tax Court within 90 days.

**Injured spouse** (Form 8379, pp. 453–454): your share of a joint refund taken for your spouse's separate debts — not about tax errors.

**Taxpayer rights** (pp. 454–457): the 1988, 1996 and 1998 Taxpayer Bills of Rights (and the 2014 "ten rights" adopted into law in 2015, and the 2019 Taxpayer First Act) give rights to representation, to suspend interviews, to Collection Due Process, to recover litigation costs when the IRS's position wasn't substantially justified, court approval before seizing a home, and a **practitioner privilege** (section 7525) for tax advice from federally authorised practitioners in noncriminal matters, not covering return-preparation information.
