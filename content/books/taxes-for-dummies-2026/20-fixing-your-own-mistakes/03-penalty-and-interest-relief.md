## Learning objectives

- Use reasonable cause to abate penalties, and know its limits (Boyle)
- Know the new Automatic Exemption from Penalty replacing First Time Abate
- Know the limited grounds for abating interest

## The ideas

About half of assessed penalties are later abated (p. 441). Penalties aren't deductible and accrue interest.

**Reasonable cause** (pp. 441–446): you "exercised ordinary business care and prudence" but couldn't comply: death or serious illness of you or a close family member, casualty destroying records, being unavoidably absent, erroneous IRS advice (written advice: Form 843; oral advice if documented with names, dates and questions), inability to obtain records, reliance on a professional's substantive advice. Under *United States v. Boyle* (1985), relying on an agent to meet a **filing deadline** isn't reasonable cause; relying on substantive advice (e.g., whether a return is required) can be. The timing between the event and compliance matters. Request in writing (or by phone), and appeal denials.

**First Time Abate → Automatic Exemption from Penalty (AEP)**: historically, the IRS removed failure-to-file, failure-to-pay and failure-to-deposit penalties on request for taxpayers with three clean prior years (First Time Abate). In July 2026 (IR-2026-83) the IRS announced **AEP**, which applies that relief automatically during processing for eligible original returns starting with tax year 2025 (and 2026 quarterly returns), fully replacing First Time Abate for returns originally due on or after January 1, 2027. It doesn't cover estimated tax penalties, accuracy-related penalties or information return penalties; reasonable-cause requests remain available. (This development postdates the book.)

**Interest** generally can't be abated for reasonable cause (p. 447), but it can be when: the underlying tax is abated; on an erroneous refund up to $50,000 you didn't cause; the IRS unreasonably delayed a ministerial or managerial act (Form 843); the IRS failed to bill within 30 days after you agreed to a deficiency; the 36-month notice rule applies; or you pay within 21 days of a bill (pp. 447–450).
