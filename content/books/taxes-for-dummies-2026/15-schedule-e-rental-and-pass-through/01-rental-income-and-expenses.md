## Learning objectives

- Know what counts as rental income (advance rent, deposits, lease cancellation, tenant-paid expenses)
- Separate deductible repairs from capitalised improvements
- Allocate expenses in a partly owner-occupied building
- Depreciate the building but not the land
- Answer the Form 1099 questions at the top of Schedule E

## The ideas

Schedule E Part I reports **rents and royalties** from property you don't operate as a business (a self-employed author or musician uses Schedule C instead) (p. 337). The book mentions a Tax Court case holding that a first-time author's royalties aren't subject to self-employment tax until the book is revised or a second one is begun (p. 337) — treat that as a narrow, fact-specific position, not a general rule.

**Income** (p. 340): all rent received, whether or not you get a Form 1099-MISC. Advance rent is income when received. A refundable security deposit isn't income (until you keep it). A lease-cancellation payment is rent. Expenses a tenant pays for you in lieu of rent are income (and deductible). Renting to a relative below fair rent limits deductions to the rent received.

**Expenses** (pp. 340–344): advertising, travel to the property, cleaning and maintenance, commissions, insurance (a multi-year premium is deducted year by year: $900 for three years = $300 a year), legal fees (lease preparation deductible; purchase fees added to basis), management, mortgage interest (Form 1098), other interest (points on a rental loan are amortised over the loan term, unlike points on a main home), repairs, supplies, taxes, utilities, depreciation and depletion.

**Repairs vs improvements** (p. 342): a repair keeps property in ordinary operating condition (fixing a leak) and is deducted now; an improvement adds value or prolongs life (a new roof) and is depreciated: residential rental buildings over 27½ years, commercial over 39 years (31½ for property placed in service before May 13, 1993). *Correction:* the book says furniture and appliances in an apartment building are 7-year property (p. 343); IRS Publication 527 classes appliances, carpeting and furniture used in residential rental real estate as 5-year property. They and can use section 179 or bonus depreciation; the building can't.

**Land isn't depreciable** (p. 343): allocate the purchase price between land and building, using the assessor's split or comparable land sales.

**Mixed-use buildings** (p. 340): if you live in one unit of a two-family house, deduct 50% of shared expenses (taxes and interest on your half go on Schedule A), and 100% of expenses specific to the rental unit.

**Questions A and B** ask whether you made payments requiring Forms 1099 and whether you filed them (p. 338). The reporting threshold for services is $600 for 2025 payments and **$2,000** for payments from 2026 (OBBBA; indexed after 2026). *Correction:* the book cites a $50 per-return penalty; for information returns due in 2026 the penalty is $60 per return if filed within 30 days late, $130 through August 1, and $340 after that ($680 for intentional disregard), and a separate penalty applies for failing to furnish the payee copy.
