## Learning objectives

- Apply the under-15-days rule
- Classify a property using the greater-of-14-days-or-10% personal-use test
- Allocate expenses between rental and personal use (IRS and Bolton methods)
- Recognise that below-market rent to family counts as personal use

## The ideas

The day-count rules (sidebar, p. 348):

1. **Rented fewer than 15 days**: the rent is tax-free and not reported; no rental expenses are deductible (interest and taxes stay itemizable).
2. **Rented 15+ days and personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of days rented at fair rent**: it's a residence. Rental expenses are deductible only up to rental income, in a set order; the excess carries forward. No rental loss is allowed.
3. **Personal use within that limit**: it's a rental property. A loss is possible, subject to the passive loss rules. You still allocate expenses between rental and personal days.

**Allocation**: operating expenses × rental days ÷ total days used (rented + personal). Days merely available for rent don't count; days spent working full time on repairs aren't personal days. Under the *Bolton* case, mortgage interest and taxes may be allocated by rental days ÷ 365, which leaves more room for other expenses under the income limit and more interest and taxes on Schedule A (the IRS still uses its own method; some courts accept Bolton).

**Personal use** includes use by you, family members (unless they pay fair rent and it's their main home), and anyone paying less than fair rent. The book's Tax Court example: renting to a brother at a discount was personal use, so the loss was disallowed (p. 349).
