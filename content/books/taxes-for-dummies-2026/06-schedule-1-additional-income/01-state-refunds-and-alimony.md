## Learning objectives

- Apply the tax benefit rule to state and local income tax refunds, including the SALT cap
- Distinguish pre-2019 and post-2018 divorce agreements for alimony
- Explain why taxable alimony counts as compensation for IRA purposes

## The ideas

**Tax benefit rule.** A refund of state or local income tax is income only to the extent the earlier deduction reduced your federal tax (pp. 112–114). So:

- Took the standard deduction last year → refund not taxable.
- Itemized and deducted *sales* tax instead of income tax → not taxable.
- Itemized, but your state and local taxes exceeded the SALT cap even after the refund → not taxable (the refund just reduces an excess you couldn't deduct anyway).
- Otherwise taxable up to the lesser of the refund or the amount by which your itemized deductions exceeded the standard deduction you could have taken *that year*.

An overpayment applied to next year's estimated tax counts as a refund received. A refund for an older year follows that year's facts. *Correction:* the book's worksheet (Table 6-1, p. 114) fills in 2025 standard deduction amounts and outdated age add-ons; the comparison is with the standard deduction for the year the tax was deducted (for a 2024 refund: $14,600 single, $29,200 joint, plus 2024's extra amounts for age or blindness).

**Alimony.** For divorce or separation instruments executed before 2019 (and not modified to adopt the new rule), alimony is deductible by the payer and taxable to the recipient (line 2a, with the agreement date on 2b). For instruments executed after December 31, 2018, it is neither deductible nor taxable (pp. 114–115). Child support is never deductible or taxable.

**Alimony and IRAs.** Taxable alimony counts as compensation, so a recipient with no job can still contribute to an IRA (p. 115). (The book cites $6,500/$7,500 limits; for 2025 the IRA limit is $7,000, or $8,000 at 50+; for 2026, $7,500/$8,600.)
