## Learning objectives

- Know which behaviours and return features raise audit risk
- Understand why matching and accuracy matter most

## The ideas

**Accuracy** (pp. 573–574): check income documents against the return (including e-delivered forms), math, SSNs, signatures. Math errors bring correction notices rather than audits, but invite scrutiny.

**Report all income** (p. 574): third-party matching catches most omissions (W-2s, 1099s, 1099-K, 1099-DA). The 1099-NEC/MISC threshold is $600 for 2025 payments and $2,000 from 2026. Knowingly hiding income risks civil fraud penalties (75%) and prosecution.

**Itemizing, income level and returns types** (pp. 574–575): itemizers, very high incomes, EITC claims (fraud risk), Schedule C filers (especially with recurring losses) and estate tax returns are audited at higher rates. Take every deduction you're entitled to, but don't inflate; a near tie favours the standard deduction (unchallengeable).

**Other red flags** (pp. 575–577): frivolous protest arguments (a $5,000 frivolous return penalty applies); preparers who promise big refunds or charge by refund size (the IRS reviews all of a bad preparer's clients); hobby losses year after year; nonfiling; misclassifying employees. Random research audits happen regardless. The IRS pays whistleblowers, so cheaters can be reported.
